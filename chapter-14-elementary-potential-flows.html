Elementary two-dimensional potential flows, the Rankine half body and the doublet

A uniform stream moves at 2 m/s past a line source supplying \(2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) of fluid per unit depth. Can the fluid issuing from that source be replaced by a solid nose while leaving the exterior flow unchanged? The answer fixes a nose 0.500 m upstream of the source and a body approaching a full width of 3.142 m downstream. Those two dimensions cannot be chosen independently. The same building blocks raise a second question: can an equal source and sink, brought together, produce a finite disturbance with no net discharge? Their strength must increase as their separation shrinks. Bringing fixed-strength sources together instead produces zero flow away from the origin.

Grounded in Lecture 28, Some Simple 2D Potential Flows, and Lecture 29, Potential Flows: Rankine Half Body and Doublet, NPTEL Fluid Mechanics, Prof. Sanjay Mittal, IIT Kanpur. Lecture material: Cartesian and polar representations, Bernoulli pressure recovery, inclined uniform flow, sources and sinks, free vortices and circulation, linear superposition, the Rankine half body and stagnation streamline, a shifted source, the source–sink doublet limit, reversal of doublet direction, and the construction from opposite vortices. Research extension: Hess and Martin's surface-source formulation connects elementary superposition with a boundary-value calculation for prescribed bodies. Original teaching constructions: the running case, domain and branch conditions, detailed derivations and corrections, downstream half-body geometry and limits, finite-separation error analysis, three computed figures, executable checks, and six solved exercises. Recording labels follow the course manifest; the introductory cards use different lecture numbers.

The running case and the field model L28 1:00

The coordinates \(x,y\) are lengths in a horizontal plane, measured in metres; \(z\) is perpendicular to that plane. The velocity field is two-dimensional: \(V_z=0\), and \(V_x,V_y\) do not depend on \(z\). The velocity is \(\mathbf V=V_x\mathbf i+V_y\mathbf j\), in m/s, with Cartesian unit vectors \(\mathbf i,\mathbf j\). Density \(\rho\) is constant, pressure \(p\) is in pascals, and the flow is steady. Viscosity is omitted from the momentum model. Gravity may produce a separate hydrostatic pressure variation in \(z\); all pressure comparisons here are at one elevation.

The fluid domain \(\mathcal D\) is the part of the plane where velocity is finite and continuously differentiable. A source, sink, or ideal vortex is an excluded point, not an ordinary fluid parcel at which to evaluate a differential field equation. Later a body interior will also be excluded. Conservation of mass in this constant-density model requires zero divergence. Irrotationality requires zero vorticity, the curl of velocity. In two dimensions these conditions are

\[ \partial_xV_x+\partial_yV_y=0,\qquad \omega_z=\partial_xV_y-\partial_yV_x=0\quad\text{in }\mathcal D. \tag{14.1} \]

Here \(\partial_x\) denotes partial differentiation in \(x\), and \(\omega_z\), in s\(^{-1}\), is the out-of-plane vorticity component. Inviscid and irrotational are separate hypotheses. An inviscid flow can have nonzero vorticity; this chapter chooses the irrotational subclass.

The running case has uniform speed \(U=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) in positive \(x\) and signed source discharge per depth \(q=2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Positive \(q\) adds fluid and negative \(q\) removes it. A finite span \(W\) would carry volume rate \(Q=qW\), in m\(^{3}\)/s, if end effects were negligible. Thus \(q\) is a volume rate divided by a length, not a three-dimensional point-source strength. All numerical inputs are constructed, rather than measurements of a particular fluid or device.

Potential, stream function and polar coordinates L28 5:28

A velocity potential \(\phi\) is a scalar whose gradient equals velocity. A stream function \(\psi\) is a scalar whose derivatives enforce continuity. Both have units m\(^{2}\)/s. The sign convention throughout is

\[ V_x=\partial_x\phi=\partial_y\psi,\qquad V_y=\partial_y\phi=-\partial_x\psi. \tag{14.2} \]

Substituting the potential into continuity gives \(\nabla^2\phi=0\). Substituting the stream function into the curl gives \(\omega_z=-\nabla^2\psi\), so irrotationality gives \(\nabla^2\psi=0\). Here \(\nabla^2=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2\) is the planar Laplacian. Continuity alone allows a stream function locally; irrotationality alone allows a potential locally. Neither guarantees a single-valued scalar on a domain with holes.

A smooth single-valued potential gives zero circulation around every closed curve in its domain: integrating \(\nabla\phi\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf s\) gives the net potential change. Zero curl gives that conclusion for loops contractible to a point within the fluid domain, but not necessarily for loops around an excluded vortex. This distinction will determine which angle-dependent scalar requires a cut.

A streamline is tangent to velocity wherever speed is nonzero. Equation (14.2) gives \(\mathrm d\psi=-V_y\,\mathrm dx+V_x\,\mathrm dy\), which vanishes along a velocity-directed displacement. Thus \(\psi=\text{constant}\) traces streamlines. The same differential measures flow across an oriented curve, using the normal on its right, so differences of \(\psi\) give signed discharge per depth. Also \(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi=V_x(-V_y)+V_yV_x=0\): regular equipotentials and streamlines are orthogonal. At a stagnation point, where velocity vanishes, a unique tangent need not exist.

Polar coordinates are \(r=(x^2+y^2)^{1/2}\gt0\) and counterclockwise angle \(\theta\) from positive \(x\). The unit vectors are \(\mathbf e_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) and \(\mathbf e_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\). Inserting \(\mathrm d\mathbf x=\mathbf e_r\,\mathrm dr+\mathbf e_\theta r\,\mathrm d\theta\) into \(\mathrm d\phi=\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf x\), and into the expression for \(\mathrm d\psi\), gives

\[ V_r=\partial_r\phi=\frac1r\partial_\theta\psi,\qquad V_\theta=\frac1r\partial_\theta\phi=-\partial_r\psi. \tag{14.3} \]

The factors \(1/r\) convert angle into physical distance; omitting them would give the wrong units for \(V_\theta\). For any twice differentiable scalar \(F\), taking the divergence of its gradient yields

\[ \nabla^2F=\frac1r\partial_r(r\partial_rF) +\frac1{r^2}\partial_\theta^2F. \tag{14.4} \]

As a check, put \(F=x=r\cos\theta\). The two terms are \(\cos\theta/r\) and \(-\cos\theta/r\), which cancel. These formulas apply for \(r\gt0\); the origin requires a separate regularity or exclusion decision.

Recovering pressure after velocity L28 12:20

In the horizontal plane of this chapter, gravity has no planar component. In the following planar equations, \(\nabla\) differentiates in \(x,y\) at fixed elevation; the curl uses the same derivatives and has only an out-of-plane component. For steady inviscid constant-density flow, the planar Euler equation is \((\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V=-\nabla p/\rho\). The vector identity \((\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V=\nabla(|\mathbf V|^2/2)-\mathbf V\times\boldsymbol\omega\), with \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf V\), therefore gives \(\nabla[p/\rho+|\mathbf V|^2/2]=0\) when the flow is irrotational.

A scalar with zero planar gradient is constant on a connected fluid region at fixed elevation. Thus \(p/\rho+|\mathbf V|^2/2=B\), with \(B\) in m\(^{2}\)/s\(^{2}\). A reference region of speed \(U_\infty\) and pressure \(p_\infty\) at that elevation therefore gives

\[ \frac{p-p_\infty}{\rho}=\frac{U_\infty^2-|\mathbf V|^2}{2}. \tag{14.5} \]

This follows from the differential momentum equation and zero vorticity; it does not require a globally single-valued potential. A free vortex can have a single-valued pressure although its potential changes after one circuit. Disconnected fluid regions need not have the same \(B\).

For the running case \(U_\infty=U=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), stagnation pressure exceeds \(p_\infty\) by \(2\rho\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). Reporting pressure change divided by density avoids inventing a material property. A choice of fluid must supply both \(\rho\) and the pressure range over which constant-density single-phase flow is credible.

Uniform flow at an angle L28 14:41

Let \(U_\infty\) be a nonnegative uniform speed and \(\alpha\) its fixed inclination to positive \(x\). The board uses \(\theta\) for this inclination as well as for polar position. Here \(\alpha\) is fixed, whereas a point's \(\theta\) varies across the plane. The components are \(V_x=U_\infty\cos\alpha,V_y=U_\infty\sin\alpha\).

Integrating \(\phi_x=V_x\) gives \(\phi=U_\infty x\cos\alpha+f(y)\). The condition \(\phi_y=V_y\) requires \(f'(y)=U_\infty\sin\alpha\), hence \(f=U_\infty y\sin\alpha+C_\phi\). Similarly, integrating \(\psi_y=V_x\) and imposing \(-\psi_x=V_y\) gives

\[ \phi=U_\infty(x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha),\qquad \psi=U_\infty(y\cos\alpha-x\sin\alpha). \tag{14.6} \]

Additive constants \(C_\phi,C_\psi\) have been set to zero because derivatives remove them. They shift contour labels, not velocity. Constant \(\psi\) gives parallel straight lines; constant \(\phi\) gives their perpendicular family. Pressure at equal elevation is uniform because speed is uniform. Its value is set by reference data and need not equal atmospheric pressure.

For \(U_\infty=2\ \mathrm{m/s},\alpha=30^\circ\), velocity is \((\sqrt3,1)\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Differentiating (14.6) recovers both components. Setting \(\alpha=0\) gives the running case \(\phi=Ux,\psi=Uy\). There is no singularity, and the Laplacian vanishes on the whole plane.

A source, a sink and the meaning of strength L28 22:30

A line source at the origin emits fluid equally in all planar directions. Away from the line, symmetry gives \(V_\theta=0\), and continuity requires \(\partial_r(rV_r)=0\). Hence \(V_r=k/r\), with constant \(k\) in m\(^{2}\)/s. Its normalization follows by integrating the outward velocity around a circle:

\[ q=\int_0^{2\pi}V_r r\,\mathrm d\theta=2\pi k, \qquad k=\frac q{2\pi}. \tag{14.7} \]

Circumference grows as \(r\), so speed must fall as \(1/r\) to preserve discharge. The running source has \(k=1\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). At radii 0.5 and 1 m its speeds are 2 and 1 m/s, but either circle carries \(2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). For \(q\lt0\), the field points inward and represents a sink.

Equation (14.3) gives \(\phi_r=k/r,\phi_\theta=0\) and \(\psi_\theta=k,\psi_r=0\). Integration yields

\[ V_r=\frac k r,\quad V_\theta=0,\qquad \phi=k\ln(r/r_0),\quad\psi=k\theta. \tag{14.8} \]

The arbitrary reference length \(r_0\gt0\) makes the logarithm dimensionless; changing it adds a potential constant. The source potential is single-valued for \(r\gt0\). Its stream function requires an angle branch: after increasing \(\theta\) by \(2\pi\), \(\psi\) increases by \(q\). This jump records discharge across a cut, not a velocity discontinuity. Differentiation is local on either side. Using \(\arctan(y/x)\) without quadrant information assigns the wrong angle to half the plane.

Constant \(\psi\) gives radial rays, and constant \(\phi\) gives circles, as Figure 14.1 shows. The circulation \(\Gamma_C=\oint_C\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf s\), along a counterclockwise closed path \(C\), is zero even around the origin: on a circle, radial velocity is perpendicular to tangential displacement. More generally the single-valued potential makes every closed-path integral zero. Curl also vanishes for \(r\gt0\). These facts do not remove the source singularity.

Continuity holds in every annulus excluding the origin. A disk containing the origin has outward flux \(q\), requiring an imposed line supply. Applying the source-free divergence theorem across that point would violate its smoothness hypothesis. The singularity identifies a missing supply mechanism; the surrounding fluid conserves mass.

For an isolated source or sink, \(U_\infty=0\) and (14.5) gives \((p-p_\infty)/\rho=-k^2/(2r^2)\). Source and sink have the same pressure profile because their speeds have the same magnitude. At \(r=1\) and 0.5 m the running source gives \(-0.5\) and \(-2\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). The unbounded predicted suction as \(r\to0\) signals failure of the point representation as a physical injector. A real injector has finite size and a pressure supply. Phase behavior and compressibility must be checked before extending this ideal field toward its centre.

A free vortex and circulation around a hole L28 45:15

A free vortex has zero radial velocity and tangential velocity proportional to \(1/r\). Define circulation \(\Gamma\) as positive for counterclockwise motion. Integrating on a circle gives \(\Gamma=\int_0^{2\pi}V_\theta r\,\mathrm d\theta\). With \(\gamma=\Gamma/(2\pi)\), in m\(^{2}\)/s,

\[ V_r=0,\quad V_\theta=\frac\gamma r,\qquad \phi=\gamma\theta,\quad\psi=-\gamma\ln(r/r_0). \tag{14.9} \]

To derive the scalars, \(\phi_r=0\) makes \(\phi\) independent of \(r\), and \(\phi_\theta=rV_\theta=\gamma\) fixes its angular dependence. Conditions \(\psi_\theta=0,-\psi_r=\gamma/r\) give the logarithm and its minus sign. For \(\Gamma=2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), speed at 1 m is 1 m/s counterclockwise. Negative \(\Gamma\) reverses arrows without changing circles or pressure.

Continuity holds because \(V_r=0\) and \(V_\theta\) has no angular variation. The polar curl is \(\omega_z=[\partial_r(rV_\theta)-\partial_\theta V_r]/r=0\) at every \(r\gt0\). It remains exactly zero arbitrarily close to the excluded centre and is undefined at the centre. Nevertheless, an enclosing counterclockwise loop has circulation \(\Gamma\). Stokes's theorem cannot replace that integral by a zero curl integral over a disk containing a point outside the field's domain.

For any loop the circulation is \(\gamma\int_C\mathrm d\theta\). A non-enclosing loop has zero net angle change; a simple counterclockwise enclosing loop has angle change \(2\pi\). The potential changes by \(\Gamma\) around the latter. It can be single-valued on a cut domain, but not on the full punctured plane when \(\Gamma\ne0\). Velocity and stream function remain single-valued there.

Vortex streamlines are circles and equipotentials radial rays: the scalar fields exchange geometric roles relative to a source. Under the steady inviscid constant-density assumptions, (14.5) gives \((p-p_\infty)/\rho=-\gamma^2/(2r^2)\). For \(\gamma=k\) this is the source pressure profile. Equal pressure profiles do not imply equal velocities: one field carries radial discharge and the other circulation.

What linear superposition preserves L29 0:50

All elementary fields solve (14.1) on their nonsingular domains. If \(\phi_1,\phi_2\) are harmonic on a common domain, and \(c_1,c_2\) are constant coefficients, then \(\nabla^2(c_1\phi_1+c_2\phi_2)=c_1\nabla^2\phi_1+c_2\nabla^2\phi_2=0\). Differentiation gives \(\mathbf V=c_1\mathbf V_1+c_2\mathbf V_2\). Local stream functions add with the same coefficients. The common domain and consistent signs are essential conditions.

Pressure does not superpose: \(|\mathbf V_1+\mathbf V_2|^2=|\mathbf V_1|^2+|\mathbf V_2|^2+2\mathbf V_1\cdot\mathbf V_2\). The cross term must remain in Bernoulli pressure recovery. A proposed wall does not automatically survive either. A fixed impermeable wall with unit normal \(\mathbf n\) requires \(\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf n=0\). Adding a harmonic field with nonzero normal velocity violates that wall.

One construction task chooses strengths, computes their sum, and identifies a streamline to serve as a boundary. A different task prescribes the body and solves for strengths that make the summed normal velocity vanish. Hess and Martin (1974), NASA CR-134694, printed p. 4, describe the latter: a surface source distribution is determined by a normal-velocity condition, then discretized into linear equations at control points. Their report develops an axisymmetric method; it does not make this chapter's planar point source a representation of every body.

Uniform flow plus a source: the stagnation point L29 6:37

Add the running uniform stream to the positive source at the origin. With \(k=q/(2\pi)\gt0,U\gt0\),

\[ \phi=Ux+k\ln(r/r_0),\qquad\psi=Uy+k\theta, \tag{14.10} \] \[ V_x=U+\frac{kx}{x^2+y^2},\qquad V_y=\frac{ky}{x^2+y^2}. \tag{14.11} \]

Both derivative methods in (14.2) give the positive sign of \(V_y\). In particular \(\partial_x\theta=-y/r^2\), so \(-\psi_x=+ky/r^2\). Above the source the fluid moves outward and downstream. A negative sign here would destroy continuity: the altered source field would have divergence \(2k(y^2-x^2)/r^4\), nonzero at generic points.

A stagnation point \(A=(x_A,y_A)\) requires both components zero. Since \(k\gt0\) and the origin is excluded, \(V_y=0\) gives \(y_A=0\). Then \(V_x=U+k/x_A=0\), so

\[ x_A=-\ell,\quad y_A=0,\qquad \ell=\frac{k}{U}=\frac{q}{2\pi U}. \tag{14.12} \]

The length \(\ell\) measures the competition: a stronger source pushes \(A\) upstream, while a faster stream pushes it toward the source. The running case gives \(\ell=0.500\) m. Substitution gives \(V_x=2-2=0,V_y=0\). Solving only \(V_x=0\) would produce a locus containing points with nonzero transverse motion.

Upstream fluid moves toward \(A\), and source fluid between \(A\) and the origin moves toward it from the opposite direction. Adjacent fluid turns into upper and lower branches. At nonzero velocity, a smooth field gives a unique local streamline direction. At \(A\), velocity is zero, so level-set branches can meet without assigning two nonzero velocities to one particle. They are limiting streamlines approaching or leaving \(A\); a particle need not cross the stagnation point in finite time.

The dividing streamline and the Rankine half body L29 18:49

Choose \(0\lt\theta\lt2\pi\), with a cut along positive \(x\). At \(A\), \(\theta=\pi\), so \(\psi_A=k\pi=q/2\). The level \(\psi=q/2\) has upper and lower branches. On its upper branch, \(0\lt\theta\lt\pi\), substituting \(y=r\sin\theta\) into (14.10) gives

\[ y=\ell(\pi-\theta),\qquad r=\frac{\ell(\pi-\theta)}{\sin\theta},\qquad x=\ell(\pi-\theta)\cot\theta. \tag{14.13} \]

The lower branch is its reflection, with \(\pi\lt\theta\lt2\pi\), on the same level \(q/2\). Choosing the principal angle \((-\pi,\pi)\) instead gives labels \(+q/2\) above and \(-q/2\) below. Their difference is the branch jump \(q\); the physical boundary is unchanged. A contour algorithm must not connect points across a cut merely because their displayed values straddle a contour level.

The nose follows by a limit. Set \(\delta=\pi-\theta\to0^+\). Then \(\sin\theta=\sin\delta\sim\delta\), \(r\to\ell,y\to0,x\to-\ell\), agreeing with (14.12). One term further gives \(x=-\ell+\ell\delta^2/3+O(\ell\delta^4)\) and \(y=\ell\delta\); hence \(x+\ell\sim y^2/(3\ell)\). The sides join into a rounded nose with vertical tangent, not a corner.

Downstream, \(\theta\to0^+\) gives \(y\to\pi\ell\), while \(x\sim\pi\ell/\theta\to+\infty\). Thus the asymptotic full width \(b_\infty\) is

\[ b_\infty=2\pi\ell=\frac qU. \tag{14.14} \]

Mass balance independently gives the same result. The source-fed stream tube carries \(q\); its speed approaches \(U\) downstream, requiring width \(q/U\). The running body has \(b_\infty=\pi\) m. At \(x=0\), \(\theta=\pi/2\), so its upper surface is at \(y=\pi\ell/2=0.785398\) m. Its full width there is half the eventual width. The half body never closes downstream.

The boundary admits a direct check. Its tangent is \((x'(\theta),y'(\theta))\), with \(y'=-\ell\) and \(x'=-\ell\cot\theta-\ell(\pi-\theta)\csc^2\theta\). Differentiating the constant-\(\psi\) condition gives \(V_xy'-V_yx'=0\). Dividing by \((x'^2+y'^2)^{1/2}\) gives the normal velocity. The program evaluates it independently from (14.11) at 999 boundary points. The proposed stationary solid is therefore impermeable in the exterior field.

This replacement is the Rankine half body, plotted in Figure 14.2. Its source lies inside the removed region, not in the physical exterior fluid. The wall permits tangential slip; this is not the viscous no-slip condition. Other streamlines can define ideal walls locally, but a global replacement must have coherent geometry, exclude unwanted singularities, and retain the specified far field.

A rounded nose can approximate local leading-edge geometry, but this entire half body is not a closed airfoil. The construction fixes \(b_\infty/|x_A|=2\pi\), preventing independent choice of width and nose position. At fixed \(U\), \(q\to0\) collapses the body toward the downstream axis. At fixed \(q\), \(U\to0\) sends \(\ell\) to infinity: a pure source has no finite upstream stagnation point. These limits distinguish disappearance of a body from enlargement of it.

At the upper surface at \(x=0\), (14.11) gives \(\mathbf V=(2,4/\pi)\ \mathrm{m/s}\). For this steady inviscid constant-density irrotational exterior, at equal elevation, (14.5) gives \((p-p_\infty)/\rho=-8/\pi^2=-0.810569\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). At the nose it gives \(+2\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). Pressure rises as incoming fluid slows, then falls as it accelerates around the nose. Adding the isolated-source pressure to uniform pressure would miss the interaction term \(Ukx/r^2\), giving the wrong answer wherever that term is nonzero.

A source displaced from the origin L29 24:11

Combining sources at different positions requires distances and angles measured from each one. Put a source at \((a,0)\), with signed displacement \(a\) in metres. Define \(r_1=[(x-a)^2+y^2]^{1/2}\) and \(\theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x-a)\) on a chosen branch. Translation of (14.8) gives \(\phi=k\ln(r_1/r_0),\psi=k\theta_1\), velocity \(k(x-a,y)/r_1^2\), and excluded point \((a,0)\).

Origin-based polar coordinates give \(r_1^2=r^2-2ar\cos\theta+a^2\). Thus, for \(r\gt0\) away from the source,

\[ \phi=\frac q{2\pi}\left[\ln(r/r_0)+\frac12 \ln\left(1-\frac{2a}{r}\cos\theta+\frac{a^2}{r^2}\right)\right]. \tag{14.15} \]

Each term in the logarithm is dimensionless. At \(a=0\) the second logarithm vanishes. With \(a=0.2\) m and observation point \((1,0)\) m, \(r_1=0.8\) m and the quadratic in parentheses is 0.64. For \(k=1\ \mathrm{m^2/s},r_0=1\) m, both expressions give \(\phi=\ln0.8=-0.223144\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). The source point remains forbidden even if an origin-based expression obscures its location.

The source–sink limit at fixed moment L29 28:06

Place a source \(+q\) at \((-a,0)\) and sink \(-q\) at \((+a,0)\), with \(q\gt0,a\gt0\). Full separation is \(2a\). Let \(r_-=[(x+a)^2+y^2]^{1/2}\) be distance to the source and \(r_+=[(x-a)^2+y^2]^{1/2}\) distance to the sink. Superposition gives the exact potential

\[ \phi_a=\frac q{2\pi}\ln\frac{r_-}{r_+} =\frac q{4\pi}\left[ \ln\left(1+\frac{2a\cos\theta}{r}+\frac{a^2}{r^2}\right) -\ln\left(1-\frac{2a\cos\theta}{r}+\frac{a^2}{r^2}\right)\right]. \tag{14.16} \]

The common \(\ln r\) term cancels because net discharge is zero. For \(a/r\ll1\), the first logarithmic difference is \(4a\cos\theta/r\). The next nonzero term is cubic in \(a/r\): exchanging \(a\) with \(-a\) reverses the potential, eliminating even powers. Consequently

\[ \phi_a=\frac{qa}{\pi}\frac{\cos\theta}{r} +O\left(\frac{qa^3}{r^3}\right). \tag{14.17} \]

The source–sink moment is discharge magnitude times separation, \(2aq\), in m\(^{3}\)/s. Define normalized doublet strength \(m=qa/\pi=(2aq)/(2\pi)\), also in m\(^{3}\)/s. The controlled limit holds \(m\) fixed as \(a\to0,q\to\infty\). On a region a positive distance from the origin, (14.17) converges to

\[ \phi_d=m\frac{\cos\theta}{r}=\frac{mx}{x^2+y^2}. \tag{14.18} \]

This is a doublet with the stated orientation. Holding \(q\) fixed instead makes \(m\to0\), giving zero velocity at each fixed nonzero point. Neither limit is uniform within the shrinking region \(r=O(a)\). The finite pair excludes two singular points, and the limiting doublet still excludes the origin. Zero discharge does not remove a singularity.

The running doublet has \(m=1\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\). Half-separations \(a=0.4,0.2,0.1,0.05\) m require source magnitudes \(q=\pi/a=7.854,15.708,31.416,62.832\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Moment remains \(2\pi\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\) while each discharge grows. This is an ideal singular representation, not a physical device with infinitely strong injection and drainage.

Doublet velocity, exact streamlines and opposite vortices L29 35:37

Using (14.3), differentiation gives \(V_r=-m\cos\theta/r^2,V_\theta=-m\sin\theta/r^2\). Integrating \(\psi_\theta=rV_r=-m\cos\theta/r\) gives \(\psi=-m\sin\theta/r+f(r)\). The other condition, \(-\psi_r=V_\theta\), requires \(f'=0\). Choosing its constant zero,

\[ \psi_d=-\frac{m\sin\theta}{r}=-\frac{my}{x^2+y^2},\qquad V_x=\frac{m(y^2-x^2)}{r^4},\quad V_y=-\frac{2mxy}{r^4}. \tag{14.19} \]

Differentiating \(mx/r^2\) independently recovers the Cartesian components. Their magnitude is \(|m|/r^2\), so a pure nonzero doublet has no finite stagnation point in its domain. Closed-loop flux and circulation vanish. On a circle centered at the origin, the corresponding integrands are proportional to \(-\cos\theta\) and \(-\sin\theta\). For arbitrary enclosing loops, deformation to a circle within the nonsingular region preserves the integrals. A loop enclosing just one member of a finite pair instead carries flux \(+q\) or \(-q\); the doublet approximation does not resolve that member.

For a nonzero streamline label \(C\), in m\(^{2}\)/s, rearranging \(\psi_d=C\) gives

\[ x^2+\left(y+\frac m{2C}\right)^2=\left(\frac m{2C}\right)^2. \tag{14.20} \]

Every nonzero level is exactly a circle with centre \((0,-m/(2C))\) and radius \(|m/(2C)|\), with the origin removed. The zero level is the \(x\)-axis excluding its origin. Figure 14.3 plots these geometries. They do not become noncircular far from the origin; finite-separation source–sink flow or doublet-plus-uniform flow is a different field. Replacing \(m\) by \(-m\) keeps the contour families and reverses velocity directions.

For a quiescent far field under steady inviscid constant-density assumptions, Bernoulli gives \((p-p_\infty)/\rho=-m^2/(2r^4)\). At 1 and 0.5 m, the running doublet gives \(-0.5\) and \(-8\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). Its pressure singularity grows faster than that of a source or vortex. A body may hide it from the physical fluid; interpreting flow through its centre requires another model.

Opposite vortices supply another construction. Place circulation \(+\Gamma\) at \((0,+a)\) and \(-\Gamma\) at \((0,-a)\), with \(a\gt0\). Their potential on compatible local branches is \(\gamma[\operatorname{arg}(x+i(y-a))-\operatorname{arg}(x+i(y+a))]\), where \(i^2=-1\) and \(\operatorname{arg}\) is complex angle. Taylor expansion, using \(\partial_y\theta=x/r^2\), gives \(-2a\gamma x/r^2+O(\gamma a^3/r^3)\). Holding \(2a\gamma\) fixed gives a doublet with \(m=-2a\gamma\); reversing the vortices gives positive \(m\). A vortex pair separated along \(x\) instead gives a doublet rotated by a right angle. Both orientation and fixed moment matter. Coincident finite opposite vortices simply cancel.

Finite separation, cancellation and an error criterion

The limiting doublet is useful when pair separation is small compared with observation distance. For this calculation and its Python listing, \(z=x+iy\) is a local complex coordinate encoding in-plane position, in metres; the physical perpendicular coordinate introduced in the model is absent from these formulas. Let \(w=V_x-iV_y\), in m/s, encode the two planar velocity components. This local complex velocity is distinct from the sound-speed symbol \(w\) used elsewhere in the book. Direct differentiation of (14.16) gives

\[ w_a=\frac q{2\pi}\left(\frac1{z+a}-\frac1{z-a}\right) =-\frac m{z^2-a^2},\qquad w_d=-\frac m{z^2}. \tag{14.21} \]

The rational expression combines the pair before evaluation. For nonzero \(m,z\) away from the pair points, subtracting the exact expressions and dividing by \(|w_d|\) gives

\[ \frac{|w_a-w_d|}{|w_d|}=\frac{a^2}{|z^2-a^2|} \le\frac{a^2}{r^2-a^2}\quad(r\gt a). \tag{14.22} \]

The inequality uses \(|z^2-a^2|\ge |z|^2-a^2\). To guarantee relative velocity error at most \(\varepsilon\gt0\) at every point \(r\ge R\gt a\), it suffices that \(a/R\le[\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon)]^{1/2}\). For 1 % error, \(a/R\le0.09950\), with \(a\) the half-separation. The maximum full separation divided by \(R\) is twice that value.

At \((2,1)\) m, the running errors are 0.03261532, 0.00803842, 0.00200240 and 0.00050015. Consecutive ratios tend to four, as the \(a^2\) law predicts. This finite-separation error exists even with perfect arithmetic. Separately, subtracting two large nearly equal velocities in floating-point arithmetic can lose significant digits as \(a\) becomes extremely small at fixed \(m\). Equation (14.21) avoids that subtraction. Cancellation noise must not be interpreted as failure of the mathematical limit.

The boundary formulation has a related obligation. Hess and Martin, pp. 4–5, treat body geometry and source density as separate approximations. Refining one representation does not make the other exact. Here the analytic half-body tangent provides an independent normal-velocity test, rather than merely redrawing a contour of the field being checked.

Executable flux, boundary and convergence checks

The program implements the running source, uniform-flow combination and finite pair at fixed moment. Coordinates and half-separations are in metres, velocities in m/s. It uses the standard library, reads no files, and writes only standard output. The fixed test inputs have moderate magnitude; this is not a general solver for arbitrary bodies or extreme floating-point ranges. A singular source or pair point raises ValueError. A nonfinite squared radius or denominator is also rejected.

The closed-path quadrature uses an offset circle enclosing the singularity, an outward flux normal, and counterclockwise circulation tangent. It tests the Cartesian field rather than reusing the radial expression that defined source strength. The half-body test compares (14.11) with the independently differentiated tangent of (14.13). The pair error is measured against the limiting field and checked against (14.22). These are mathematical and numerical checks, not validation against measurements.

"""Chapter 14 numerical model; standard library only, SI units."""
from math import atan2, cos, hypot, isfinite, pi, sin

U, QDEPTH, M = 2.0, 2*pi, 1.0


def source(x, y):
    r2 = x*x + y*y
    if not isfinite(r2) or r2 == 0:
        raise ValueError("finite nonsingular coordinates required")
    return QDEPTH*x/(2*pi*r2), QDEPTH*y/(2*pi*r2)


def half_body(x, y):
    vx, vy = source(x, y)
    return U + vx, vy


def doublet(x, y, a=0.0):
    # Vx - i Vy: algebraic pair formula avoids subtracting large fields.
    den = complex(x, y)**2 - a*a
    if den == 0 or not (isfinite(den.real) and isfinite(den.imag)):
        raise ValueError("finite nonsingular coordinates required")
    w = -M/den
    return w.real, -w.imag


def loop(field, radius=1.3, n=4096):
    # Offset circle encloses origin; integrate outward flux and CCW work.
    flux, circ = 0.0, 0.0
    for j in range(n):
        th = (j + 0.5)*2*pi/n
        c, s = cos(th), sin(th)
        vx, vy = field(0.2 + radius*c, -0.1 + radius*s)
        ds = radius*2*pi/n
        flux += (vx*c + vy*s)*ds
        circ += (-vx*s + vy*c)*ds
    return flux, circ


ell = QDEPTH/(2*pi*U)
flux, circ = loop(half_body)
assert abs(flux - QDEPTH) < 1e-11 and abs(circ) < 1e-11
df, dc = loop(doublet)
assert abs(df) < 1e-11 and abs(dc) < 1e-11
print(f"source+uniform flux {flux:.6f} m^2/s; circulation {circ:.6f}")
print(f"doublet closed-loop flux/circulation < {1e-11:.0e} m^2/s")

residual = 0.0
for j in range(1, 1000):
    th = pi*j/1000
    y = ell*(pi - th)
    x = y*cos(th)/sin(th)
    dx = -ell*cos(th)/sin(th) - y/sin(th)**2
    dy = -ell
    vx, vy = half_body(x, y)
    vn = abs(vx*dy - vy*dx)/hypot(dx, dy)
    residual = max(residual, vn)
assert residual < 1e-11
assert hypot(*half_body(-ell, 0.0)) < 1e-12
print(f"nose x={-ell:.6f} m; asymptotic width={QDEPTH/U:.6f} m")
print("half-body maximum normal speed < 1e-11 m/s")

vx, vy = half_body(0.0, ell*pi/2)
print(f"at x=0: y={ell*pi/2:.6f} m; V=({vx:.6f}, {vy:.6f}) m/s")
print(f"pressure change / density={(U*U - vx*vx - vy*vy)/2:.6f} m^2/s^2")
print("a (m)  pair velocity relative error at (2,1) m  ratio")
previous = None
ideal = doublet(2.0, 1.0)
for a in (0.4, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05):
    pair = doublet(2.0, 1.0, a)
    error = hypot(pair[0] - ideal[0], pair[1] - ideal[1])/hypot(*ideal)
    exact_error = a*a/abs(complex(2.0, 1.0)**2 - a*a)
    assert abs(error - exact_error) < 1e-14
    ratio = "-" if previous is None else f"{previous/error:.4f}"
    print(f"{a:.2f}   {error:.8f}  {ratio}")
    previous = error

try:
    half_body(0.0, 0.0)
except ValueError:
    print("excluded source point: ValueError")
else:
    raise AssertionError("singular-point check failed")

# Roundoff experiment: finite-separation error is far below machine precision.
a, z = 1e-12, complex(2.0, 1.0)
direct = M/(2*a)*(1/(z+a) - 1/(z-a))
stable = -M/(z*z-a*a)
roundoff = abs(direct-stable)/abs(stable)
print(f"a=1e-12 m: subtraction relative error {roundoff:.3e}; "
      f"exact pair error {a*a/abs(z*z-a*a):.3e}")
assert roundoff > 1e-6

The last line separates approximation error from arithmetic error. At \(a=10^{-12}\) m, the exact pair differs from the ideal doublet by only \(2.000\times10^{-25}\) relative velocity. Direct subtraction instead differs from the stable pair evaluation by \(1.451\times10^{-4}\) in this run. That much larger error comes from floating-point evaluation, not finite separation.

Two deliberate errors distinguish the checks. Reversing the source's transverse velocity makes the first flux assertion fail. Keeping velocity unchanged but reversing the boundary tangent's \(y'\) makes the normal-velocity assertion fail. These failures compare different representations. Multiplying both the pair and limiting doublet by the same wrong constant, however, leaves their relative convergence error unchanged. The separate discharge normalization is needed to detect that shared mistake.

What the model captures and what it misses

The half body answers the opening question: its upstream nose is at \(-0.500\) m, full width at \(x=0\) is 1.571 m, and asymptotic width is 3.142 m. The exterior satisfies continuity, zero vorticity, the far-field velocity and impermeability. In the whole-plane construction the source supplies an interior stream tube; after replacement the source is absent from the physical exterior domain. No discharge is created within ordinary exterior fluid.

The doublet retains a finite disturbance with zero net discharge when \(qa/\pi=m\) stays fixed. Its velocity falls as \(1/r^2\). At the running observation point, \(a=0.1\) m gives 0.20024 % relative velocity error and \(a=0.05\) m gives 0.050015 %. These quantify replacement of a specified pair by a doublet, not agreement with an experiment or satisfaction of an arbitrary body's boundary.

The idealization omits viscous wall stress, the no-slip layer, and separation; it does not predict drag from those effects. A point source omits a finite injector and its supply pressure; a point vortex omits its rotational core. Constant density and a single fluid phase may fail where the ideal field predicts large speed or low pressure. Domain exclusions, boundary checks and a distance criterion must accompany every application. Superposition simplifies the field equations; physical boundary data and material assumptions determine whether their solution answers the intended question.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive and check a representation). Derive the two scalar fields for uniform speed 2 m/s at \(30^\circ\) from their derivatives. Find the discharge between streamlines differing by \(0.40\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) in \(\psi\), and their perpendicular separation. Does an additive label constant change either?

Solution

The components are \((\sqrt3,1)\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Integrating \(\phi_x=\sqrt3\) gives \(\phi=\sqrt3x+f(y)\), and \(\phi_y=1\) gives \(f=y+C_\phi\). Similarly \(\psi_y=\sqrt3\) and \(-\psi_x=1\) give \(\psi=\sqrt3y-x+C_\psi\). Label difference is signed discharge across a connecting curve with its specified right normal; its magnitude is \(0.40\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Since \(|\nabla\psi|=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), perpendicular separation is \(0.40/2=0.20\) m. Adding a constant leaves the difference and derivative fields unchanged. The absolute label itself is not a discharge.

Exercise 2 (counterexample and topology). A student concludes that a free vortex has zero circulation because its vorticity vanishes. For \(\Gamma=2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), compare counterclockwise circles of radius 1 m centered at the origin and at \((2,0)\) m. Identify the multivalued scalar and the missing hypothesis of Stokes's theorem.

Solution

Circulation is \(\gamma\int_C\mathrm d\theta\), with \(\gamma=1\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). The first path encloses the excluded origin once, giving angle change \(2\pi\) and circulation \(2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). The displaced circle lies at \(x\ge1\) m and does not enclose the origin, so its net angle change and circulation are zero. Its entire disk is within the smooth domain, consistent with Stokes's theorem. The first disk includes the singular point where curl is undefined, violating smoothness on the spanning surface. Potential \(\gamma\theta\) changes by \(\Gamma\) around that path; stream function \(-\gamma\ln(r/r_0)\) is single-valued. Changing the cut cannot change either physical circulation.

Exercise 3 (inverse design and a downstream tolerance). A half body must approach full width 2.00 m in a 3.00 m/s stream. Find its discharge, nose position and full width at \(x=0\). Where does width first reach 95 % of its asymptotic value? Could this construction also put its nose 0.50 m upstream?

Solution

Mass balance gives \(q=Ub_\infty=6.00\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\); hence \(\ell=1/\pi=0.318310\) m and \(x_A=-0.318310\) m. At \(x=0\), \(\theta=\pi/2\), giving full width \(\pi\ell=1.00\) m. At 95 % width, \(2\ell(\pi-\theta)=0.95(2\pi\ell)\), so \(\theta=0.05\pi\) and \(x=0.95\cot(0.05\pi)=5.99806\) m. This is the first crossing: \(y\) increases as \(\theta\) decreases, and \(dx/d\theta\lt0\) on the upper branch. Indeed the sign of its negative is that of \(\sin\theta\cos\theta+\pi-\theta\), positive for \(0\lt\theta\lt\pi\). A 0.50 m upstream nose would require width \(2\pi(0.50)=\pi\) m, inconsistent with 2.00 m. Another singularity distribution or geometry is required.

Exercise 4 (boundary diagnosis). Add circulation \(\Gamma=2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) at the source of the running half body. Show that the new field satisfies (14.1) away from the origin. Locate stagnation using both components and test whether the old upper boundary at \(x=0\) remains impermeable.

Solution

Both fields solve (14.1) on the common punctured domain, so their sum does. With \(k=\gamma=1\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(V_x=U+(kx-\gamma y)/r^2\), \(V_y=(ky+\gamma x)/r^2\). The second equation gives \(y=-x\); substitution into the first gives \(U+k/x=0\), hence \((x,y)=(-0.5,+0.5)\) m. This point is nonsingular and both components vanish. At the old boundary point \((0,\pi/4)\) m, the vortex adds \((-4/\pi,0)\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The old tangent is \((-\pi/4,-1/2)\) m per radian. Its old normal velocity was zero; the addition produces magnitude \((2/\pi)/[(\pi/4)^2+1/4]^{1/2}=0.683767\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The old wall therefore leaks. Superposition preserves the equations but fails this proposed boundary.

Exercise 5 (limit, error bound and numerical judgment). Hold \(m=1\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\) and require relative velocity error at most 1 % everywhere outside \(R=1\) m. Find a sufficient maximum full pair separation and corresponding minimum source discharge. Contrast fixed-\(q\) shrinking and floating-point cancellation.

Solution

The worst-case bound \(a^2/(R^2-a^2)\le0.01\) gives \(a\le\sqrt{0.01/1.01}=0.0995037\) m, full separation at most 0.199007 m. Keeping \(m\) fixed requires \(q=\pi m/a\ge31.5726\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). The bound is attained on the \(x\)-axis at \(r=R\), where \(z^2-a^2\) is positive real. Fixed finite \(q\) instead gives \(m\to0\) and zero limiting field at every fixed nonzero point. At fixed \(m\), separately evaluated source velocities grow as \(1/a\) but their difference stays finite; rounding of large nearly cancelling terms can contaminate the result. The combined rational expression avoids that subtraction. The genuine finite-separation error remains proportional to \(a^2\).

Exercise 6 (pressure limitation and model choice). Prescribe admissible pressure depression divided by density \(D_p=(p_\infty-p_{\min})/\rho=50\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), as an operating bound rather than a fluid-property correlation. For a quiescent far field, find the smallest admissible radii of an isolated source with \(k=1\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) and doublet with \(m=1\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\). What if a proposed body leaves smaller radii exposed?

Solution

Steady inviscid constant-density irrotational Bernoulli flow at equal elevation gives source depression \(k^2/(2r^2)\). Thus \(r\ge |k|/\sqrt{2D_p}=0.100\) m. For the doublet, depression is \(m^2/(2r^4)\), so \(r\ge[m^2/(2D_p)]^{1/4}=0.316228\) m. At 0.05 m the source predicts \(200\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), four times the bound; at 0.10 m the doublet predicts \(5000\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), one hundred times the bound. Either the exposed region must be excluded from the application or the model changed. Finite injector geometry, a different source distribution or body shape, and the actual fluid's phase and compressibility limits must be considered. Adding a potential constant changes no velocity or pressure. Clipping pressure while retaining velocity would violate the momentum relation used to recover it.
