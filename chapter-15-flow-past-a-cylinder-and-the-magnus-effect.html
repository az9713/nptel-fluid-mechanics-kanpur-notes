Flow past a cylinder: potential and real flow, rotation and the Magnus effect

A cylinder 0.20 m in diameter stands across a uniform flow of 10 m/s. If the fluid density is 1.2 kg/m³, an ideal flow without circulation predicts zero force per metre of cylinder. Prescribing a counterclockwise circulation of 6.283185 m²/s changes the vertical force to 75.40 N/m downward while leaving the drag zero. Can spinning a real cylinder produce that force? The calculation answers a pressure-force question exactly within an inviscid model. Predicting a rotor's actual force requires viscosity, its wake, and its ends. The distinction matters whenever this solution is used to estimate a structural load or design a rotating lifting device.

Grounded in the NPTEL Fluid Mechanics course of Prof. Sanjay Mittal, IIT Kanpur: Lecture 30A, Lecture 30B, and Lecture 31. Lecture material: uniform flow plus doublet, cylinder surface speed and pressure, d'Alembert's paradox, real boundary layers and separation, pressure drag and drag crisis, adding a free vortex, stagnation-point movement, lift and circulation, the ad hoc rotation correspondence, and two- versus three-dimensional viscous rotating-cylinder flows. Research extensions: Prandtl's boundary-layer argument (NACA TM452, pp. 4–5); Yang, Wang, Guo, and Zhang (2023), finite-cylinder computations and their parameter ranges (pp. A15-1 and A15-5). Original teaching constructions: the numerical case, explicit doublet normalization, exterior-domain and topology arguments, complete pressure-force integration, off-surface stagnation roots, computed figures, the synthetic base-pressure model, executable checks, and six worked exercises. The recording labels follow the course playlist; the title cards use 35, 36, and 37.

Geometry, data, and the ideal model

Place the cylinder axis along the Cartesian coordinate \(z\), with its centre at \(x=y=0\). The incoming velocity is \(U\mathbf i\), where \(U=10\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\mathbf i\) points along \(+x\). The radius is \(R=0.10\ \mathrm m\), so the diameter is \(D=2R=0.20\ \mathrm m\). Polar coordinates satisfy \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\): the distance \(r\) is nonnegative and the angle \(\theta\) increases counterclockwise from \(+x\). The outward radial unit vector is \(\mathbf e_r\); \(\mathbf e_\theta\) points toward increasing angle. Thus the front of the cylinder is at \(\theta=\pi\), its rear at \(0\), and its upper and lower shoulders at \(\pi/2\) and \(3\pi/2\). The shoulders are the points farthest from the streamwise axis.

The density \(\rho=1.2\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), incoming speed, and geometry are constructed inputs, not a specification of air at a measured thermodynamic state. The upstream pressure \(p_\infty\) is uniform and sufficiently high for the fluid to remain in its assumed phase. The ideal model is steady, two-dimensional, incompressible with constant density, inviscid, and irrotational throughout the exterior fluid \(r\gt R\). Two-dimensional means \(V_z=0\) and no field depends on \(z\); inviscid means viscous stress is omitted; irrotational means the vorticity \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf V\) vanishes. These last two conditions are separate assumptions. Body forces are neglected. The force is consequently a force per unit span along \(z\), in N/m, not the total force on a finite cylinder.

The boundary data are uniform flow at infinity and impermeability at \(r=R\): the radial fluid velocity is zero. No tangential velocity is prescribed in this inviscid problem. A real cylinder at angular speed \(\Omega\), positive counterclockwise in rad/s, instead imposes the empirical continuum no-slip condition \(V_\theta(R,\theta)=\Omega R\), as well as impermeability. The ideal solution will generally violate this condition. Its purpose is to calculate the exterior velocity and pressure under prescribed circulation, not to solve the viscous wall problem.

The fields are described by a velocity potential \(\phi\), defined locally by \(\mathbf V=\nabla\phi\), and a stream function \(\psi\). Both have units m²/s. In polar coordinates their definitions are

\[ V_r=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}=\frac1r\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta},\qquad V_\theta=\frac1r\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}=-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}. \tag{15.1} \]

The stream-function definition makes the divergence zero, because the two mixed derivatives cancel in \(r^{-1}\partial(rV_r)/\partial r+r^{-1}\partial V_\theta/\partial\theta\). It also gives \(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla\psi=0\), so curves of constant \(\psi\) are streamlines: velocity is tangent to them. The potential solves Laplace's equation \(\nabla^2\phi=0\) wherever the velocity is smooth. These relations and the boundary conditions are restated here so the construction is independent of other potential-flow chapters.

Uniform flow plus a doublet makes an impermeable circle L30A 2:04

Uniform flow has \(\phi=Ur\cos\theta\) and \(\psi=Ur\sin\theta\). A doublet at the origin adds \(m\cos\theta/r\) to the potential and \(-m\sin\theta/r\) to the stream function, where \(m\gt0\) has units m³/s. Here \(m\) is exactly the coefficient of \(\cos\theta/r\). It includes the factor \(1/(2\pi)\) that appears when the doublet is constructed from a two-dimensional source and sink.

To fix that normalization, let a source of volume flow per unit span \(q_s\gt0\), in m²/s, lie at \(x=-\ell/2\), and an equal sink at \(x=+\ell/2\). Their potential is \(q_s(\ln r_+-\ln r_-)/(2\pi)\), where \(r_+\) and \(r_-\) are distances to the source and sink respectively. At fixed \(r\gt0\), expanding in the separation \(\ell\) gives \(\ln r_+-\ln r_-=\ell\cos\theta/r+O((\ell/r)^3)\). Taking \(\ell\to0\) with \(q_s\ell/(2\pi)=m\) fixed gives the stated doublet. The source and sink strengths become unbounded, but their moment remains finite; neither singularity belongs to the eventual exterior fluid.

Superposition is valid because Laplace's equation is linear. Differentiating the sum, rather than adding speeds, gives

\[ \phi=(Ur+m/r)\cos\theta,\quad \psi=(Ur-m/r)\sin\theta, \qquad V_r=(U-m/r^2)\cos\theta,\quad V_\theta=-(U+m/r^2)\sin\theta. \tag{15.2} \]

Choose \(m=UR^2\). Then \(V_r(R,\theta)=0\) at every angle, and \(\psi=0\) on the circle. Replacing the region inside that circle by solid material leaves an exterior solution of exactly the required impermeability condition:

\[ \phi=U(r+R^2/r)\cos\theta,\qquad \psi=U(r-R^2/r)\sin\theta. \tag{15.3} \]

The same zero-stream-function set includes the streamwise axis outside the cylinder. Calling the circle a streamline does not mean it is the entire zero level set. The singular doublet remains a useful representation inside the excluded solid; there is no physical fluid source at the cylinder centre.

A stagnation point is a point at which both velocity components vanish. With \(m=UR^2\gt0\), the factor \(U+m/r^2\) cannot vanish, so \(V_\theta=0\) requires \(\sin\theta=0\). At \(\theta=0\) or \(\pi\), the radial condition then gives \(r=R\). There are two stagnation points, at \((R,0)\) and \((-R,0)\). The polar radius is \(R\gt0\) in both cases; the negative Cartesian location is supplied by the angle, not by a negative radius.

For the running case, \(m=0.10\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\). At \(r=2R,\theta=0\), the speed is \(U(1-1/4)=7.5\ \mathrm{m/s}\); at \(r=2R,\theta=\pi/2\), its magnitude is \(U(1+1/4)=12.5\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Both approach 10 m/s as the radius increases. On the wall, the tangential velocity is \(-2U\sin\theta\): it vanishes at front and rear but reaches 20 m/s in magnitude at each shoulder. Impermeability therefore works while no slip fails almost everywhere for a stationary wall.

Surface pressure and the favourable and adverse gradients L30A 24:19

To calculate the pressure, use Bernoulli's equation for the stated steady, constant-density, inviscid, irrotational exterior flow without body force. Its constant is global on this connected smooth fluid region: Euler's equation and zero vorticity give \(\nabla(p/\rho+|\mathbf V|^2/2)=0\). Thus the upstream state and any wall point obey

\[ p-p_\infty=\frac{\rho}{2}(U^2-|\mathbf V|^2),\qquad C_p\equiv\frac{p-p_\infty}{\rho U^2/2}=1-\frac{|\mathbf V|^2}{U^2}. \tag{15.4} \]

The pressure coefficient \(C_p\) is dimensionless; the reference \(\rho U^2/2\) is the dynamic pressure, 60 Pa in the running case. On the wall without circulation, \(V_r=0\) and \(V_\theta=-2U\sin\theta\), so

\[ C_p(\theta)=1-4\sin^2\theta. \tag{15.5} \]

Both stagnation points have \(C_p=1\), or 60 Pa above the upstream pressure. Both shoulders have \(C_p=-3\), or 180 Pa below it. Negative \(C_p\) is a pressure deficit relative to \(p_\infty\), not a negative absolute pressure. The zero-pressure-deficit angles satisfy \(\sin^2\theta=1/4\): \(30^\circ,150^\circ,210^\circ,330^\circ\). Figure 15.2 compares this symmetric distribution with a prescribed-circulation distribution.

Along the upper surface the fluid travels from \(\theta=\pi\) toward \(0\). If \(s=R(\pi-\theta)\) is distance measured downstream along this semicircle, differentiating (15.5) gives \(dp/ds=(4\rho U^2/R)\sin\theta\cos\theta\). The derivative is negative on the front half \(\pi/2\lt\theta\lt\pi\): the pressure falls in the direction of motion and the pressure gradient is favourable. On the rear half \(0\lt\theta\lt\pi/2\), the derivative is positive: the pressure rises downstream and the gradient is adverse. Equation (15.4) then says that the fluid decelerates. No gravitational height enters this exchange.

The angular origin needs attention when comparing diagrams. Measuring an upper-surface angle \(\vartheta=\pi-\theta\) from the front leaves \(\sin^2\vartheta=\sin^2\theta\), so (15.5) has the same form. This chapter keeps \(\theta\) measured from \(+x\) in velocities, forces, and plots. A change of angular orientation must also change the tangent and force projections; equality of the noncirculating pressure formula alone does not license mixing conventions.

Integrating the force and understanding zero drag L30A 43:41

The outward normal from the solid into the fluid is \(\mathbf n=\mathbf e_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). Pressure pushes inward on the cylinder, so a wall element of arc length \(R\,d\theta\) carries force per unit span \(-p\mathbf e_rR\,d\theta\). Denote its streamwise and vertical integrals by \(F_D'\) and \(F_L'\). Positive drag is along \(+x\), positive lift along \(+y\); the prime records division by span. A uniform pressure integrates to zero on this closed circle because both sine and cosine integrate to zero. It is therefore legitimate to replace \(p\) by \(p-p_\infty\):

\[ F_D'=-\frac{\rho U^2R}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}C_p\cos\theta\,d\theta,\qquad F_L'=-\frac{\rho U^2R}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}C_p\sin\theta\,d\theta. \tag{15.6} \]

Substituting (15.5) shows the cancellations explicitly. The drag integrand has primitive \(\sin\theta-(4/3)\sin^3\theta\), equal at both limits. In the lift integral, \(C_p(2\pi-\theta)=C_p(\theta)\), while the sine changes sign, so upper and lower contributions cancel. There is no viscous traction in this model. Hence \(F_D'=F_L'=0\), independent of \(R\), \(U\), and \(\rho\). The corresponding coefficients, defined by \(C_D=F_D'/(\rho U^2D/2)\) and \(C_L=F_L'/(\rho U^2D/2)\), both vanish.

The zero drag is the cylinder instance of d'Alembert's paradox: a reversible ideal exterior flow fails to predict the resistance of a real bluff body. A bluff body here means a shape whose separated wake can contribute substantially to its pressure drag. It does not mean that its flow is turbulent at every speed. The paradox is a mismatch between model and physical boundary conditions, not an algebraic contradiction. Increasing the Reynolds number does not make no slip become a permissible boundary condition for the globally inviscid field (15.3).

Why real cylinders form boundary layers and pressure drag L30B 0:47

In a viscous continuum the stationary wall imposes zero velocity. The transition from this wall speed to the outer-flow speed occurs across a boundary layer, a region with strong velocity gradients normal to the wall. Viscosity can matter there even when it has little effect far away. Prandtl's Motion of fluids with very little viscosity states adherence to the solid and explains why a thin transition with large velocity differences can produce appreciable effects despite small viscosity (NACA TM452, p. 4, PDF p. 6). On p. 5 (PDF p. 7), he treats the pressure as imposed across this thin layer by the outer flow.

A local balance explains the apparent contradiction. Let \(\nu=\mu/\rho\) be the kinematic viscosity, with \(\mu\) the dynamic viscosity, and define \(\mathrm{Re}_D=UD/\nu\). Away from a wall, inertia scales as \(U^2/D\) and viscous acceleration as \(\nu U/D^2\), so their ratio is \(\mathrm{Re}_D\). In an attached thin layer of thickness \(\delta\), viscous acceleration instead scales as \(\nu U/\delta^2\). Balancing it against \(U^2/D\) gives \(\delta/D\sim\mathrm{Re}_D^{-1/2}\). This is an ordering estimate for an attached laminar layer, not a separation-location formula or a measured thickness coefficient. At a constructed \(\mathrm{Re}_D=10^4\), it gives an order of 0.002 m for the running diameter, and the local viscous term is still of the same order as inertia.

The adverse pressure gradient on the rear of the cylinder asks slowed near-wall fluid to move into higher pressure. A sufficiently strong adverse gradient can produce reversal near the wall and detachment of the flow, called separation. The separated wake then changes the outer pressure field itself; continuing to apply (15.5) around the rear is no longer justified. A lower rear or base pressure than the front pressure gives a net force in \(+x\). Viscosity consequently produces both direct shear drag and, through the changed flow, pressure drag. It is incorrect to identify all viscous consequences with the direct shear force.

A recirculating region contains fluid that returns upstream over part of its motion. The symmetric pair of recirculating vortices in a mean-flow sketch is not proof that instantaneous flow is steady. A time average at a point is the average of its velocity over a stated time interval; streamlines of this averaged field generally differ from particle paths in the unsteady flow. Alternating vortices carried downstream form a vortex street. Neither a universal onset Reynolds number nor a universal pressure minimum follows from the qualitative separated-flow sketch.

A simple constructed pressure model quantifies why base pressure matters without pretending to predict a wake. Retain (15.5) on the front semicircle \(\pi/2\le\theta\le3\pi/2\), and prescribe one constant \(C_{p,b}\) on the rear semicircle. This discontinuous model is only a force-integration exercise. The front integral \(\int_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}(1-4\sin^2\theta)\cos\theta\,d\theta=2/3\); the rear cosine integral is 2. With the normalization above,

\[ C_D=-\tfrac12(2/3+2C_{p,b})=-\tfrac13-C_{p,b}. \tag{15.7} \]

Choosing \(C_{p,b}=-1.2\) gives \(C_D=0.8667\) and \(F_D'=10.4\ \mathrm{N/m}\) for the running case. Raising that prescribed base value to \(-0.8\), with the entire front distribution held fixed, reduces the model force to 5.6 N/m. This demonstrates sensitivity to a pressure deficit, not a measured cylinder drag law. Base pressure alone does not determine drag unless the remaining pressure distribution and shear force are also known or controlled. The negative drag obtained by extending this artificial model to \(C_{p,b}\gt-1/3\) signals an unsuitable prescribed distribution for a passive stationary-cylinder design estimate.

Transition can reduce pressure drag while increasing shear drag L30B 17:11

A laminar boundary layer has relatively ordered motion; a turbulent layer has irregular fluctuations and transports momentum across the layer. These fluctuations can bring higher streamwise momentum toward the wall, helping the layer remain attached farther into an adverse pressure gradient. Around a bluff cylinder, delayed separation can narrow the wake, improve rear pressure recovery, and reduce pressure drag. The same mixing can increase wall shear. The net change depends on the balance of those two forces, not on a rule that turbulence always increases or always decreases total resistance.

The drag crisis is the sharp fall in cylinder drag coefficient associated with changes of boundary-layer transition and separation in a particular range of Reynolds number. It concerns the boundary layer and separation pattern, not merely whether vortices exist in the wake. The qualitative drag-crisis curve in the source recording has no calibrated transition value. No universal numerical threshold, separation angle, or positive base-pressure coefficient is assigned here. Surface roughness, incoming disturbances, confinement, and end conditions must accompany a quantitative comparison.

A streamlined shape can preserve attachment over much of its surface in an appropriate operating range, making direct skin friction relatively more important than for a separated bluff body. Streamlining does not guarantee attachment: changing the operating condition can create an adverse gradient strong enough to separate even an airfoil. For the running cylinder, neither the ideal zero drag nor the two synthetic values after (15.7) is an acceptable measured load estimate. A pressure survey integrated with (15.6), plus the shear contribution, would provide the needed force evidence.

Adding circulation without changing impermeability L31 5:19

Rotation motivates a new ideal model, but rotation cannot drive an inviscid fluid through shear: an axisymmetric circular wall has no normal velocity even when it spins. The inviscid boundary data therefore do not distinguish \(\Omega=0\) from a spinning cylinder. Instead prescribe circulation \(\Gamma=\oint_C\mathbf V\cdot d\mathbf s\), where \(C\) is a counterclockwise closed curve surrounding the cylinder once, and \(d\mathbf s\) points along the curve. Positive \(\Gamma\), in m²/s, is counterclockwise. A free-vortex field has \(V_\theta=\Gamma/(2\pi r)\), \(V_r=0\); its circulation around a circle is exactly \(2\pi rV_\theta=\Gamma\).

Its potential \(\Gamma\theta/(2\pi)\) is local rather than single-valued around the entire cylinder: increasing the angle by \(2\pi\) increases the potential by \(\Gamma\), while leaving its gradient unchanged. Adding it to (15.3), and choosing the stream-function constant so the wall remains \(\psi=0\), gives

\[ \phi=U(r+R^2/r)\cos\theta+\frac{\Gamma\theta}{2\pi},\qquad \psi=U(r-R^2/r)\sin\theta-\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln(r/R). \tag{15.8} \] \[ V_r=U(1-R^2/r^2)\cos\theta,\qquad V_\theta=-U(1+R^2/r^2)\sin\theta+\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}. \tag{15.9} \]

The logarithm has a dimensionless argument. At the wall it is zero, as is the uniform-plus-doublet stream function; the radial velocity also remains zero. At infinity the vortex velocity decays as \(1/r\), while the doublet velocity decays as \(1/r^2\). Thus the far-field velocity is still \(U\mathbf i\). The growth of the vortex stream function as \(-\ln r\) does not contradict this velocity limit: velocity depends on derivatives, not on the value of \(\psi\).

In the smooth exterior the free-vortex vorticity is \(r^{-1}\partial(rV_\theta)/\partial r=0\). Yet its circulation is nonzero. Stokes's theorem gives zero circulation for a loop that bounds a surface entirely in the smooth irrotational fluid. A loop enclosing the solid cannot be filled by such a surface in this two-dimensional exterior domain. Its nonzero circulation is therefore compatible with zero local vorticity. A mathematical vortex singularity placed at the excluded origin is a representation of that circulation; the model does not contain a finite fluid vortex core there.

For the running case choose \(\Gamma=2\pi UR=6.283185\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). The added tangential speed at the wall is 10 m/s. At the upper shoulder it opposes the \(-20\ \mathrm{m/s}\) base velocity, leaving \(-10\ \mathrm{m/s}\); at the lower shoulder it adds to \(+20\ \mathrm{m/s}\), giving \(+30\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The unequal speeds will produce unequal pressures even though impermeability is identical.

Stagnation points merge and then move off the surface L31 13:46

Define the dimensionless circulation \(\beta=\Gamma/(4\pi UR)\). It is a circulation parameter, not yet a rotation ratio. On the wall, (15.9) becomes \(V_\theta=2U(\beta-\sin\theta)\), so the two surface stagnation points satisfy

\[ \sin\theta_s=\beta,\qquad |\beta|\le1. \tag{15.10} \]

For \(0\le\beta\lt1\), the angles are \(\theta_s=\sin^{-1}\beta\) and \(\pi-\sin^{-1}\beta\). Both move upward as positive circulation increases, while remaining symmetric about the \(y\)-axis. At \(\beta=0\), the original front and rear points return. At \(\beta=1\), they meet at the upper shoulder. Negative circulation reflects this picture across \(y=0\); angles must then be interpreted modulo \(2\pi\).

For \(|\beta|\gt1\), the surface condition has no real solution. The other way to make \(V_r=0\) in the exterior is \(\cos\theta=0\). For positive circulation, only \(\theta=\pi/2\) can cancel the positive vortex velocity against the base velocity. With \(s_r=r/R\), the remaining equation is \(s_r^2-2\beta s_r+1=0\). Its roots are reciprocal:

\[ \frac{r_s}{R}=|\beta|+\sqrt{\beta^2-1},\qquad \frac{r_{\mathrm{inner}}}{R}=|\beta|-\sqrt{\beta^2-1}. \tag{15.11} \]

The plus root exceeds 1 and is the physical exterior stagnation radius; the other lies inside the excluded solid. The exterior point lies at \(\theta=\pi/2\) for \(\Gamma\gt0\) and \(3\pi/2\) for \(\Gamma\lt0\). At \(|\beta|=1\) both roots approach 1, continuously matching the merged surface point. At large \(|\beta|\), \(r_s/R\sim2|\beta|\), consistent with balancing the uniform flow against the vortex far from the doublet.

The running case has \(\beta=0.5\), giving surface angles \(30^\circ\) and \(150^\circ\), or coordinates \((\pm0.08660,0.05000)\ \mathrm m\). At \(\beta=1.5\), the exterior radius is \(2.618034R=0.261803\ \mathrm m\). Closed streamlines surround the cylinder inside the separatrix, the streamline through the exterior stagnation point that divides closed from passing motion. This is a circulation-induced topology in an irrotational field, not a viscous separated wake. The cylinder is still \(\psi=0\), but the exterior stagnation streamline now has \(\psi=\psi(r_s,\theta_s)\), generally nonzero. Substituting \(\beta=1.5\) gives \(\psi/(UR)=-0.651203\); keeping its level at zero would draw the wrong separatrix.

Figure 15.1 shows the computed streamlines for zero, moderate, and larger circulation. The displaced surface stagnation points and the closed curves in the right panel are changes in the ideal field's geometry; none is a computed viscous wake.

The pressure asymmetry gives lift with a definite sign L31 32:59

Bernoulli's equation (15.4) remains applicable under the same steady, inviscid, irrotational, constant-density, zero-body-force assumptions in the connected exterior. It does not require a globally single-valued velocity potential: the gradient of its Bernoulli quantity is zero throughout that region. Define \(a_\Gamma=\Gamma/(2\pi UR)=2\beta\). Using the surface velocity from (15.9),

\[ C_p=1-(-2\sin\theta+a_\Gamma)^2 =1-4\sin^2\theta+4a_\Gamma\sin\theta-a_\Gamma^2. \tag{15.12} \]

The first two terms reproduce (15.5). The constant \(-a_\Gamma^2\) changes pressure without producing a resultant on the closed circle. The term \(4a_\Gamma\sin\theta\) changes sign between upper and lower sides and is responsible for lift. For the running \(a_\Gamma=1\), the upper shoulder has \(C_p=0\) and the lower shoulder \(C_p=-8\). These are respectively 0 Pa and \(-480\ \mathrm{Pa}\) relative to upstream. Higher pressure above than below pushes the cylinder downward.

To derive the force, insert (15.12) in (15.6). In the drag integral, the constant, sine-squared, and sine terms each multiply cosine; each has zero integral over the period. In the lift integral, the constant and sine-squared terms are odd under reflection across the streamwise axis. The only surviving integral is \(4a_\Gamma\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta=4\pi a_\Gamma\). Therefore

\[ F_D'=0,\qquad F_L'=-2\pi\rho U^2R a_\Gamma=-\rho U\Gamma,\qquad C_L=-\frac{\Gamma}{UR}=-4\pi\beta. \tag{15.13} \]

The sign follows from the declared counterclockwise circulation and force on the solid. It would reverse if circulation were defined clockwise-positive or if the force on the fluid were requested. For the running case, \(F_L'=-1.2\times10\times6.283185=-75.398224\ \mathrm{N/m}\) and \(C_L=-6.283185\). The force normalization is \(\rho U^2D/2=12\ \mathrm{N/m}\). Reversing circulation reverses lift; setting circulation to zero recovers both zero forces. These are independent symmetry tests of the formula. They establish the cylinder result; a general theorem for arbitrary profiles needs its own geometric hypotheses and derivation.

Why circulation does not determine itself from rotation L31 40:37

The dimensionless rotation rate is \(\alpha=\Omega R/U\), the cylinder's surface speed divided by the incoming speed. It describes a moving solid; \(\beta\) describes a chosen exterior velocity field. Matching just the free-vortex contribution at \(r=R\) to \(\Omega R\) gives the additional prescription

\[ \Gamma=2\pi\Omega R^2,\qquad a_\Gamma=\alpha,\qquad \beta=\alpha/2, \qquad C_L=-2\pi\alpha. \tag{15.14} \]

This is an ad hoc correspondence, not a consequence of inviscid impermeability. It also does not impose no slip. Under (15.14), the total surface velocity is \(-2U\sin\theta+\Omega R\), which differs from the solid speed \(\Omega R\) by \(-2U\sin\theta\). No choice of the single constant \(\Gamma\) eliminates that angle-dependent difference when \(U\ne0\).

The running circulation would correspond, under this prescription, to \(\Omega=100\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), or 954.93 revolutions/minute, with \(\alpha=1\). The ideal calculation still supplies no motor torque, viscous power, or measured circulation at that shaft speed. Determining those quantities requires the viscous flow and its boundary and initial data. In particular, extrapolating the linear lift law to arbitrarily large rotation cannot establish an efficient rotor design; power is absent from the model.

The Magnus effect denotes the transverse force associated with a spinning body in a relative flow. The prescribed-circulation construction captures a pressure asymmetry and its lift sign, but it does not explain how spinning the wall creates the circulation. Viscosity supplies the missing interaction. A spinning sphere also has a finite three-dimensional wake, so its force coefficient cannot be borrowed from an infinite cylinder merely by choosing a diameter. Similarly, the aerodynamic lift of a flying disc is not established by showing a spinning-cylinder calculation; the disc's orientation and geometry create a separate aerodynamic problem.

Viscous rotation adds wake dynamics and dependence on span L31 50:50

A viscous Newtonian model with constant density and viscosity retains \(\partial\mathbf V/\partial t+(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V=-\nabla p/\rho+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf V\), \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0\), and the moving-wall no-slip condition. The time derivative permits wake oscillations; the viscous term transmits rotation and creates near-wall vorticity. Its independent parameters include \(\mathrm{Re}_D\) and \(\alpha\). No corresponding Reynolds-number dependence exists in (15.13), which is one reason it cannot predict the viscous regimes.

The Lecture 31 two-dimensional direct numerical simulations, meaning numerical integration of the full viscous governing equations without a turbulence closure, concern \(\mathrm{Re}_D=200\) and \(0\le\alpha\le5\). The slide attributes them to Mittal and Kumar (2003) and identifies steady intervals \(1.91\lt\alpha\lt4.34\) and \(\alpha\gt4.70\) within that studied range. Outside these intervals it shows ordinary vortex shedding at small rotation and a second shedding interval at higher rotation. These are source-specific computational results, not universal thresholds. The mean lift becomes more negative as positive rotation increases over the displayed cases, while the drag is generally finite. The ideal straight line (15.14) supplies neither the oscillations nor those boundaries.

The three-dimensional examples in the same recording introduce disturbances varying along the cylinder span. The closing slide specifies aspect ratio 12, where the aspect ratio is span divided by diameter. At \(\alpha=4.5\), its displayed structure varies strongly along the span; at \(\alpha=5\), the recording describes a transient disturbance followed by a steadier state for that configuration. A snapshot at dimensionless time \(tU/D=27\), with \(t\) the elapsed time, is evidence about one instant, not a proof of eventual stability. End conditions, permitted spanwise wavelengths, and duration matter when interpreting such simulations.

A more recent primary example makes the geometric limitation explicit. Yang, Wang, Guo, and Zhang (2023) study cylinders with two free ends, \(\mathrm{Re}_D\lt500\), aspect ratio at most 2, and \(\alpha\le2\). Their aspect-ratio-one results include an approximately \(0.3\lesssim\alpha\lesssim0.9\) stable interval for \(\mathrm{Re}_D\lt360\), followed by new low-frequency unsteadiness at stronger rotation (p. A15-1, PDF p. 1). These approximate intervals are findings for their finite geometry and tested parameter space, not corrections to the infinite-cylinder calculation. Their force definition includes pressure and viscous contributions and uses reference area equal to span times diameter; their wall condition specifies the solid's rotating velocity (p. A15-5, PDF p. 5). Keeping these conditions explicit prevents a misleading comparison of coefficients from different models.

Executable pressure integration and failure checks

The standard-library Python program implements (15.9), obtains pressure independently from the speed through (15.4), and integrates its force with periodic midpoint quadrature. Its inputs are SI values at the top and prescribed circulation; it reads and writes no files and prints only to standard output. It rejects a point inside the cylinder, nonfinite radius, angle or circulation, and quadrature counts that are not integers at least eight. The positive constants \(U,R,\rho\) are fixed teaching inputs rather than a general parameter-validation interface.

The assertions compare the numerical force against the independently derived sign and magnitude in (15.13), check impermeability at the listed wall angles, test zero and reversed circulation, and substitute the exterior stagnation root into both velocity components. Increasing quadrature resolution gives the same six printed decimals because this pressure integrand contains only low-order trigonometric harmonics, which a complete equally spaced period integrates exceptionally well. This is an arithmetic and sign check, not empirical validation of potential flow.

import math

U, R, RHO = 10.0, 0.1, 1.2  # constructed inputs: m/s, m, kg/m^3
GAMMA = 2*math.pi*U*R        # prescribed CCW circulation, m^2/s


def velocity(r, theta, gamma=GAMMA):
    if not math.isfinite(r) or r < R:
        raise ValueError("point must lie on or outside cylinder")
    if not math.isfinite(theta) or not math.isfinite(gamma):
        raise ValueError("angle and circulation must be finite")
    vr = U*(1 - R**2/r**2)*math.cos(theta)
    vt = -U*(1 + R**2/r**2)*math.sin(theta) + gamma/(2*math.pi*r)
    return vr, vt


def pressure(theta, gamma=GAMMA):
    vr, vt = velocity(R, theta, gamma)
    return 0.5*RHO*(U**2 - vr**2 - vt**2)


def force(n, gamma=GAMMA):
    if not isinstance(n, int) or n < 8:
        raise ValueError("quadrature needs an integer n >= 8")
    step = 2*math.pi/n
    angles = [(i + 0.5)*step for i in range(n)]
    drag = -R*step*math.fsum(pressure(t, gamma)*math.cos(t)
                               for t in angles)
    lift = -R*step*math.fsum(pressure(t, gamma)*math.sin(t)
                               for t in angles)
    return drag, lift


for n in (32, 64, 128):
    drag, lift = force(n)
    assert abs(drag) < 1e-10
    assert abs(lift + RHO*U*GAMMA) < 1e-10
    print(f"n={n:3d}: drag={abs(drag):.6f}, lift={lift:.6f} N/m")
print(f"Gamma={GAMMA:.6f} m^2/s, CL={lift/(RHO*U**2*R):.6f}")
for degrees in (0, 30, 90, 150, 270):
    theta = math.radians(degrees)
    vr, vt = velocity(R, theta)
    cp = pressure(theta)/(0.5*RHO*U**2)
    assert abs(vr) < 1e-12
    print(f"theta={degrees:3d} deg: Vtheta={vt:7.3f} m/s, Cp={cp:7.3f}")
assert abs(force(64, 0.0)[1]) < 1e-10
assert abs(force(64, -GAMMA)[1] - RHO*U*GAMMA) < 1e-10
for beta in (0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5):
    gamma = 4*math.pi*U*R*beta
    if beta <= 1:
        angle = math.degrees(math.asin(beta))
        print(f"beta={beta:.1f}: surface angles {angle:.3f}, "
              f"{180 - angle:.3f} deg")
    else:
        radius = R*(beta + math.sqrt(beta**2 - 1))
        vr, vt = velocity(radius, math.pi/2, gamma)
        assert math.hypot(vr, vt) < 1e-12
        print(f"beta={beta:.1f}: outer stagnation r/R={radius/R:.6f}")
print("zero circulation and circulation reversal checks passed")

Three deliberate changes expose different failures. Replacing the doublet's minus sign in \(V_r\) by a plus makes the wall impermeability assertion fail. Replacing the positive vortex contribution to \(V_\theta\) by a negative one makes the numerical lift oppose the claimed circulation and fails the lift assertion. Reversing the pressure-force sign in force also fails that assertion. Those mutations were run separately. The rejected interior point and invalid quadrature count were also exercised. Merely checking that drag is zero would miss both lift-sign errors. Shared mistakes in the pressure formula and its expected force could still escape, which is why the signed pressure explanation and independent integral are retained.

What the running case establishes

The prescribed-circulation model predicts the entire exterior velocity, two surface stagnation locations at \(30^\circ\) and \(150^\circ\), upper and lower shoulder pressure coefficients 0 and \(-8\), zero drag, and 75.40 N/m downward lift. Those conclusions are exact deductions within the declared steady, two-dimensional, inviscid, incompressible, irrotational, zero-body-force model. Their dependence on circulation is explicit. The doublet singularity and the multivalued potential are compatible with a smooth, single-valued exterior velocity.

The model does not determine the circulation from shaft speed, satisfy tangential no slip, predict separation or vortex shedding, or calculate torque and motor power. Its zero drag is a property of its pressure field, not a prediction that a physical cylinder at large Reynolds number needs no support against the stream. The base-pressure exercise illustrates the missing pressure-drag mechanism but contains prescribed values rather than measured data. A finite rotor further requires its span, ends, and nearby boundaries. A design calculation must supply the additional viscous and experimental information before turning the ideal 75.40 N/m into a working load specification.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive and check normalization). A source \(q_s\) at \(-\ell/2\) and sink \(-q_s\) at \(+\ell/2\) form the pair used in (15.2). Show how \(m=q_s\ell/(2\pi)\) follows. At \(r=2R,\theta=0\), choose \(q_s\ell=2\pi UR^2\) and compare the finite-pair potential with the doublet for \(\ell/R=0.2\) and 0.1. What happens if \(q_s\ell\) is mistaken for \(m\)?

Solution

At a fixed point, \(r_+^2=r^2+r\ell\cos\theta+\ell^2/4\) and \(r_-^2=r^2-r\ell\cos\theta+\ell^2/4\). Expanding their logarithms to first order gives \(\ln r_+-\ln r_-=\ell\cos\theta/r+O((\ell/r)^3)\). The coefficient multiplying \(\cos\theta/r\) is therefore \(q_s\ell/(2\pi)\). On the positive axis with \(r=2R\), the ratio of finite-pair to doublet potential is \((r/\ell)\ln[(r+\ell/2)/(r-\ell/2)]\). It equals 1.000834586 and 1.000208411 for the stated separations. Halving separation divides the relative error by about four, consistent with the next term \(\ell^2/(12r^2)\). If \(m=q_s\ell\) is used instead, the doublet is too strong by \(2\pi\); at fixed \(U\) the impermeable-circle radius becomes \(\sqrt{2\pi}R=2.506628R\), rather than \(R\). The error changes the geometry, not merely the units of a coefficient.

Exercise 2 (compute an inverse pressure problem). In the running ideal case, suppose two shoulder pressure taps give \(C_p(\pi/2)=0\) and \(C_p(3\pi/2)=-8\). Infer the sign and magnitude of circulation without using (15.13). Then infer lift by integration. Why would using just one tap be ambiguous?

Solution

With \(a_\Gamma=\Gamma/(2\pi UR)\), (15.12) gives upper and lower values \(1-(a_\Gamma-2)^2\) and \(1-(a_\Gamma+2)^2\). Subtracting lower from upper eliminates the quadratic and constant terms: \(\Delta C_p=8a_\Gamma\). Here \(\Delta C_p=8\), so \(a_\Gamma=1\) and \(\Gamma=2\pi UR=6.283185\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), counterclockwise. Inserting the full distribution \(C_p=-4\sin^2\theta+4\sin\theta\) into the lift integral leaves \(4\int\sin^2\theta\,d\theta=4\pi\). Thus \(F_L'=-(\rho U^2R/2)4\pi=-75.398224\ \mathrm{N/m}\). A single pressure reading measures a squared speed. The upper reading alone gives \(a_\Gamma=1\) or 3; the lower tap resolves the sign of the local speed contribution. The inference assumes the ideal field and cannot by itself validate it for a real wall.

Exercise 3 (counterexample and assumption check). A student claims that enforcing \(\Gamma=2\pi\Omega R^2\) makes the potential flow satisfy the boundary condition of a rotating cylinder. Evaluate the relative tangential velocity at \(\theta=0,\pi/2,3\pi/2\) for \(\Omega=100\ \mathrm{rad/s}\). Can another constant circulation enforce no slip everywhere?

Solution

The wall speed is \(\Omega R=10\ \mathrm{m/s}\). With the proposed circulation the ideal fluid speeds are 10, \(-10\), and 30 m/s at the three angles, so the fluid-minus-wall speeds are 0, \(-20\), and \(+20\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The agreement on the streamwise axis is accidental. For any circulation the difference is \(-2U\sin\theta+\Gamma/(2\pi R)-\Omega R\). Requiring zero at both shoulders demands that the same constant equal \(+2U\) and \(-2U\), impossible for \(U\ne0\). Impermeability holds at all these points because \(V_r=0\); tangential no slip fails. A viscous wall layer, rather than a different scalar circulation alone, is required to repair the boundary condition.

Exercise 4 (derive and classify stagnation topology). For prescribed \(\beta=-1.5\), find every stagnation point of the mathematical field (15.9) with \(r\gt0\), identify which belongs to the exterior fluid, and calculate the stream-function value of its separatrix. Explain why the cylinder's zero stream-function level is different.

Solution

A wall stagnation point would require \(\sin\theta=-1.5\), impossible. Elsewhere \(V_r=0\) requires \(\cos\theta=0\). Positive \(r\) and negative circulation can cancel only at the lower shoulder direction \(\theta=3\pi/2\). The radius equation is \(s_r^2-3s_r+1=0\), giving \(s_r=(3\pm\sqrt5)/2=2.618034,0.381966\). The larger root is in the fluid, at \((0,-0.261803)\ \mathrm m\); the smaller is at \((0,-0.038197)\ \mathrm m\), inside the excluded cylinder. Substitution into (15.8) gives \(\psi/(UR)=-(s_r-1/s_r)+3\ln s_r=+0.651203\). A streamline through that point must retain this value, whereas at \(r=R\) both terms in (15.8) vanish and \(\psi=0\). The two levels coincide at the limiting merger \(|\beta|=1\), not generally outside it.

Exercise 5 (design a force measurement). Two tests of the same stationary cylinder show identical base-pressure coefficients. Does this establish equal drag? Construct two upper/lower symmetric pressure distributions with equal base values but different pressure drag, and specify the missing measurements needed for total drag.

Solution

Let the first distribution be any \(C_p(\theta)\), and let the second add \(\Delta C_p=A\sin^2\theta\cos\theta\), with \(A\) a dimensionless chosen amplitude. At the base \(\theta=0\), and also at the front, the increment is zero; upper/lower symmetry is preserved. Yet its drag increment is \(-\tfrac12A\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=-A\pi/8\), because \(\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta=(1-\cos4\theta)/8\). For \(A=0.4\), the coefficient changes by \(-0.157080\), or \(-1.884956\ \mathrm{N/m}\) in the running case, despite identical base values. These are prescribed mathematical distributions used to disprove the inference, not claimed realizable measured states. Obtain enough circumferential pressure samples to resolve the cosine-weighted integral, and measure or model the streamwise viscous traction. For unsteady tests specify averaging duration and force fluctuations; for finite cylinders include spanwise variation and end forces. Base pressure can be a useful controlled comparison indicator but is not a complete force survey.

Exercise 6 (judge a simulation and its limits). A designer uses the Lecture 31 two-dimensional \(\mathrm{Re}_D=200\) steady interval to claim that a finite cylinder of aspect ratio one at \(\alpha=1\) must be steady. Explain the inference error using Yang et al.'s finite-cylinder results. Then prescribe a minimal comparison that could test the claim, and state what the chapter's code can contribute.

Solution

There are two errors. First, \(\alpha=1\) lies outside both two-dimensional steady intervals quoted in the chapter: \(1.91\lt\alpha\lt4.34\) and \(\alpha\gt4.70\), within the studied range \(0\le\alpha\le5\). The cited two-dimensional result therefore does not establish steadiness even for its own geometry at the proposed rotation rate. Second, a two-dimensional calculation excludes variation along the cylinder axis, whereas a finite cylinder with two free ends permits end flow and three-dimensional disturbances. Even a rotation rate inside a two-dimensional steady interval would not establish steadiness for that finite geometry.

Yang et al.'s tested aspect-ratio-one geometry has an approximate stable interval \(0.3\lesssim\alpha\lesssim0.9\) below \(\mathrm{Re}_D=360\), and stronger rotation introduces low-frequency wake patterns (p. A15-1). This is evidence about their finite geometry and tested parameter space, not an exact universal onset at 0.9. To test the designer's actual case, keep \(\mathrm{Re}_D=200\), \(\alpha=1\), aspect ratio, free-end geometry, inflow, and confinement fixed. Allow three-dimensional disturbances, integrate long enough to distinguish transient decay from sustained variation, and compare lift and drag time histories under spatial and time-step refinement. A pressure survey or independent simulation provides further evidence. The chapter's code checks inviscid pressure-force algebra for prescribed circulation. It has no viscosity, axial coordinate, perturbation evolution, or time integration and therefore cannot settle the stability question.
