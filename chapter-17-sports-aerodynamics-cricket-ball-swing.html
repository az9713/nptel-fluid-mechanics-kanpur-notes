Sports aerodynamics: conventional and reverse swing of a cricket ball

A ball of mass 0.156 kg and diameter 71 mm travels 18 m at 30 m/s and arrives 0.30 m to the side of its initial line. If its initial lateral velocity is zero, a constant lateral force of 0.260 N would produce that displacement in 0.600 s. The force is small, but its direction decides whether a prediction based on the seam orientation succeeds or fails. Why can the same orientation produce motion toward the seam in one flow state and away from it in another? The answer requires the boundary layers and the pressure distribution, rather than speed alone. This constructed flight case connects those mechanisms to a measurable trajectory.

Grounded in Lecture 33 of NPTEL Fluid Mechanics, Prof. Sanjay Mittal, IIT Kanpur. Lecture material: sphere drag crisis and roughness, sports-projectile comparisons, ball geometry, swing terminology, force and flow measurements, conventional and reverse swing, delayed laminar separation, environmental effects, wake vortices, trajectories, contrast swing, spin, and the proposed baseball-like knuckleball. Research extensions: Parekh, Chaplot and Mittal (2024) on a five-trip model and the initial laminar mechanism; Shah and Mittal (2023) on force measurements and estimated knuckleball trajectories; the accessible abstract of Deshpande, Shakya and Mittal (2018) on roughened balls. Original teaching constructions: the 18 m flight, surface-force derivation, similarity argument, force-history integrals, computed figures, sensitivity checks, executable calculation, and six exercises. The figures are explanatory constructions, not reproductions of measurements.

The running case and its force model

Let \(x\) measure distance along the ball's initial flight from bowler toward batter, \(y\) measure horizontal lateral displacement, and \(z\) point vertically upward. The ball's centre has velocity components \(V_x,V_y,V_z\), in m/s, at time \(t\), in seconds. Air at rest in the ground frame approaches the ball from its front in the ball frame. Its relative speed is \(U\). For the first calculation, take \(V_x=U=30\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(V_y(0)=0\), \(y(0)=0\), and the release-to-observation distance \(L=18\ \mathrm m\). This is an airborne segment, with no bounce or bat impact; it is not an assertion about a regulation pitch length.

The mass is \(m=0.156\ \mathrm{kg}\), the diameter \(D=0.071\ \mathrm m\), and the frontal area \(A=\pi D^2/4=0.003959\ \mathrm{m^2}\). These are chosen inputs of the running case, close to the ball measured by Shah and Mittal (2023): their specimen's diameter is 71 mm (E16-3), and their trajectory calculation uses 0.156 kg (E16-8). Take the air density \(\rho=1.20\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) as a rounded assumed input, not a measured atmospheric state. Use their 25 °C kinematic viscosity \(\nu=1.562\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) (E16-3). The dynamic viscosity is \(\mu=\rho\nu\), in Pa s. The chosen density and source viscosity define the calculation; they do not constitute a precision property table.

The formal model is Newton's law for the centre of mass, with the aerodynamic force supplied by a force coefficient. Define the dynamic pressure \(p_d=\rho U^2/2\), in Pa, the signed lateral force \(F_y\), in N, and the lateral force coefficient \(C_Y=F_y/(p_dA)\). Positive \(C_Y\) means force along \(+y\); it does not by itself mean conventional swing until the seam direction is specified. The force model and the flight model are different parts of the problem:

\[ F_y=p_dA C_Y,\qquad m\frac{d^2y}{dt^2}=F_y. \tag{17.1} \]

The first equation defines a coefficient. A measurement or a flow calculation must determine its value and sign. The second is a mechanical law. Integrating it can predict a trajectory from a specified force, but cannot discover the boundary-layer state that generated that force. A ball-flow solver would additionally solve the fluid equations around the seam and in the wake. The program below performs only the trajectory calculation.

The flow interpretation treats air as a continuum, so velocity and stress are meaningful at scales much larger than molecular lengths. It assumes incompressible flow, meaning that a moving fluid parcel retains its density, with uniform constant density and viscosity for each case. A Newtonian fluid has viscous stress proportional to the local rate of deformation. No slip is the empirical continuum boundary condition that fluid at a solid wall has the wall's velocity. These assumptions support the boundary-layer description; they are not consequences of the centre-of-mass law (17.1).

A sphere: why turbulence can reduce pressure drag L33 2:10

A cricket ball is a bluff body: a body for which appreciable flow separation leaves a wake behind it. A boundary layer is the near-surface region where viscosity changes the air velocity from the wall velocity, imposed by the no-slip condition, toward the outer-flow velocity. A laminar boundary layer has an orderly mean transport structure without the irregular turbulent fluctuations. A turbulent boundary layer has such fluctuations and substantial momentum transport across the layer. Neither term describes the entire atmosphere around the ball.

For a local patch of a stationary smooth surface, use \(s\) along the external flow and \(n\) normal to the wall, measured into the fluid. The tangential fluid velocity is \(V_s\). In a Newtonian constant-property fluid the wall shear is \(\tau_w=\mu(\partial V_s/\partial n)_{n=0}\), counted positive for attached forward flow. These definitions and the following boundary-layer interpretation are taken as prerequisites here. A rising outer pressure in the downstream direction is an adverse pressure gradient. It slows the already slow near-wall fluid. In steady two-dimensional attached-flow reasoning, separation begins when the wall derivative reaches zero and reverse flow develops beyond it. A three-dimensional ball has separation lines, and a single zero-shear point is insufficient to characterize its whole surface.

Turbulent mixing can bring high tangential momentum from farther from the wall into the near-wall region. Consequently, in appropriate pressure gradients a turbulent layer remains attached farther around a sphere than a laminar one. Later separation changes the rear pressure and can reduce the pressure contribution to drag even though surface friction increases. There is no conflict: total drag is the sum of pressure and viscous contributions, and those contributions need not change in the same direction.

Define drag magnitude \(F_D\) as the aerodynamic force opposing flight, and \(C_D=F_D/(p_dA)\). The Reynolds number formed with relative speed and diameter is

\[ \mathrm{Re}_D=\frac{\rho UD}{\mu}=\frac{UD}{\nu},\qquad F_D=\frac12\rho U^2A C_D. \tag{17.2} \]

For the running case \(\mathrm{Re}_D=136{,}364\). The ratio follows by comparing inertial force per volume, of order \(\rho U^2/D\), with viscous force per volume, of order \(\mu U/D^2\). It does not prove that viscosity is negligible at the surface: the boundary layer has a much smaller normal length scale than \(D\).

A drag crisis is a rapid fall of \(C_D\) as the boundary-layer and separation pattern changes with Reynolds number. Equation (17.2) also explains why a falling coefficient need not mean a falling dimensional force. At fixed density and diameter, two states obey \(F_{D,2}/F_{D,1}=(U_2/U_1)^2(C_{D,2}/C_{D,1})\). For a constructed 20 % speed increase, the drag falls only if the coefficient falls below \(1/1.2^2=0.694\) of its previous value. If it halves, drag becomes 0.72 of its previous value. These are arithmetic conditions, not universal sphere data.

A laminar separation bubble is a local region in which a laminar boundary layer separates, the detached shear layer transitions, and flow reattaches before a later final separation. Thus “laminar separation” need not mean permanent detachment. The sequence matters for swing because reattachment can sustain low surface pressure farther downstream. No unique crisis Reynolds number is assumed here: surface condition, incoming disturbances, seam geometry, and support interference all matter.

Roughness and the comparison of sports projectiles L33 9:57

A seam is an organized protrusion, whereas distributed roughness covers an area of the surface. Either can disturb a boundary layer, but they need not produce the same disturbance or the same separation pattern. A protrusion can trigger transition, induce a small separation region, or modify a still-laminar flow. The useful question is how its height compares with the local boundary-layer thickness. A fixed seam can become more influential as the boundary layer becomes thinner with increasing Reynolds number.

Dimensional analysis organizes these possibilities. Let \(k_s\) be a representative seam height, \(k_r\) a representative distributed-roughness height, \(\theta_s\) the seam angle, and \(I_\infty\) the incoming root-mean-square velocity fluctuation divided by \(U\). For an incompressible flow of a fixed roughness pattern, the force coefficient can be written schematically as

\[ C_Y=\mathcal F\left(\mathrm{Re}_D,\theta_s,\frac{k_s}{D},\frac{k_r}{D},I_\infty,\frac{\Omega D}{2U},\hbox{surface pattern and spin axis}\right). \tag{17.3} \]

Here \(\Omega\) is the magnitude of the ball's angular velocity, in rad/s, and \(\Omega D/(2U)\) compares surface rotation speed with translation speed. The function \(\mathcal F\) is unknown. Its arguments identify the information a speed-only rule discards. A single roughness height cannot describe the spatial arrangement of scratches or the difference between the two halves; that information is retained explicitly as a pattern.

Golf dimples, tennis-ball felt, baseball stitching, cricket seams, and shuttlecock shape illustrate different ways of controlling transition, separation, and resistance. Comparing their drag coefficients does not compare equal dimensional forces or athletic effort. At the same \(C_D\), a larger \(A\) or larger \(U\) raises drag; energy lost over a path is the integral of drag along that path. Whole-game effort additionally includes muscle work, duration, movement, and recovery. A drag-coefficient graph alone cannot rank how tiring different sports are. Historical dimensions in such a comparison are not current equipment specifications.

Seam orientation and the meaning of conventional swing L33 18:56

The prominent seam divides a cricket ball into two hemispheres. It is a band of stitching, with a plane approximately through the centre. Its orientation relative to the incoming air breaks the reflection symmetry that a smooth nonrotating sphere would possess. The main seam is the organizing geometric feature here; secondary seams and surface markings introduce additional departures from an ideal sphere.

Take a top view from \(+z\), looking down on the horizontal \(x,y\) plane. The ball flies to the right along \(+x\); the relative air flows to the left along \(-x\). Choose the seam plane vertical, and its horizontal trace angled from the forward \(+x\) direction toward \(+y\) by \(\theta_s=30^\circ\). On the upstream, leading half of the ball, the seam is then encountered on the \(+y\) side. Call that side S, the seam side, and call the opposite side N, the non-seam side. These names identify which side encounters the seam in the front part of the flow; they do not claim the seam is absent everywhere on the other half.

With this fixed convention, conventional swing has a positive mean lateral force toward S, and reverse swing has a negative mean lateral force toward N. Reversing the seam angle reverses these coordinate signs for an otherwise reflected geometry. “Inswing” and “outswing” also require the batter's stance, so they are not needed to define the aerodynamic regimes. The papers' lateral \(z\) coordinate is this chapter's \(y\), while this chapter reserves \(z\) for vertical height. Their seam-angle symbol is \(\phi_T\); it is translated to \(\theta_s\) here to preserve the book's use of \(\phi\) for velocity potential.

Figure 17.1 fixes the viewing convention before the pressure mechanism is considered. Swing is motion in the air caused by aerodynamic force. Deflection when a seam strikes the pitch is a contact-mechanics problem. Spin-induced Magnus force is a further aerodynamic mechanism: rotation changes the flow asymmetry even for a ball without an angled cricket seam. A stationary ball mounted at an angle in a wind tunnel can have seam-induced side force with \(\Omega=0\). Thus a measured side force is not automatically evidence of Magnus force. A spinning real delivery can combine the mechanisms.

From separation asymmetry to a lateral force L33 32:06

The ball does not respond directly to a label such as “turbulent.” It responds to forces. Let \(S_b\) denote its closed surface and \(\mathbf n\) the unit normal pointing outward from the ball into the air. The air pressure \(p\) pushes inward, giving force \(-p\mathbf n\,dA_b\) on a surface patch of area \(dA_b\). Let \(\mathbf t_\nu\) be the viscous traction of the air on the ball. Taking the \(y\) component and summing gives

\[ F_y=\int_{S_b}[-p n_y+t_{\nu,y}]\,dA_b. \tag{17.4} \]

This is the surface-force construction of Chapter 2, applied with the normal of the solid body. Define the far-field pressure \(p_\infty\) and pressure coefficient \(C_p=(p-p_\infty)/p_d\). A uniform pressure produces no resultant because \(\int_{S_b}\mathbf n\,dA_b=\mathbf0\), by the divergence theorem applied to a constant field. Substituting \(p=p_\infty+p_dC_p\) into (17.4) and dividing by \(p_dA\) yields

\[ C_Y=-\frac1A\int_{S_b} C_p n_y\,dA_b +\frac1{p_dA}\int_{S_b}t_{\nu,y}\,dA_b. \tag{17.5} \]

The sign can be checked without a flow solver. On S, \(n_y\) is positive. If \(C_p\) becomes more negative there while the rest stays fixed, the pressure contribution to \(C_Y\) increases. Lower pressure on S therefore pulls the resultant toward S relative to its original value. A point of large suction alone is insufficient: (17.5) weights its area and orientation, and includes every surface patch. Separation affects this integral by changing where pressure recovers and where low-pressure regions persist.

For a simple check, approximate pressure by one constant \(p_S\) on the \(y>0\) hemisphere and another \(p_N\) on \(y<0\), neglecting viscous lateral force. The projected areas give \(\int_{y>0}n_y\,dA_b=A\) and \(\int_{y<0}n_y\,dA_b=-A\). Hence \(F_y=(p_N-p_S)A\), or \(C_Y=C_{p,N}-C_{p,S}\). The running force 0.260 N corresponds to \(p_N-p_S=65.7\ \mathrm{Pa}\), about 12.2 % of its 540 Pa dynamic pressure. This model checks the sign and scale; a real distribution is continuous, three-dimensional, and far from constant over each hemisphere.

The wake is the disturbed flow downstream of the ball. An asymmetric surface flow generally leaves an asymmetric wake. Momentum balance provides a complementary force measurement: the net lateral momentum transferred to the air is opposite to the lateral force on the ball, after accounting for pressure and other stresses on the control surface. It would be incorrect to infer force merely from which side of a picture contains more visible smoke; velocity fluxes and boundary stresses determine that balance.

Different measurements constrain different pieces of the explanation. A force balance measures the resultant. Surface-pressure ports sample the integrand of (17.5). Oil-film patterns indicate surface-flow directions and accumulation near separation. Particle image velocimetry measures particle displacement over a known short time interval to estimate velocity in an illuminated plane. A plane does not reveal all three-dimensional structures. Agreement among these diagnostics is more informative than fitting a trajectory alone, which can conceal errors shared by its assumed force inputs.

Conventional swing, reverse swing, and the initial laminar mechanism L33 34:34

A useful first mechanism for conventional swing is that the seam changes the boundary layer on S while N retains early laminar separation. If S then separates later and sustains stronger integrated suction, (17.5) gives force toward S. Once N also undergoes transition and reattachment, its separation and pressure recovery can differ from S enough to reverse the pressure imbalance. Turbulence on both sides does not require equal pressure distributions: the seam has disturbed one side differently.

The phrase “the seam makes one side turbulent” is nevertheless incomplete. Parekh, Chaplot and Mittal (2024) resolved a five-trip sphere model at a fixed seam angle of 30°, for \(5\times10^4\le\mathrm{Re}_D\le4.5\times10^5\) (A23-1, A23-6). Their initial conventional-swing states at \(7.5\times10^4\) and \(10^5\) retain laminar boundary layers. The seam delays laminar separation, especially toward the poles; the affected area grows with Reynolds number (A23-9, A23-12–13). This changes the pressure integral continuously before a seam-side turbulent separation bubble becomes the main feature.

Inferring a positive coefficient from a trajectory therefore does not establish a turbulent boundary layer on S. A positive pressure integral can arise from different flow states. In the 2024 study, later conventional swing contains a seam-side separation bubble and turbulent regions. At \(\mathrm{Re}_D=4.5\times10^5\), a bubble also exists on N, whose suction exceeds S, and the coefficient is negative (A23-11). Their model is a sphere with five specified trip wires, not a scan of the 71 mm specimen. Its major trip height is 1.4 % of diameter and the four minor trips are each 0.49 % (A23-6).

An actual ball, a sphere with five trips, and a sphere with one trip can reverse at different Reynolds numbers. For the actual-ball experiments of Shah and Mittal (2023), the tested range is \(0.45\times10^5\) to \(3.40\times10^5\), with seam angles from 0° to 90° in 10° steps and very low tunnel turbulence (E16-3). Their 30° specimen changes force direction around \(1.7\)–\(1.8\times10^5\) (E16-6–8). Assigning the 2024 model's reversal range to this specimen would be a substantial error. Ball geometry and surface condition are part of the result.

Both experiment and computation support the possibility of reverse swing with a new ball. Neither supports a universal bowling speed at which it must occur. For a specified ball and atmosphere, speed converts to Reynolds number through (17.2); the coefficient still requires the other arguments of (17.3). The running value \(1.36\times10^5\) therefore sets a comparison scale, not a regime certification.

Environmental properties and three-dimensional wake structure L33 46:31

At fixed diameter and speed, changing kinematic viscosity changes Reynolds number inversely. Two hypothetical atmospheres with \(\nu_1=1.50\times10^{-5}\) and \(\nu_2=1.68\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) give \(142{,}000\) and \(126{,}786\), respectively, for the running ball. These are an input sensitivity experiment, not a forecast for named grounds or temperatures. The ratio is 1.12. A force curve changing rapidly in this interval could respond strongly to such a property change; a nearly flat curve would respond weakly.

Density also changes force directly through \(p_d\). At fixed \(C_Y\), a 10 % density increase raises \(F_y\) by 10 %. But if the density change shifts Reynolds number and thereby changes \(C_Y\), the total effect can have a different size or even a different sign. Statements about temperature or humidity therefore require a thermodynamic state, a property model, and a relevant force curve.

The pressure difference between the two sides also produces three-dimensional vortical structures. Let \(\mathbf V_{\mathrm a}\) be the local air-velocity field, in m/s, distinct from the ball's centre-of-mass velocity. The air vorticity is \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf V_{\mathrm a}\), in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), a measure of local fluid rotation. The 2024 computation finds pairs of streamwise, wing-tip-like vortices, and their polarity reverses when the lateral pressure asymmetry reverses (A23-1, A23-21). These structures make a two-dimensional separation sketch incomplete. “Wing-tip-like” identifies a structural analogy, rather than a claim that the ball has wings. No acoustic consequence or visible condensation is inferred from the existence of these computed vortices.

Force histories, flight time, and the required deflection L33 51:13

Suppose downstream speed is constant, lateral angles are small, and prescribed lateral force is constant. The flight time is \(t_f=L/U\). Integrating \(m\ddot y=F_y\) with zero initial lateral velocity gives \(\dot y=F_yt/m\); a second integration gives \(y=F_yt^2/(2m)\). To obtain a target displacement \(Y=y(t_f)\), therefore,

\[ F_y=\frac{2mYU^2}{L^2},\qquad C_Y=\frac{4mY}{\rho A L^2}. \tag{17.6} \]

The force scales with the square of speed, because faster flight leaves less time to accumulate displacement. The inferred coefficient is independent of speed within this model: the \(U^2\) in required force cancels the \(U^2\) in dynamic pressure. For \(Y=0.30\ \mathrm m\), \(t_f=0.600\ \mathrm s\), \(F_y=0.2600\ \mathrm N\), and \(C_Y=0.1216\). The final lateral velocity is 1.00 m/s, with slope \(V_y/U=0.0333\), about 1.91°. This makes the small-angle approximation plausible for an initial estimate. It does not justify a constant speed or coefficient in real flight.

A source coefficient of roughly 0.35 in a conventional-swing state of the five-trip model would exceed the required 0.122. That is a scale comparison, not an assignment of 0.35 to the running ball: the source geometry, flow state, and averaging differ. Likewise \(-0.1216\), maintained throughout the flight, would give \(-0.30\ \mathrm m\). A deflection alone cannot identify the seam angle or the microscopic surface condition.

A changing force requires its history. Integrate Newton's law once to obtain lateral velocity, then integrate that result over time. Interchanging the integration order gives

\[ Y=\frac1m\int_0^{t_f}(t_f-t)F_y(t)\,dt =\frac{\rho A}{2m}\int_0^L(L-x)C_Y(x)\,dx. \tag{17.7} \]

The second form assumes the same constant \(U\) and uses \(x=Ut\). Early force has more influence on final displacement because it acts on lateral velocity for longer. For example, \(C_Y=C_0\cos(\pi x/L)\), with \(C_0=0.1216\), is positive during the first half and negative during the second. Its integral over \(x\) is zero, so final lateral velocity is zero; displacement is nevertheless \(4/\pi^2\) times the constant-coefficient displacement, or 0.12159 m. The negative second-half force brakes an existing lateral velocity. It does not instantly reverse motion.

The displacement factor follows directly from \(\int_0^L(L-x)\cos(\pi x/L)\,dx=2L^2/\pi^2\), obtained by integration by parts; the constant-force integral is \(L^2/2\). Their ratio is \(4/\pi^2\). Integrating curvature once also gives \(dy/dx=\rho AC_0L\sin(\pi x/L)/(2m\pi)\), positive for every interior point. Figure 17.2 plots the resulting trajectories and exposes the difference between a force reversal and a motion reversal.

A more complete trajectory would evolve relative speed, gravity, seam orientation, and possibly spin. In the horizontal approximation of Shah and Mittal (2023), forces are interpolated from stationary wind-tunnel measurements at the instantaneous Reynolds number and seam angle (E16-8). This is a quasi-steady closure: flow is assumed to adjust rapidly enough to use the time-averaged stationary force. It neglects aerodynamic memory and instantaneous force fluctuations. Their calculated optimum around 90 km/h for a fixed nonrotating configuration is tied to that data and model (E16-10), not a speed-only law for all deliveries.

Gravity is omitted only from our horizontal calculation. During 0.600 s, free fall alone produces \(gt_f^2/2=1.77\ \mathrm m\) of vertical displacement for \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) and zero initial vertical velocity. A real release angle, vertical velocity, lift, and bounce are consequently essential for a full delivery. Axial drag is also omitted, so our flight time is imposed rather than predicted. These omissions prevent treating the calculation as a validated flight simulator.

Contrast swing and slowly changing seam orientation L33 52:29

Differential roughness adds another source of asymmetry. N need not remain the smoother side of an aged ball. The accessible abstract of Deshpande, Shakya and Mittal (2018) reports measurements on manually roughened balls and a reversal of the usual ordering for certain seam orientations: a ball with rough N and smooth S can exhibit reverse swing at lower speeds and conventional swing at higher speeds. This result is confined to the qualitative finding stated in that abstract; no original full-paper page, roughness calibration, or numerical threshold is claimed.

This form of contrast swing is consistent with the force model. Roughness on N changes its transition and separation history independently of the seam's action on S. The difference in (17.5) can change sign in an order unlike that for a new ball. Calling a ball “old” does not supply a roughness map. The scale, location, and pattern of wear matter, so age alone cannot determine \(C_Y\).

Slow rotation changes seam orientation during flight. A baseball-like knuckleball aims for a lateral force whose direction changes as the seam turns. Shah and Mittal's 2023 proposal uses a spin axis close to vertical, so the seam sweeps through horizontal orientations (E16-3, E16-8). This differs from spin about a horizontal axis, which can change vertical force while retaining lateral reflection symmetry in an idealized setup. At sufficiently low spin, seam-induced changes can dominate a fixed-direction Magnus contribution; the latter cannot be ignored for arbitrary spin rate.

Let \(N_r\) be the number of full rotations during flight. For constant angular speed \(\Omega\), \(N_r=\Omega t_f/(2\pi)\). A half-turn in the running 0.600 s flight requires \(\Omega=\pi/0.600=5.24\ \mathrm{rad/s}\). Its surface-speed ratio is \(\Omega D/(2U)=0.00620\). A small ratio distinguishes this slow rotation from a strongly spinning delivery; it does not quantify the omitted Magnus force. The force curve as a function of orientation is still needed.

The 2023 study combines measured forces with a horizontal trajectory model. For its initial seam angle of 30°, its criterion balances final displacement, the number of lateral direction changes, and release speed. It identifies a favorable region near half a rotation and 115 km/h, with a final displacement about 0.2 m (E16-11–12, Fig. 10). This is an estimated trajectory criterion, not a demonstrated probability of dismissing a batter. A force reversal requires \(F_y\) to change sign; a lateral direction reversal requires \(V_y\) to change sign. For continuous histories, each reversal passes through zero, but a zero without a sign change is insufficient. Equation (17.7) explains why force and motion can reverse at different locations. The cosine example has a force reversal halfway through but no lateral direction reversal before the endpoint.

Executable force and trajectory checks

The program uses the standard library only. Inputs are the declared SI values, a signed prescribed coefficient as a function of downstream position, and a positive integer step count. It returns displacement in metres and slope \(dy/dx\). It integrates \(y''(x)=\rho A C_Y(x)/(2m)\), obtained by substituting \(d/dt=U\,d/dx\) into (17.1). Each step evaluates curvature at its midpoint and advances displacement and slope with that constant curvature. It writes only printed results. Nonfinite inputs to the inverse calculation, nonpositive distance or speed, and invalid step counts raise ValueError. The supplied coefficient function is assumed to return finite values over the interval.

import math

MASS, DIAMETER = 0.156, 0.071  # kg, m; chosen case geometry
RHO, NU = 1.20, 1.562e-5      # kg/m^3 (chosen), m^2/s (25 C source)
LENGTH, SPEED, TARGET = 18.0, 30.0, 0.30  # m, m/s, m
AREA = math.pi * DIAMETER**2 / 4


def infer(length, speed, displacement):
    if not all(math.isfinite(v) for v in (length, speed, displacement)):
        raise ValueError("inputs must be finite")
    if length <= 0 or speed <= 0:
        raise ValueError("length and speed must be positive")
    time = length / speed
    force = 2 * MASS * displacement / time**2
    return time, force, force / (0.5 * RHO * speed**2 * AREA)


def integrate(coefficient, n):
    # Constant downstream speed; midpoint integration in x, not a flow solver.
    if not isinstance(n, int) or n <= 0:
        raise ValueError("n must be a positive integer")
    dx = LENGTH / n
    y, slope = 0.0, 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        x = (i + 0.5) * dx
        curvature = RHO * AREA * coefficient(x) / (2 * MASS)
        y += slope * dx + 0.5 * curvature * dx**2
        slope += curvature * dx
    return y, slope


flight, force, cy = infer(LENGTH, SPEED, TARGET)
exact = RHO * AREA * cy * LENGTH**2 / (4 * MASS)
y, slope = integrate(lambda x: cy, 400)
assert math.isclose(y, TARGET, rel_tol=1e-11)
assert math.isclose(slope, 2 * TARGET / LENGTH, rel_tol=1e-11)
assert math.isclose(exact, TARGET, rel_tol=1e-11)
assert integrate(lambda x: 0.0, 100) == (0.0, 0.0)
assert math.isclose(integrate(lambda x: -cy, 400)[0], -TARGET)
print(f"Re_D={SPEED*DIAMETER/NU:.0f}; flight={flight:.3f} s")
print(f"required force={force:.4f} N; C_Y={cy:.4f}")
print(f"constant C_Y: y={y:.4f} m; final slope={slope:.5f}")
for factor in (0.8, 1.0, 1.2):
    result = integrate(lambda x: factor*cy, 400)[0]
    print(f"coefficient factor={factor:.1f}: y={result:.4f} m")
for n in (100, 200, 400):
    value = integrate(lambda x: cy*math.cos(math.pi*x/LENGTH), n)[0]
    print(f"changing force n={n}: y={value:.8f} m")
print("zero, sign and analytic checks passed")

changing force n=200: y=0.12158417 m
changing force n=400: y=0.12158511 m
zero, sign and analytic checks passed

The constant-force and zero-force tests compare with independent mechanical limits. Reversing the coefficient must reverse displacement. The final three displacement values approach \(0.30(4/\pi^2)=0.12158542\ \mathrm m\), with about fourfold error reduction when step size halves, consistent with midpoint accuracy. The coefficient sensitivity is linear: a ±20 % coefficient error changes 0.30 m into 0.24 or 0.36 m. This measures dependence on an uncertain force input, not numerical uncertainty.

Two deliberate mutations were actually tested: removing the factor one-half from the inferred force coefficient fails the displacement assertion against 0.30 m; reversing the curvature sign fails the same assertion and yields the wrong direction. Input probes for zero speed, a nonfinite displacement, and zero steps raise ValueError. The tests detect these faults in the constructed model; they do not establish that any coefficient matches a real ball.

What the force model establishes and what it misses

The running case needs about 0.260 N of sustained lateral force. That force corresponds to a coefficient of 0.122 for the assumed air and geometry, and to a two-hemisphere pressure difference of roughly 66 Pa in the simplest pressure model. These checks connect an observable deflection to a plausible aerodynamic scale. They do not recover the actual pressure distribution: many different distributions have the same integral.

The mechanism requires a chain of evidence: seam and surface condition modify local boundary layers; separation, transition, and reattachment change the three-dimensional pressure distribution; the surface integral gives force; Newton's law accumulates its history into motion. Initial conventional swing can involve delayed laminar separation. Later conventional swing and reverse swing may involve bubbles and turbulent regions, with different sides providing stronger suction. Neither “both sides turbulent” nor “new ball” fixes the force sign.

The continuum, incompressible, constant-property description is assumed throughout the flow interpretation. The trajectory calculations further assume a small lateral angle, constant downstream speed, zero initial lateral velocity, and a prescribed mean force, without wind, gravity coupling, bounce, aerodynamic torque, or unsteady force fluctuations. Comparison with tracking data would have to estimate those omitted effects and measure the ball state. Completing the algebra does not substitute for that comparison.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive a force integral). In the two-hemisphere model, derive the projected area \(A\) directly by integrating \(n_y\) over \(y>0\). Find \(F_y\) when \(p_N-p_S=50\ \mathrm{Pa}\) for the 71 mm ball. Explain why a uniform added atmospheric pressure has no effect.

Solution

Use polar angle \(\alpha\) measured from \(+y\), radius \(R=D/2\), and azimuth \(\beta\). Then \(n_y=\cos\alpha\) and \(dA_b=R^2\sin\alpha\,d\alpha\,d\beta\). Over the hemisphere, \(\alpha\) runs from 0 to \(\pi/2\) and \(\beta\) from 0 to \(2\pi\). The integral is \(2\pi R^2\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\alpha\sin\alpha\,d\alpha=\pi R^2=A\). Reflection gives \(-A\) on N, so \(F_y=-p_SA+p_NA=50(0.003959)=0.1980\ \mathrm N\), toward S. Adding equal pressure to both sides cancels, equivalently because the closed-surface integral of the normal is zero. This result neglects viscous lateral traction and imposes piecewise-constant pressures.

Exercise 2 (compute and test an assumption). Repeat the inverse calculation for the same 0.30 m deflection over 18 m at 40 m/s. Compute flight time, force, coefficient, and final slope. Does an identical coefficient prove the same seam orientation will work?

Solution

The time is \(18/40=0.450\ \mathrm s\). Twice integrating the constant force gives \(F_y=2(0.156)(0.30)/(0.450)^2=0.4622\ \mathrm N\). Dynamic pressure is \(1.20(40)^2/2=960\ \mathrm{Pa}\), so \(C_Y=0.4622/(960A)=0.1216\), unchanged. Final lateral velocity is \(2Y/t_f=1.333\ \mathrm{m/s}\), giving slope 0.03333, also unchanged. But Reynolds number rises to \(40(0.071)/(1.562\times10^{-5})=181{,}818\). The force coefficient can change with Reynolds number and can cross zero for a particular ball. The required coefficient is unchanged; its aerodynamic availability has not been established.

Exercise 3 (counterexample to a regime rule). A student concludes that observing conventional swing proves that S is turbulent and N is laminar. Give a source-grounded counterexample and state the weaker conclusion that an observed trajectory can support.

Solution

The 30° five-trip calculation of Parekh and colleagues (2024), A23-9 and A23-12–13, produces initial conventional swing at \(7.5\times10^4\) and \(10^5\) through delayed laminar separation on S. The boundary layers have not undergone the claimed seam-side turbulent transition. The statement is therefore false. After excluding wind and initial lateral velocity, a trajectory can constrain a weighted force-history integral such as (17.7); even that requires a flight model. It cannot identify the surface flow state uniquely. Pressure and near-surface flow measurements would distinguish the mechanisms.

Exercise 4 (derive the effect of timing). Keep constant \(U\). Apply \(+F_0\) during the first half of flight and \(-F_0\) during the second. Derive final lateral velocity and displacement. Use \(F_0=0.260\ \mathrm N\), \(m=0.156\ \mathrm{kg}\), \(t_f=0.600\ \mathrm s\). Does the ball reverse lateral direction?

Solution

The first interval produces velocity \(F_0t_f/(2m)\). The second subtracts exactly that velocity, so \(V_y(t_f)=0\). Displacement is the first triangle under the velocity curve plus the second triangle, each with base \(t_f/2\) and height \(F_0t_f/(2m)\). Their sum is \(F_0t_f^2/(4m)=0.150\ \mathrm m\), half the constant-positive-force displacement. Throughout the interior \(V_y\) is positive. The force changes sign but the ball does not reverse lateral direction. A larger negative impulse or additional time would be needed.

Exercise 5 (sensitivity and inference). Suppose displacement is 0.30 m, but release has an unrecognized lateral velocity of 0.20 m/s. Under the constant-force model, what coefficient is actually needed? Separately derive the first-order relative sensitivity of inferred coefficient to errors in \(m,Y,\rho,D,L\).

Solution

Initial lateral velocity contributes \(0.20(0.600)=0.120\ \mathrm m\). Force must supply only 0.180 m, requiring 60 % of the previous force and coefficient: \(F_y=0.1560\ \mathrm N\), \(C_Y=0.0730\). Failing to measure initial velocity would overestimate the coefficient by \(0.30/0.18=1.667\). For zero initial lateral velocity, substitute \(A=\pi D^2/4\) into (17.6) to get \(C_Y=16mY/(\rho\pi D^2L^2)\). Logarithmic differentiation gives \(dC_Y/C_Y=dm/m+dY/Y-d\rho/\rho-2\,dD/D-2\,dL/L\). Each term is a signed small error, not an independent standard deviation. A 1 % distance overestimate alone causes approximately a 2 % coefficient underestimate. The relation omits the potentially larger model error from unknown initial velocity.

Exercise 6 (design an experiment). Design a test of whether a new ball reverses swing, and whether initial conventional swing requires turbulence on S. Identify controlled variables, independent observables, and a result that would refute a speed-only explanation.

Solution

Mount a characterized new ball at a fixed seam angle with defined S/N sides and a documented support. Measure diameter, seam profile, roughness pattern, air temperature, pressure, relative speed, and incoming turbulence. Sweep Reynolds number, repeating conditions and accounting for support force. A calibrated force balance must show a reproducible change in signed mean side force; a trajectory picture alone is insufficient. Pressure samples on both sides test the surface integral, while near-wall velocities, oil flow, or suitable transition diagnostics test separation, reattachment, and turbulent fluctuations. Positive side force accompanied by laminar S-side separation delayed relative to N refutes the claim that conventional swing always requires S-side turbulence. To test speed alone, hold speed and atmosphere fixed and change only seam angle or documented roughness distribution. A reproducible force-sign change then refutes a rule based solely on speed. Report uncertainty and specimen variability; an inconclusive near-zero force is not proof of absence of swing.
