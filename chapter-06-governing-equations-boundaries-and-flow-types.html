Governing equations in coordinates, boundary conditions and kinds of flow

A cylindrical vessel of radius 0.10 m contains 1.571 L of liquid, initially 50 mm deep. The vessel is brought to a rotation rate of 10 rad/s. If the liquid eventually rotates with it, its speed at the wall is 1.0 m/s and its acceleration there is 10 m/s², even though the velocity field is steady. The pressure rises outward by 500 Pa at any fixed height. With surface tension neglected, the free surface rises from 24.52 mm at the centre to 75.48 mm at the wall. A calculation that treats cylindrical velocity components as independent scalars misses the acceleration and predicts a flat surface. A calculation that prescribes the surface pressure but does not move the interface has a different defect: it supplies a force condition without a condition for where the liquid goes.

Grounded in Lecture 11 and Lecture 12 of NPTEL Fluid Mechanics, Prof. Sanjay Mittal, IIT Kanpur. Lecture material: assumptions and closure of the incompressible flow equations; inertia, pressure, viscosity and body force; nonlinearity, derivative order and mechanical pressure; Cartesian and cylindrical forms, including gradient, divergence, curl and Laplacian; Euler flow; far-field and solid-wall conditions; two-fluid velocity and traction conditions, their flat-interface reduction and the free-surface limit; initial velocity; steady, uniform, one-, two- and three-dimensional, fully developed, spatially periodic and temporally periodic flow. Research extensions: Thompson and Troian's molecular simulations distinguish a Newtonian bulk fluid from its slip boundary condition; Brackbill, Kothe and Zemach's surface-tension formulation supplies the normal and tangential stress conditions beyond a tension-free interface. Original teaching constructions: the rotating-vessel case, all numerical material parameters, the closure count with transported density, pressure constraint, cylindrical derivations and checks, the two-layer shear calculation, Figures 6.1–6.3, the executable checks and six exercises. A rotating vessel as an initial and boundary value problem

The liquid is a constructed Newtonian material of density \(\rho=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and dynamic viscosity \(\mu=0.10\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\). These are inputs for a teaching example, not measured properties of a named liquid. Gravity is \(\mathbf g=-g\mathbf k\), with \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) and \(\mathbf k\) the upward unit vector. The cylindrical vessel has radius \(R=0.10\ \mathrm m\), a flat bottom at height \(z=0\), and walls high enough to prevent overflow. Its initial liquid depth, also its mean depth throughout, is \(H_m=0.050\ \mathrm m\). The final angular velocity is \(\Omega=10\ \mathrm{rad/s}\). A radian is dimensionless in the equations. The gas above the liquid supplies uniform pressure \(p_{\mathrm{atm}}\); gas shear and surface tension are neglected in the principal calculation.

The formal model consists of a liquid region \(\mathcal D(t)\), a velocity field \(\mathbf V(\mathbf x,t)\) in m/s, a pressure field \(p(\mathbf x,t)\) in Pa, and boundary data on the solid walls and the liquid–gas interface. Here \(\mathbf x=(x,y,z)\) is position in metres and \(t\) is time in seconds. The upper boundary is unknown: write it as \(z=\eta(x,y,t)\), where \(\eta\) is a height, not a prescribed wall. The fluid properties and the wall motion complete the model inputs. The outputs are \(\mathbf V,p,\eta\). A final steady state is only one part of this problem; predicting startup requires their time evolution.

The continuum hypothesis assigns these smooth fields to regions large compared with molecular scales. For a gas the relevant comparison is its molecular mean free path \(\lambda\) against the smallest resolved flow length \(\ell\), expressed by the Knudsen number \(\mathrm{Kn}=\lambda/\ell\). Small \(\mathrm{Kn}\) is needed for a conventional continuum description; a liquid also requires spatial averaging over many molecules. Neither condition alone proves the no-slip boundary condition. The example assumes a macroscopic continuum and a fixed temperature, so heating does not change \(\rho\) or \(\mu\). The viscosity divided by density is the kinematic viscosity, \(\nu=\mu/\rho=10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\).

Vectors and tensors are written in bold; the board uses an under-tilde. The three Cartesian components of velocity are \(V_x,V_y,V_z\). The total stress tensor is \(\mathbf T\); its component \(T_{ij}\) is force in direction \(j\) on a face whose normal is direction \(i\), the face-first convention. Thus the traction, force per unit area on a plane of unit normal \(\mathbf n\), is \(\mathbf t=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf T\), or \(t_j=\sum_i n_iT_{ij}\). Ordinary fluids without body couples have symmetric stress. The needed stress law is restated below.

The balance laws and what closes them L11 0:19

Conservation of mass for a moving fluid element gives \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt+\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0\). The material derivative \(\mathrm D/\mathrm Dt\) measures the rate seen by that element, rather than by an observer fixed at a point. For a scalar \(a\), the chain rule along the element's trajectory gives \(\mathrm Da/\mathrm Dt=\partial a/\partial t+\mathbf V\cdot\nabla a\). An incompressible motion preserves each element's volume. Its density therefore stays fixed along its trajectory, \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\), and mass conservation reduces to

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0. \tag{6.1} \]

Incompressible does not mean that all elements have the same density. A stratified liquid can have \(\rho=\rho(z)\) initially and transport these different values without compressing any element. The running case makes the additional assumption of one uniform, constant density. Without it, density is another unknown: the transport equation \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\) and the initial density field are required as well.

Newton's second law for a material element balances its acceleration against body and surface forces. A body force per unit mass is \(\mathbf f\), in m/s²; gravity is the example \(\mathbf f=\mathbf g\). With the face-first stress convention, the local balance is \(\rho\,\mathrm DV_j/\mathrm Dt=\rho f_j+\sum_i\partial T_{ij}/\partial x_i\), for \(j=x,y,z\). This follows by subtracting the tractions on opposite faces of a small box and dividing by its volume. The scalar pressure contributes \(-\partial p/\partial x_j\). Closing the balance requires a law for the remaining stress.

The incompressible Newtonian constitutive model is

\[ T_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+\tau_{ij},\qquad \tau_{ij}=\mu\left(\frac{\partial V_j}{\partial x_i} +\frac{\partial V_i}{\partial x_j}\right). \tag{6.2} \]

Here \(\delta_{ij}\) is 1 for equal indices and 0 otherwise; \(\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf T+p\mathbf I\) is the viscous part of the stress, and \(\mathbf I\) is the identity tensor. Pressure is positive in compression. The law states proportionality of viscous stress to rate of deformation, with \(\mu\) as the proportionality coefficient; it is a material model, not a consequence of mass or momentum conservation. It excludes elastic memory and dependence of viscosity on the local rate of shear. Temperature dependence may be included in a larger model, but is excluded here.

For constant \(\mu\), differentiate (6.2) and sum over \(i\). The first velocity term gives \(\mu\sum_i\partial_i^2 V_j\). Interchanging smooth mixed derivatives makes the second \(\mu\partial_j(\sum_i\partial_iV_i)\), which vanishes by (6.1). Consequently the flow equations are

\[ \rho\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf V}{\partial t} +(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V\right]+\nabla p =\mu\nabla^2\mathbf V+\rho\mathbf f. \tag{6.3} \]

The Laplacian \(\nabla^2\) is the sum of second spatial derivatives when applied to a scalar in Cartesian coordinates; its vector meaning is developed below. Every term in (6.3) has units \(\mathrm{N/m^3}\). For example, \(\rho\,\mathrm DV_j/\mathrm Dt\) has units \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}s^{-2}}\), as do \(\partial_jp\) and \(\mu\partial_i^2V_j\). If viscosity varies, differentiating the product in (6.2) creates terms involving its gradient. Replacing them by \(\mu\nabla^2\mathbf V\) would silently change the model.

For prescribed \(\rho,\mu,\mathbf f\), there are four unknown scalar fields, \(V_x,V_y,V_z,p\), and four scalar equations: continuity and three momentum components. Before the constitutive law, even a symmetric total stress contributes six independent unknown fields; conservation alone does not determine them. With nonuniform incompressible density there are five unknown fields and five equations after adding density transport. With temperature-dependent properties or compressible motion, energy, constitutive data and an equation of state are needed. Counting equations is a closure check. It establishes neither compatible boundary data nor existence, uniqueness or stability of a solution.

Acceleration, nonlinearity and the role of pressure L11 4:39

The left bracket in (6.3) is an acceleration. The local part \(\partial_t\mathbf V\) measures change at a fixed position; the convective part \((\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V\) measures change because an element moves between positions. In the final rotating vessel the local part is zero, while the convective part supplies the inward acceleration of each circular trajectory. A steady field does not imply unaccelerated particles.

The convective term is nonlinear in the velocity. For two fields \(\mathbf U\) and \(\mathbf W\), expanding it for their sum yields \((\mathbf U+\mathbf W)\cdot\nabla(\mathbf U+\mathbf W)=(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U+(\mathbf W\cdot\nabla)\mathbf W+(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf W+(\mathbf W\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\). The last two terms generally do not vanish. For example, take \(\mathbf U=(a y,0,0)\) and \(\mathbf W=(0,b x,0)\), with rates \(a,b\) in \(\mathrm s^{-1}\). Each is divergence-free and each has zero convective acceleration alone. Their sum has acceleration \((abx,aby,0)\). With \(a=2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},b=3\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), at \(x=0.10\ \mathrm m,y=0.20\ \mathrm m\) the acceleration is \((0.60,1.20,0)\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). Superposing two velocity solutions need not superpose their required pressure fields or forces.

Equation (6.3) balances inertia, pressure force, viscous force and body force. Inertia is the mass-times-acceleration side of Newton's law, rather than an extra physical force in an inertial frame. These contributions can have very different magnitudes. For speed scale \(U\) and variation length \(L\), convective inertia scales as \(\rho U^2/L\), while viscosity scales as \(\mu U/L^2\). Their ratio is the Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_L=\rho UL/\mu\). A small value supports neglecting convection only if those scales represent the actual gradients. Unsteady inertia has the separate scale \(\rho U/t_c\), where \(t_c\) is a time scale. A rapid startup can retain inertia even when steady convection is small.

Pressure enters through a first spatial derivative. Therefore replacing \(p(\mathbf x,t)\) by \(p(\mathbf x,t)+C(t)\), with \(C\) independent of position, changes no interior equation. This freedom does not allow changing a prescribed external pressure or a traction boundary condition independently. With velocity prescribed on an enclosed boundary, a pressure reference fixes the freedom; at a free surface the gas pressure supplies a physical reference. Pressure remains a measurable normal-stress contribution. Saying that only its gradient enters the interior motion does not make absolute pressure irrelevant to cavitation or to a thermodynamic model.

For the constant-density incompressible model, pressure enforces the volume constraint rather than being computed from an ideal-gas law. This can be seen by taking the divergence of (6.3). Smoothness and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0\) remove both the unsteady divergence and the viscous divergence. Applying the product rule to the convective term leaves

\[ \nabla^2p=-\rho\sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial V_j}{\partial x_i} \frac{\partial V_i}{\partial x_j} +\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf f. \tag{6.4} \]

The second-derivative contribution \(V_i\partial_i(\partial_jV_j)\) is zero, which is the crucial cancellation. Equation (6.4), with boundary information from momentum and any specified traction, determines pressure at each time. For rigid rotation, \(V_x=-\Omega y,V_y=\Omega x,V_z=0\); the sum in (6.4) is \(-2\Omega^2\), so \(\nabla^2p=2\rho\Omega^2\). The pressure \(p=p_c+\rho\Omega^2(x^2+y^2)/2-\rho gz\), with constant \(p_c\), passes this check. Equation (6.4) alone is not a replacement for all four flow equations or their boundary conditions.

Cartesian components and the inviscid reduction L11 14:38

In fixed Cartesian axes the unit vectors \(\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\) do not change with position. A vector derivative can therefore be taken component by component. Writing all four equations makes their coupling explicit:

\[ \frac{\partial V_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial V_y}{\partial y} +\frac{\partial V_z}{\partial z}=0, \tag{6.5} \] \[ \rho\left(\partial_tV_x+V_x\partial_xV_x+V_y\partial_yV_x+V_z\partial_zV_x\right)+\partial_xp =\mu(\partial_x^2V_x+\partial_y^2V_x+\partial_z^2V_x)+\rho f_x, \tag{6.6} \] \[ \rho\left(\partial_tV_y+V_x\partial_xV_y+V_y\partial_yV_y+V_z\partial_zV_y\right)+\partial_yp =\mu(\partial_x^2V_y+\partial_y^2V_y+\partial_z^2V_y)+\rho f_y, \tag{6.7} \] \[ \rho\left(\partial_tV_z+V_x\partial_xV_z+V_y\partial_yV_z+V_z\partial_zV_z\right)+\partial_zp =\mu(\partial_x^2V_z+\partial_y^2V_z+\partial_z^2V_z)+\rho f_z. \tag{6.8} \]

Here \(\partial_x\) means \(\partial/\partial x\), and similarly for the other coordinates and time. In each momentum equation the differentiated velocity is the component whose momentum is being balanced. The advecting velocities are still all three components. Replacing every occurrence of \(V_x\) by \(V_y\) in the entire \(x\)-equation would corrupt the advection operator as well as change the desired component.

An inviscid model sets the viscous stress to zero. For incompressible motion it retains (6.1) and gives the Euler equations,

\[ \rho\left[\partial_t\mathbf V+(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V\right] +\nabla p=\rho\mathbf f. \tag{6.9} \]

Convection is still nonlinear. The highest velocity derivative in space is now first order rather than second. This change is why viscous wall data cannot simply be carried over unchanged: no penetration remains, but an inviscid solid wall generally permits tangential slip. A first-order system is not automatically easy or well posed; characteristics, the incompressible pressure constraint, geometry and data compatibility remain.

The running vessel illustrates a singular boundary issue. Once established, rigid rotation has zero rate of deformation, so its viscous force is zero despite nonzero viscosity. It solves both the viscous and inviscid interior equations with the same pressure gradient. But the spin-up from rest depends on tangential stresses at the moving walls. An inviscid liquid initially at rest does not acquire the vessel's rotation through a no-penetration condition alone. Removing a force from the eventual interior balance does not remove its role in creating that state.

Cylindrical coordinates and scalar differential operators L11 22:57

For a circular vessel the coordinates \(r,\theta,z\) follow the geometry: \(x=r\cos\theta,y=r\sin\theta\), with \(r\ge0\) the distance from the axis and \(\theta\) the angle about it. At \(r>0\) the local unit vectors are \(\mathbf e_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)\), \(\mathbf e_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0)\) and \(\mathbf k\). Thus \(\mathbf V=V_r\mathbf e_r+V_\theta\mathbf e_\theta+V_z\mathbf k\). The components are physical speeds in mutually perpendicular directions, not rates of change of the coordinate labels: \(\dot r=V_r,\dot\theta=V_\theta/r,\dot z=V_z\) along a trajectory. In particular, \(V_\theta\) is in m/s while \(\dot\theta\) is in \(\mathrm s^{-1}\).

A small displacement has components \(\mathrm dr,r\,\mathrm d\theta,\mathrm dz\). If \(a(r,\theta,z,t)\) is a smooth scalar, \(\mathrm da=(\partial_ra)\mathrm dr+(\partial_\theta a)\mathrm d\theta+(\partial_za)\mathrm dz=\nabla a\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf x\). Matching the physical displacement components gives

\[ \nabla a=\mathbf e_r\partial_ra+\mathbf e_\theta\frac1r\partial_\theta a+\mathbf k\partial_za,\qquad \frac{\mathrm Da}{\mathrm Dt}=\partial_ta+V_r\partial_ra+\frac{V_\theta}{r}\partial_\theta a+V_z\partial_za. \tag{6.10} \]

The factor \(1/r\) converts an angular derivative to a derivative per metre of arc. This is dimensional as well as geometric: omitting it would give the angular pressure-force term units of pressure instead of pressure per length.

The divergence measures outward volume flux per unit volume. Consider a narrow wedge with dimensions \(\mathrm dr,\mathrm d\theta,\mathrm dz\). Its volume is \(r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm dz\) to first order. The outer radial face has area \((r+\mathrm dr)\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm dz\), while the inner face has \(r\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm dz\). Their difference of fluxes is \(\partial_r(rV_r)\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm dz\). The angular faces have area \(\mathrm dr\,\mathrm dz\); subtracting their fluxes gives \(\partial_\theta V_\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm dz\). The top and bottom contribute \(r\partial_zV_z\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm dz\). Division by volume yields

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf V=\frac1r\partial_r(rV_r)+\frac1r\partial_\theta V_\theta+\partial_zV_z,\qquad \nabla^2a=\frac1r\partial_r(r\partial_ra)+\frac1{r^2}\partial_\theta^2a+\partial_z^2a. \tag{6.11} \]

The scalar Laplacian follows by applying the just-derived divergence to the gradient in (6.10). As a check, \(a=r^2=x^2+y^2\) gives \(\nabla^2a=4\) in either coordinate system. A purely radial speed \(V_r=c/r\), with constant \(c\) in \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\), has zero divergence away from the axis: cylindrical shells carry the same total flux. It is singular on the axis, so it cannot represent a smooth flow throughout a full vessel without a line source or sink.

The curl \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf V\), called the vorticity, measures local circulation per oriented area and has units \(\mathrm s^{-1}\). Its \(z\)-component follows by circulating around the wedge's \(r\)-\(\theta\) face: the outer-minus-inner circumferential contribution is \(\partial_r(rV_\theta)\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta\), and the radial-edge contribution is \(-\partial_\theta V_r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta\). Divide by area \(r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta\). Circulation in the other two coordinate planes gives

\[ \omega_r=\frac1r\partial_\theta V_z-\partial_zV_\theta,\quad \omega_\theta=\partial_zV_r-\partial_rV_z,\quad \omega_z=\frac1r\left[\partial_r(rV_\theta)-\partial_\theta V_r\right]. \tag{6.12} \]

For \(V_\theta=\Omega r,V_r=V_z=0\), this gives \(\omega_z=2\Omega=20\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), consistent with a small marked element rotating at angular speed \(\Omega\). A correct determinant mnemonic is \(r^{-1}\det[\mathbf e_r,\ r\mathbf e_\theta,\ \mathbf k;\ \partial_r,\ \partial_\theta,\ \partial_z;\ V_r,\ rV_\theta,\ V_z]\), with derivatives acting on the entries below them. The factors belong inside the differentiations. A Cartesian-looking determinant that omits the factor \(r\) multiplying \(V_\theta\) loses the term \(V_\theta/r\) in \(\omega_z\). The component expressions (6.12) avoid that ambiguity.

Turning basis vectors, material acceleration and viscous force L11 30:51

The scalar material derivative in (6.10) is not the whole derivative of a vector. Differentiating the unit-vector definitions gives \(\partial_\theta\mathbf e_r=\mathbf e_\theta\) and \(\partial_\theta\mathbf e_\theta=-\mathbf e_r\); neither depends on \(r,z,t\) at a fixed position. Along a moving element, \(\mathrm D\mathbf e_r/\mathrm Dt=(V_\theta/r)\mathbf e_\theta\) and \(\mathrm D\mathbf e_\theta/\mathrm Dt=-(V_\theta/r)\mathbf e_r\). Figure 6.1 exposes the direction change that the derivative must include.

Define the scalar advection operator \(\mathcal A=\partial_t+V_r\partial_r+(V_\theta/r)\partial_\theta+V_z\partial_z\). Apply the product rule to \(\mathbf V=V_r\mathbf e_r+V_\theta\mathbf e_\theta+V_z\mathbf k\), including those two basis derivatives. The acceleration components are

\[ a_r=\mathcal A V_r-\frac{V_\theta^2}{r},\qquad a_\theta=\mathcal A V_\theta+\frac{V_rV_\theta}{r},\qquad a_z=\mathcal A V_z. \tag{6.13} \]

The inward term comes from differentiating \(V_\theta\mathbf e_\theta\); the extra tangential term comes from differentiating \(V_r\mathbf e_r\). These are geometric terms in an inertial frame, not evidence that cylindrical coordinates are a rotating observer. For pure rotation (6.13) gives \(a_r=-\Omega^2r\) and the other components zero. At the vessel wall this is \(-10\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). For steady, purely radial motion with \(V_\theta=0\), both added terms vanish, another check.

The viscous vector Laplacian has the same pitfall. Let \(\mathcal L\) denote the scalar Laplacian in (6.11). For the radial and tangential components of \(\mathbf V\), differentiating once with respect to \(\theta\) gives coefficients \(B_r=\partial_\theta V_r-V_\theta\) and \(B_\theta=\partial_\theta V_\theta+V_r\). Differentiating again gives radial coefficient \(\partial_\theta B_r-B_\theta=\partial_\theta^2V_r-2\partial_\theta V_\theta-V_r\) and tangential coefficient \(\partial_\theta B_\theta+B_r=\partial_\theta^2V_\theta+2\partial_\theta V_r-V_\theta\). The \(r\) and \(z\) differentiations do not turn the basis. Therefore

\[ (\nabla^2\mathbf V)_r=\mathcal L V_r-\frac{V_r}{r^2}-\frac2{r^2}\partial_\theta V_\theta,\quad (\nabla^2\mathbf V)_\theta=\mathcal L V_\theta-\frac{V_\theta}{r^2}+\frac2{r^2}\partial_\theta V_r,\quad (\nabla^2\mathbf V)_z=\mathcal L V_z. \tag{6.14} \]

The solid-rotation check is decisive: \(\mathcal L(\Omega r)=\Omega/r\), but subtracting \(V_\theta/r^2=\Omega/r\) gives zero vector Laplacian. In Cartesian coordinates the velocity is linear in \(x,y\), so its second derivatives are zero. Applying a scalar Laplacian separately to \(V_r,V_\theta,V_z\) would predict a spurious viscous force in undeforming rigid rotation.

Combining (6.10), (6.13) and (6.14), the incompressible equations at \(r>0\) are

\[ \frac1r\partial_r(rV_r)+\frac1r\partial_\theta V_\theta+\partial_zV_z=0, \tag{6.15} \] \[ \rho\left(\mathcal A V_r-\frac{V_\theta^2}{r}\right)+\partial_rp =\mu\left(\mathcal L V_r-\frac{V_r}{r^2}-\frac2{r^2}\partial_\theta V_\theta\right)+\rho f_r, \tag{6.16} \] \[ \rho\left(\mathcal A V_\theta+\frac{V_rV_\theta}{r}\right)+\frac1r\partial_\theta p =\mu\left(\mathcal L V_\theta-\frac{V_\theta}{r^2}+\frac2{r^2}\partial_\theta V_r\right)+\rho f_\theta, \tag{6.17} \] \[ \rho\mathcal A V_z+\partial_zp=\mu\mathcal L V_z+\rho f_z. \tag{6.18} \]

The board writes the radial viscous operator as \(\partial_r[r^{-1}\partial_r(rV_r)]\). Expanding this gives \(\partial_r^2V_r+r^{-1}\partial_rV_r-V_r/r^2\), exactly the radial part of (6.14); the same equivalence holds for \(V_\theta\). These are two arrangements of one operator. At the axis the basis is undefined, although a physical velocity field may be perfectly smooth. Equations containing \(1/r\) must be interpreted through a regular Cartesian field or a suitable limit. In rigid rotation \(V_\theta=\Omega r\) vanishes at the axis and all physical quantities remain finite.

For the eventual rotating state, (6.15) holds, the tangential equation gives zero on both sides, and the radial and vertical equations reduce to \(\partial_rp=\rho\Omega^2r\) and \(\partial_zp=-\rho g\). Integration gives \(p=p_c+\rho\Omega^2r^2/2-\rho gz\). At fixed height the wall pressure exceeds the axis pressure by \(\rho\Omega^2R^2/2=500\ \mathrm{Pa}\). Its outward gradient produces the inward pressure force \(-\nabla p\). A sign error here would require pressure to pull outward while a particle accelerates inward.

Far-field data and the two parts of a wall condition L11 38:04

External flow occupies a region around a body, such as an aircraft in the atmosphere. Far from a localized disturbance one commonly imposes \(\mathbf V\to\mathbf V_\infty\) and \(p\to p_\infty\), with \(\mathbf V_\infty,p_\infty\) the undisturbed velocity and pressure in the chosen frame. In a ground frame with still ambient air, \(\mathbf V_\infty=\mathbf0\). In a frame translating with an aircraft whose ground-frame velocity is \(\mathbf U_b\), the far velocity is \(-\mathbf U_b\), while the aircraft is fixed. A constant translational change of frame changes every velocity consistently; it does not turn the ambient fluid into a moving wall.

A decaying disturbance is an assumption appropriate to that external-flow problem. Finite energy is useful motivation but does not by itself prove a pointwise far-field limit for every mathematically admissible velocity field. Wakes may extend far downstream, and a finite computational boundary must be placed and tested rather than declared to be infinity. The rotating vessel is an internal free-surface problem: its solid walls bound the liquid, so no liquid far-field velocity belongs among its data. Adding one would describe a different geometry.

At an impermeable solid surface with velocity \(\mathbf V_s(\mathbf x,t)\) and unit normal \(\mathbf n\), fluid cannot cross the surface. The relative normal velocity must vanish:

\[ (\mathbf V-\mathbf V_s)\cdot\mathbf n=0. \tag{6.19} \]

This is no penetration, a geometric and mass-conservation condition for an impermeable boundary. It applies to viscous and inviscid models. It does not apply unchanged to porous walls with suction or blowing: then the relative flux is specified separately. Motion normal to a wall need not be zero; it must match the wall's normal motion.

No slip specifies the tangential part as well. For either of the two independent unit tangents \(\mathbf s\) in three dimensions, \((\mathbf V-\mathbf V_s)\cdot\mathbf s=0\). Together with (6.19) this is

\[ \mathbf V=\mathbf V_s\quad\text{on a no-slip, impermeable wall}. \tag{6.20} \]

No slip is an empirical continuum boundary model, separate from the Newtonian bulk constitutive law. Nonzero viscosity alone does not prove it. At molecular scales liquid–solid interaction can permit slip; rarefied gases can require velocity-slip and temperature-jump conditions even while a continuum bulk description remains useful. This chapter solves no energy equation and imposes no temperature boundary condition. If thermal transport were added, no temperature jump would be an additional interface model.

Thompson and Troian (1997), pp. 360–361, simulate shear between atomistic walls. Their bulk liquid remains Newtonian while slip depends on wall interaction and shear rate. They define the linear slip length by velocity difference divided by shear rate. This evidence separates a boundary law from a bulk law; it is not a slip measurement for the constructed vessel. A flat-wall Navier model writes \(V_{\mathrm{slip}}=b_s\,\partial_nV_t\), with \(b_s\) a slip length, \(V_t\) tangential velocity and \(n\) distance into the liquid. No slip is the limit \(b_s=0\).

For the vessel's cylindrical side, the final wall velocity is \(\mathbf V_s=\Omega R\mathbf e_\theta\). No penetration alone fixes \(V_r(R)=0\). No slip also fixes \(V_\theta(R)=\Omega R\) and \(V_z(R)=0\). On the bottom, every point moves at \(\Omega r\mathbf e_\theta\); using a stationary-bottom condition would be incompatible with the rigid-rotation state. In an Euler model the moving side and bottom have zero normal velocity, so (6.19) communicates no angular speed to the fluid. The choice of tangential condition has a physical consequence, even when the final viscous term is zero.

An interface needs a motion condition and a force condition L12 8:56

Consider two immiscible fluids, numbered 1 and 2, with properties \(\rho_\alpha,\mu_\alpha\), velocities \(\mathbf V_\alpha\) and pressures \(p_\alpha\), where \(\alpha=1,2\). Immiscible means that they remain distinguishable phases separated by an interface. Assume no evaporation, condensation or other mass transfer. Choose one unit normal \(\mathbf n\) pointing from fluid 1 into fluid 2, and let \(\mathbf V_I\) denote an interface velocity. Only its normal component is needed to locate the interface. Both fluids must share that normal motion:

\[ (\mathbf V_1-\mathbf V_I)\cdot\mathbf n =(\mathbf V_2-\mathbf V_I)\cdot\mathbf n=0. \tag{6.21} \]

Equality of the two normal fluid velocities is necessary, but an evolving unknown interface must also be advanced with that common velocity. To derive its evolution, describe the interface as the zero set \(F(\mathbf x,t)=0\), with \(\nabla F\ne0\). A particle on a material interface stays on it, so the chain rule gives \(\partial_tF+\mathbf V_\alpha\cdot\nabla F=0\). For the graph \(F=z-\eta(x,y,t)\),

\[ \partial_t\eta+V_{\alpha x}\partial_x\eta+V_{\alpha y}\partial_y\eta=V_{\alpha z} \quad\text{at }z=\eta. \tag{6.22} \]

This is the kinematic condition: it specifies interface motion. A horizontal interface can rise or fall, in which case its normal fluid velocity is not zero. Zero normal speed follows from a stationary flat interface with no phase change, not from horizontal orientation alone. A viscosity-based no-interfacial-slip model further imposes equality of the two tangential velocities and hence \(\mathbf V_1=\mathbf V_2\) at the interface. This is an additional interface model; inviscid fluids can have a jump in tangential velocity while retaining (6.21).

The dynamic condition is a force balance. First neglect surface tension and any interface mass. Take a pillbox whose thickness tends to zero across the interface. Its inertia and body force vanish with thickness, whereas its broad-face tractions stay proportional to area. With the same normal on both sides this yields

\[ \mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf T_2-\mathbf T_1)=\mathbf0. \tag{6.23} \]

Equivalently, tractions computed with the two outward normals \(\mathbf n\) and \(-\mathbf n\) are opposite. Using outward normals on both sides but demanding equal rather than opposite vectors would reverse one traction incorrectly. The pillbox does not assume that either bulk fluid is at rest: it assumes bounded bulk acceleration and no separate surface inertia. The board denotes the total stress by a tau symbol; here it is \(\mathbf T\), while \(\boldsymbol\tau\) is only its viscous part.

Surface tension prevents the rim force of the pillbox from vanishing. Let \(\sigma\) be the isotropic surface tension in N/m, \(\kappa=\nabla_s\cdot\mathbf n\) the signed curvature, and \(\nabla_s=(\mathbf I-\mathbf n\mathbf n)\cdot\nabla\) the surface gradient. With the chosen normal, a sphere containing fluid 1 has \(\kappa=2/a>0\), where \(a\) is its radius. The force per area from a tensioned interface is \(\nabla_s\sigma-\sigma\kappa\mathbf n\). Balancing this with the two bulk tractions gives

\[ \mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf T_2-\mathbf T_1) =\sigma\kappa\mathbf n-\nabla_s\sigma. \tag{6.24} \]

To see the normal term for a sphere directly, cut it into hemispheres. Excess interior pressure acts over the great-circle area \(\pi a^2\), and tension pulls around the circumference \(2\pi a\). Force balance gives \((p_1-p_2)\pi a^2=\sigma\,2\pi a\), so \(p_1-p_2=2\sigma/a=\sigma\kappa\), consistent with (6.24). Variable \(\sigma\) also creates a tangential force: a small surface strip is pulled harder at its high-tension end. Thus a flat interface can have a tangential stress jump even when its curvature is zero.

Brackbill, Kothe and Zemach (1992), p. 336, Eqs. (5)–(9), give the normal and tangential projections, including viscous normal stress and a surface-tension gradient. Their curvature orientation differs; (6.24) uses the sphere convention just checked. Their computational extension spreads the interface force over a thin region and recovers the sharp-interface force as thickness tends to zero (pp. 337–338, Eqs. (13), (29)–(30)). A resolved transition layer in a numerical model and a material interface in (6.22) are distinct modelling choices.

The dynamic condition assumes an isotropic tension and no independent membrane elasticity, surface viscosity, electrical stress or interface inertia. Those effects require added terms. The kinematic condition also changes with phase change. Equations (6.21)–(6.24) must therefore travel with their hypotheses rather than be treated as universal rules for every boundary between fluids.

The flat-interface reduction and a two-layer check L12 18:52

Place a stationary flat interface at \(y=0\), fluid 1 above and fluid 2 below. In this section the vertical coordinate is \(y\) and the tangent is \(x\); this is a local Cartesian calculation, distinct from the vessel's \(z\)-axis. Take \(\mathbf n=\mathbf j\), from lower fluid 2 into upper fluid 1, and use the same normal to compare the two tractions. With no surface tension, (6.23) is simply \(T_{1,yx}=T_{2,yx}\) and \(T_{1,yy}=T_{2,yy}\). Symmetry of the stress permits either matrix multiplication in this Newtonian calculation, but the face-first labels fix which traction component is being used.

\[ \mu_1(\partial_yV_{1x}+\partial_xV_{1y}) =\mu_2(\partial_yV_{2x}+\partial_xV_{2y}),\qquad -p_1+2\mu_1\partial_yV_{1y} =-p_2+2\mu_2\partial_yV_{2y}. \tag{6.25} \]

All quantities in (6.25) are limiting values at \(y=0\), taken within the corresponding fluid. The factor 2 multiplies the viscous normal strain on each side. Pressure need not be continuous when these normal viscous stresses differ. For example, if \(\mu_1=0.1\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\), \(\mu_2=0.02\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\) and the two normal derivatives are both \(5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), the normal-stress condition requires \(p_1-p_2=0.8\ \mathrm{Pa}\), even at zero surface tension. A local divergence-free extension has \(\partial_xV_{\alpha x}=-5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\); a zero normal velocity value at the interface does not force its normal derivative to vanish.

Now assume two-dimensional incompressible flow with all velocity components independent of \(x\) in each layer near the interface. Continuity gives \(\partial_yV_{\alpha y}=-\partial_xV_{\alpha x}=0\). Stationarity and no penetration give \(V_{\alpha y}(0)=0\), and hence the normal speed is zero throughout each connected layer. Also \(\partial_xV_{\alpha y}=0\). Continuity and the stationary-interface condition therefore reduce the traction conditions to

\[ \mu_1\frac{\mathrm dV_{1x}}{\mathrm dy} =\mu_2\frac{\mathrm dV_{2x}}{\mathrm dy},\qquad p_1=p_2 \quad\text{at }y=0. \tag{6.26} \]

Equality of shear stresses does not imply equality of velocity gradients. To compute a complete check, put a fixed lower plate at \(y=-H_2\) and a moving upper plate at \(y=H_1\), with \(H_2=1\ \mathrm{mm}\), \(H_1=2\ \mathrm{mm}\) and upper speed \(U=0.060\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Choose \(\mu_1=0.10\ \mathrm{Pa\,s},\mu_2=0.020\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\), and no axial pressure gradient or body force. Steady momentum reduces separately to \(\mu_\alpha\,\mathrm d^2V_{\alpha x}/\mathrm dy^2=0\). Each profile is linear. Let \(u_I\) be the common tangential interface speed and \(\tau_I\) the signed common \(+y\)-face \(x\)-traction.

Starting at the bottom, \(u_I=\tau_I H_2/\mu_2\). Crossing the upper layer adds \(\tau_I H_1/\mu_1\), so \(U=\tau_I(H_2/\mu_2+H_1/\mu_1)\). Therefore

\[ \tau_I=\frac{U}{H_2/\mu_2+H_1/\mu_1}=0.857143\ \mathrm{Pa},\qquad u_I=\frac{\tau_IH_2}{\mu_2}=0.042857\ \mathrm{m/s}. \tag{6.27} \]

The gradients are \(42.857\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) below and \(8.571\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) above. Their ratio is five, the inverse viscosity ratio. Both the velocity continuity and the wall values hold, and multiplying either slope by its own viscosity gives the same stress. Figure 6.2 is a plot of this calculation, not an experimentally measured profile. In the limit of identical viscosities the two line segments join with equal slope, reproducing a single fluid between the plates. With the same thicknesses and a fivefold viscosity contrast, demanding equal slopes would force equal shear only if the slope were zero, which cannot meet the moving-wall condition.

A free-surface approximation treats the outer fluid as a specified gas pressure with negligible shear. Small gas viscosity alone is insufficient: the product of gas viscosity and gas velocity gradient determines the applied shear, and wind can make it appreciable. For a stationary flat surface with developed parallel flow, zero gas shear gives \(\mu\,\mathrm dV_x/\mathrm dy=0\), while negligible viscous normal stress and curvature give \(p=p_{\mathrm{atm}}\). For general flow the conditions are projections of (6.24), not those two scalar formulas without qualifications. Even with constant nonzero surface tension, a flat interface has no curvature pressure jump; surface tension does not by itself require an interface to be curved.

The vessel surface from pressure and conservation of volume

The rotating vessel now has both pieces of its free-boundary model. In the proposed final state \(V_r=V_z=0,V_\theta=\Omega r\), a stationary axisymmetric surface satisfies the kinematic condition: fluid moves tangent to every circle on it, and no fluid crosses it. Rigid rotation has zero rate of deformation, so its stress is \(-p\mathbf I\), including on a sloping surface. With uniform gas pressure, negligible gas shear and no surface tension, the dynamic condition is \(p=p_{\mathrm{atm}}\) at \(z=\eta(r)\). Insert the pressure found from momentum:

\[ p_c+\frac12\rho\Omega^2r^2-\rho g\eta(r)=p_{\mathrm{atm}}, \qquad \eta(r)=\eta_c+\frac{\Omega^2r^2}{2g}, \tag{6.28} \]

where \(\eta_c=(p_c-p_{\mathrm{atm}})/(\rho g)\) is the central surface height. Differentiating gives \(g\,\eta'(r)=\Omega^2r\): the surface tilts until the pressure is constant along it. Radial pressure force supplies centripetal acceleration, while vertical pressure force balances gravity. Neither condition alone fixes \(\eta_c\).

The remaining constant follows from the initial volume, \(\forall=\pi R^2H_m\), where \(\forall\) denotes volume. Integrating the height over concentric annuli gives \(\forall=\int_0^R2\pi r\eta(r)\,\mathrm dr=\pi R^2\eta_c+\pi\Omega^2R^4/(4g)\). Thus

\[ \eta_c=H_m-\frac{\Omega^2R^2}{4g},\qquad \eta(R)=H_m+\frac{\Omega^2R^2}{4g}. \tag{6.29} \]

The vessel contains \(0.001570796\ \mathrm{m^3}=1.570796\ \mathrm L\). The depth correction is \(0.0254842\ \mathrm m\), yielding 24.516 mm at the centre and 75.484 mm at the wall, as plotted in Figure 6.3. The bottom pressure at the centre is \(p_{\mathrm{atm}}+\rho g\eta_c=p_{\mathrm{atm}}+240.5\ \mathrm{Pa}\), and at the rim it is \(p_{\mathrm{atm}}+740.5\ \mathrm{Pa}\), their difference 500 Pa. When \(\Omega\to0\), both heights tend to \(H_m\) and the pressure becomes hydrostatic. A 50 mm vessel rim would overflow; a design needs height above 75.5 mm plus a margin for startup waves. The model computes the steady minimum, not that wave margin.

Equations (6.28)–(6.29) require a wet bottom everywhere and no loss of liquid. The central depth must be positive, or \(\Omega^2R^2<4gH_m\). At the limit for the running vessel, \(\Omega=14.01\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), the centre first reaches the bottom. Beyond it the wetted region is annular, and integrating over a full disk with a negative central depth is unphysical. The code rejects that continuation.

For a curved free surface with constant surface tension, the correct pressure is \(p-p_{\mathrm{atm}}=\sigma\kappa\), with the liquid-to-gas normal and the curvature convention of (6.24). The height equation then couples pressure to surface curvature and wall contact conditions; the paraboloid is an approximation. A scale comparison is the Bond number \(\mathrm{Bo}=\rho gR^2/\sigma\), the ratio of a gravity pressure scale \(\rho gR\) to a curvature pressure scale \(\sigma/R\). With an illustrative \(\sigma=0.030\ \mathrm{N/m}\), \(\mathrm{Bo}=3270\), so capillary effects on the vessel-radius scale are small. Near a wall meniscus, curvature can vary on a much shorter length and the same estimate cannot dismiss it. No value of \(\sigma\) is needed for the tension-free calculation.

Initial velocity, compatible data and pressure at startup L12 38:04

The momentum equation contains a first time derivative of velocity, so an unsteady problem needs an initial velocity field, \(\mathbf V(\mathbf x,0)=\mathbf V_0(\mathbf x)\). Incompressibility requires \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V_0=0\). Initial normal velocity must agree with the boundary motion; otherwise the initial data immediately create or destroy volume. For an unknown interface its initial shape is also data. If density is transported, its initial field must be specified too. Calling the velocity the only initial field presupposes fixed density and known geometry.

There is no independently prescribed initial pressure field in the constant-density incompressible formulation. At each time pressure is determined by the velocity constraint and force data, as (6.4) illustrates. Prescribing an arbitrary \(p(\mathbf x,0)\) in addition can overdetermine the problem. At a velocity boundary, the normal component of momentum supplies pressure-gradient information; at a stress boundary, the imposed stress contains pressure itself. These are coupled boundary conditions, so the count of four equations is not a recipe to prescribe all four unknowns on every boundary.

For the vessel, start with \(\mathbf V_0=\mathbf0\) and \(\eta(r,0)=H_m\). A smooth startup law \(\Omega(t)=\Omega[1-\exp(-t/t_r)]\), with ramp time \(t_r=1\ \mathrm s\), makes the initial wall speed zero and avoids a jump between the initial liquid velocity and its wall value. The initial pressure is hydrostatic, \(p=p_{\mathrm{atm}}+\rho g(H_m-z)\), as momentum with zero initial wall speed permits. It is not selected independently. An instantaneous jump to full wall speed is a useful idealization with a nonsmooth initial boundary layer, but a classical solution continuous to that corner in space and time cannot meet both velocity values simultaneously.

The kinematic viscosity sets a diffusion scale: balancing \(\partial_tV\) with \(\nu\partial_\ell^2V\) over a length \(\ell\) gives a time of order \(\ell^2/\nu\). With \(\ell=R\), this is 100 s for the constructed liquid. This is a dimensional comparison, not a calibrated spin-up time; end-wall circulation, rotation and surface waves alter the dynamics. It does show why one cannot infer that a vessel rotated for 1 s already has the final surface (6.29). The steady candidate satisfies the equations and boundaries; determining its approach and stability is a further problem.

Steady, uniform and one-, two- or three-dimensional flow L12 39:27

A field is steady in a specified frame when its value at every fixed position is independent of time. A steady incompressible flow has \(\partial_t\mathbf V=0\), but may retain convective acceleration. A uniform velocity field has the same vector at every position in the region under discussion. Uniformity includes direction as well as speed: \(\mathbf V=U(\cos\theta\,\mathbf i+\sin\theta\,\mathbf j)\) with fixed angle \(\theta\) is uniform, while tangential velocity of constant magnitude around a circle is not. A spatially uniform field \(\mathbf V=U(t)\mathbf i\) can be unsteady. The two classifications answer independent questions.

The final vessel is steady in the inertial laboratory frame and nonuniform: \(\mathbf V=(-\Omega y,\Omega x,0)\) changes with position. Its cylindrical components are independent of \(\theta\), which is axisymmetry, not absence of azimuthal motion. A frame rotating with the vessel makes the final relative velocity zero, but momentum in that frame requires centrifugal and Coriolis terms. Changing the frame without changing the equations would erase the force that the surface is measuring.

The number of independent spatial coordinates and the number of nonzero velocity components are also different. The convention used here calls a fully developed axial pipe profile \(\mathbf V=V_z(r)\mathbf k\) one-dimensional in its velocity variation. Its physical region is three-dimensional, its axial speed varies over a cross-section, and its pressure can still vary along the axis. A plane flow \(\mathbf V=(V_x(x,y,t),V_y(x,y,t),0)\), independent of \(z\), is two-dimensional. A field independent of \(z\) but with \(V_z(x,y,t)\ne0\) has two-dimensional dependence and three components; stating both is clearer than letting “two-dimensional” hide the third component.

Consider a smooth expansion between wide parallel walls. In a developed straight part, velocity can have the form \(V_x(y)\mathbf i\). In the expansion the wall gap depends on \(x\), so the axial profile changes with \(x\) and continuity requires transverse motion. For a section-averaged speed \(V_{\mathrm{avg}}\) and local cross-sectional area \(A_s(x)\), constant-density steady mass balance gives \(A_sV_{\mathrm{avg}}=Q\), with \(Q\) the volume flow rate in \(\mathrm{m^3/s}\). Doubling area halves this mean speed; it says nothing by itself about the local profile or separation. A one-dimensional averaged model of such a duct can be useful even when the actual field depends on two or three coordinates. The averaging must be stated.

Fully developed and periodic are different reductions L12 45:03

A velocity field is fully developed along a straight conduit coordinate \(s\) when its profile stops changing with \(s\). This condition concerns velocity, not pressure. Consider a steady, no-swirl, axisymmetric pipe flow with no body force, \(\mathbf f=\mathbf0\). The field \(\mathbf V=V_z(r)\mathbf k\) makes every convective term zero. Axial momentum nevertheless gives \(\mathrm dp/\mathrm dz=\mu r^{-1}\mathrm d(r\,\mathrm dV_z/\mathrm dr)/\mathrm dr\), so pressure can drive flow while the profile is unchanged. As a check, take a candidate \(V_z=B(R^2-r^2)\), with \(B\) in \(\mathrm{m^{-1}s^{-1}}\). It obeys no slip at the wall and continuity, and its axial viscous force is \(-4\mu B\); the required pressure gradient is exactly \(-4\mu B\), not zero. This is a local check of the classification, not an assumption that all pipe flows have this profile.

Near an inlet a nearly uniform incoming profile must adjust to no slip at the wall. Viscous diffusion changes the near-wall velocity, and the affected region grows into the core while the fluid moves downstream. Conservation of volume flux also accelerates the core as wall-adjacent fluid slows. It is misleading to picture the entire unchanged core speed as fixed by the inlet. A dimensional estimate combines a diffusion time \(R^2/\nu\) and an axial travel speed \(U\), giving an entrance-length scale \(UR^2/\nu\). It supplies neither a coefficient nor a precise definition of “developed”; one must choose a tolerance and solve or measure the developing flow. Laminar and turbulent development use different transport mechanisms, so a single coefficient cannot be transferred between them.

Spatial periodicity means that translating the field by a fixed vector reproduces it, for example \(\mathbf V(x+L_p,y,z,t)=\mathbf V(x,y,z,t)\), with period length \(L_p\). A repeated array of tubes or obstacles may admit such a model well away from its entrance. Identical geometry alone does not establish identical flow in every cell: the first obstacle meets a different upstream state, and instabilities can produce longer-period or irregular states. A one-cell calculation assumes a periodic solution and restricts which patterns it can represent.

Pressure needs special care in a driven periodic cell. If the mean flow loses mechanical energy to viscosity, a pressure drop per cell or another driving force must replace it. One can write \(p=-G_px+\widetilde p\), with a constant imposed drop per length \(G_p>0\) in Pa/m and a periodic remainder \(\widetilde p\). Then \(p(x+L_p)-p(x)=-G_pL_p\), while velocity is periodic. Requiring total pressure to be periodic as well, with no other forcing, removes that drive. For \(G_p=100\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\) and \(L_p=0.020\ \mathrm m\), the pressure falls by 2 Pa per cell.

Temporal periodicity means repetition after a period \(\mathcal P>0\): \(\mathbf V(\mathbf x,t+\mathcal P)=\mathbf V(\mathbf x,t)\). The cylinder-wake example has vortices shed on alternating sides and probe signals with different amplitudes and phases. Two constructed signals \(v_1=U_1+A_1\cos(2\pi t/\mathcal P)\) and \(v_2=U_2+A_2\cos(2\pi t/\mathcal P+\beta)\), with mean speeds \(U_i\), amplitudes \(A_i\) and phase \(\beta\), share a period without sharing a value or peak time. If \(\mathcal P=0.50\ \mathrm s\) and \(\beta=\pi/2\), their corresponding maxima are displaced by 0.125 s modulo a cycle. A periodic trace at two probes is evidence of a repeatable signal there; it does not prove periodicity of the entire velocity field. Turbulent fluctuations can destroy exact repetition while leaving a dominant frequency.

Classifications describe different physical choices

Laminar motion has an organized velocity field without the irregular multiscale fluctuations characteristic of turbulence; laminar flow can be unsteady. Turbulent flow has irregular fluctuations and enhanced transport across the mean flow; its time average can be steady even when each instantaneous field is not. The Navier–Stokes equations describe both within the continuum Newtonian model. Replacing the instantaneous equations by equations for averages introduces unknown correlations of fluctuations, which need a separate closure. “Turbulent” is not a different molecular viscosity law.

The Reynolds number estimates a force ratio using specified scales; it is not a universal switch between laminar and turbulent. For the running vessel choose \(U=\Omega R\) and \(L=R\), giving \(\mathrm{Re}_R=\rho\Omega R^2/\mu=1000\). The rigid-rotation state has nonzero inertia but zero viscous force, so their actual ratio is not 1000: the scaling comparison presumes gradients that this special solution does not have. Stability depends on geometry, forcing and disturbances. No pipe-transition threshold is assigned to this rotating free-surface vessel.

Compressibility is another independent question. Let \(w\) be the speed of sound, and define Mach number \(\mathrm{Ma}=U/w\). For a small nearly isentropic pressure change, \(\Delta\rho\approx\Delta p/w^2\), a thermodynamic relation taken as given. If the pressure change is of the inertial scale \(\rho U^2\), its fractional density change is of order \(\mathrm{Ma}^2\). For an illustrative \(w=1000\ \mathrm{m/s}\), not a measured property of the constructed liquid, \(U=1\ \mathrm{m/s}\) gives \(\mathrm{Ma}=0.001\), and the 500 Pa radial rise gives \(\Delta\rho/\rho\approx5\times10^{-7}\). A long imposed pressure drop, strong heating, composition change or stratification can change density independently of that inertial estimate. This chapter therefore imposes constant density as a model assumption and uses the estimate only to check one possible source of error; it asserts no universal Mach cutoff.

Viscous and inviscid descriptions state which stress contribution is retained. A fluid with small viscosity can have important viscous forces in a thin region, where second derivatives are large. Internal flow identifies a confining geometry, such as a duct; external flow identifies a body surrounded by an ambient fluid. A free surface can occur in an internal vessel or an external jet. These labels are useful only when their actual meaning is stated: a flow may simultaneously be steady, nonuniform, axisymmetric, swirling, viscous and incompressible. The rotating vessel is such an example.

In cylindrical components this is \(V_r=Arz,V_\theta=\Omega r,V_z=-Az^2\); it is divergence-free. Applying (6.13) gives \((a_r,a_\theta,a_z)=(-\Omega^2r,2A\Omega rz,2A^2z^3)\), while (6.12) gives \((0,Ar,2\Omega)\). The two \(A^2rz^2\) contributions to the scalar derivative of \(V_r\) cancel; its inward acceleration still survives. This field tests radial motion and swirl together, which pure rotation alone cannot do.

Central differences in Cartesian coordinates construct its velocity gradient and vector Laplacian at \((0.06,0.08,0.02)\ \mathrm m\). The gradient times velocity gives convective acceleration; only afterward is the result projected on \(\mathbf e_r,\mathbf e_\theta,\mathbf k\) and compared with the cylindrical predictions. This makes the checks independent of copying the same cylindrical formula into both sides of an assertion. The remaining checks integrate the free-surface volume, compute both layer gradients and test invalid vessel geometries. The code uses NumPy 2 for np.trapezoid, reads and writes no files, and prints only the shown results. Assertions reject algebraic mismatches; surface raises ValueError for nonpositive radius or mean depth, or a dry-centre continuation. The supplied grid and finite-difference spacing are fixed teaching choices, not a convergence study of a flow solver.

import math
import numpy as np

# Constructed liquid and vessel: SI units throughout.
RHO, MU, G = 1000.0, 0.1, 9.81
R, H_MEAN, OMEGA = 0.1, 0.05, 10.0


def surface(r, radius, mean_depth, omega):
    if radius <= 0 or mean_depth <= 0:
        raise ValueError("radius and mean_depth must be positive")
    centre = mean_depth - omega**2*radius**2/(4*G)
    if centre <= 0:
        raise ValueError("wet-bottom model requires positive centre depth")
    return centre + omega**2*np.asarray(r)**2/(2*G)


# 1. Test cylindrical derivatives against Cartesian finite differences.
A = 2.0                         # 1/(m s), an independent test-field parameter
x = np.array([0.06, 0.08, 0.02])  # m; r = 0.1 m


def velocity(q):
    x, y, z = q
    return np.array([-OMEGA*y + A*x*z,
                     OMEGA*x + A*y*z, -A*z*z])


eps = 1e-4                     # m
basis = np.eye(3)
J = np.column_stack([(velocity(x + eps*e) - velocity(x - eps*e))/(2*eps)
                     for e in basis])
lap = sum((velocity(x + eps*e) - 2*velocity(x) + velocity(x - eps*e))/eps**2
          for e in basis)
r, z = math.hypot(x[0], x[1]), x[2]
er = np.array([x[0]/r, x[1]/r, 0.0])
et = np.array([-x[1]/r, x[0]/r, 0.0])
a_cart = J @ velocity(x)
a_cyl = np.array([a_cart @ er, a_cart @ et, a_cart[2]])
expected = np.array([-OMEGA**2*r, 2*A*OMEGA*r*z, 2*A*A*z**3])
curl_cart = np.array([J[2, 1]-J[1, 2], J[0, 2]-J[2, 0], J[1, 0]-J[0, 1]])
curl_cyl = np.array([curl_cart @ er, curl_cart @ et, curl_cart[2]])
assert abs(np.trace(J)) < 1e-10
assert np.allclose(a_cyl, expected, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-10)
assert np.allclose(lap, [0.0, 0.0, -2*A], atol=1e-6)
assert np.allclose(curl_cyl, [0.0, A*r, 2*OMEGA], atol=1e-10)
print("a_r, a_theta, a_z / (m/s^2):", " ".join(f"{v:.6f}" for v in a_cyl))
print("curl_r, curl_theta, curl_z / (1/s):",
      " ".join(f"{v:.6f}" for v in curl_cyl))
print("Laplacian z component / (1/(m s)):", f"{lap[2]:.6f}")

# 2. Free surface from radial/vertical momentum and volume conservation.
rr = np.linspace(0.0, R, 10001)
hh = surface(rr, R, H_MEAN, OMEGA)
volume = np.trapezoid(2*np.pi*rr*hh, rr)
assert np.isclose(volume, np.pi*R**2*H_MEAN, rtol=1e-8)
print(f"centre {hh[0]*1000:.3f} mm; rim {hh[-1]*1000:.3f} mm")
print(f"volume {volume*1000:.6f} L; "
      f"radial pressure rise {RHO*OMEGA**2*R**2/2:.3f} Pa")
print(f"Re_R {RHO*OMEGA*R**2/MU:.1f}; diffusion scale {R**2/(MU/RHO):.1f} s")

# 3. Two layers: bottom fixed, top moving, no pressure gradient.
H1, H2, MU1, MU2, U = 0.002, 0.001, 0.1, 0.02, 0.06
tau = U/(H1/MU1 + H2/MU2)
v_interface = tau*H2/MU2
assert np.isclose(v_interface + tau*H1/MU1, U)
assert np.isclose(MU1*(tau/MU1), MU2*(tau/MU2))
print(f"interface {v_interface:.6f} m/s; shear {tau:.6f} Pa")
print(f"gradients upper {tau/MU1:.6f} /s; lower {tau/MU2:.6f} /s")

The last two lines quantify mistakes rather than claiming a successful simulation. Omitting the centripetal term would miss 10 m/s² in the test field; applying the scalar Laplacian to the rotating tangential component would invent \(100\ \mathrm{m^{-1}s^{-1}}\). At the chosen viscosity the latter would create an erroneous force of 10 N/m³. These algebra checks do not establish that the constructed fluid exists with all assumed properties or that the steady vessel state is reached by a particular startup.

What is determined and what remains a modelling choice

The local balances, constitutive law, geometry and initial and boundary data form one problem. Coordinate changes must preserve that problem. Equations (6.13)–(6.18) express the same vector mechanics as Cartesian momentum; the basis derivatives create geometric terms that cannot be discarded by a claim of symmetry. Pressure is a constraint field with a physical stress interpretation, and its reference is fixed by the boundary conditions. The vessel gives an explicit check: pressure supplies 500 Pa of radial rise and the free surface records it as a 50.97 mm height difference.

The steady height prediction assumes constant density, uniform temperature, rigid rotation, a wet bottom, no overflow, negligible gas shear and no surface tension. It gives neither a spin-up history nor a stability theorem. The two-layer model assumes a stationary flat material interface, no interfacial slip, no surface stress and fully developed parallel velocity. Its pressure continuity is derived from those hypotheses; a general interface balances total traction instead. A physical experiment can test these choices. Matching a code listing to its own formulas checks implementation and arithmetic but cannot replace that experiment.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive and check geometry). Consider a steady axisymmetric field with \(V_r=c/r,V_\theta=d/r,V_z=0\) for \(r>0\), where \(c,d\) have units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\). Derive both acceleration components from the trajectory rates and turning unit vectors. At \(r=0.020\ \mathrm m,c=2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s},d=4\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), compute them. Is this a smooth solution throughout a vessel containing its axis?

Solution

The trajectory rates are \(\dot r=c/r\) and \(\dot\theta=d/r^2\). The radial speed changes along a trajectory at rate \((c/r)(-c/r^2)=-c^2/r^3\). Differentiating the tangential basis adds \(-V_\theta\dot\theta=-d^2/r^3\). Thus \(a_r=-(c^2+d^2)/r^3\). The scalar rate of \(V_\theta\) is \((c/r)(-d/r^2)=-cd/r^3\), while turning the radial basis adds \(V_r\dot\theta=cd/r^3\), so \(a_\theta=0\). Equivalently \(rV_\theta=d\) is conserved along a particle: it has no tangential torque per unit mass. Omitting the basis term would falsely give a tangential acceleration.

At the specified radius, \(r^3=8\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3}\) and \(c^2+d^2=2.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m^4/s^2}\), so \(a_r=-2.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) and \(a_\theta=0\). Continuity holds away from the axis because \(\partial_r(rV_r)=0\). But the radial flux through a cylinder per unit axial length is \(2\pi rV_r=2\pi c\ne0\), and both speeds diverge as \(r\to0\). A line source or an excluded inner cylinder is needed. It is not a smooth full-vessel solution. Satisfying the differential equations away from a singularity does not remove that singularity's physical boundary data.

Exercise 2 (compute and judge a vessel). The running vessel has walls 80 mm above the bottom. Under the tension-free rigid-rotation model, find (a) the angular speed at which overflow first occurs, (b) the angular speed at which the centre first dries, and (c) the steady pressure at the outer bottom relative to the atmosphere at the overflow limit. Which limit invalidates the fixed-volume model first? Does this determine a safe transient operating speed?

Solution

(a) Before overflow or drying, (6.29) gives \(\eta(R)=H_m+\Omega^2R^2/(4g)\). Set this to 0.080 m: \(\Omega^2=4g(0.080-0.050)/R^2=117.72\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), so \(\Omega=10.850\ \mathrm{rad/s}\). The centre is then \(H_m-0.030=0.020\) m deep, still wet.

(b) Set \(\eta_c=0\): \(\Omega^2=4gH_m/R^2=196.2\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), so \(\Omega=14.007\ \mathrm{rad/s}\). Overflow therefore occurs first. Once liquid spills, \(H_m\) is no longer conserved, so continuing (6.29) to 14.007 rad/s is not a prediction for this open 80 mm vessel.

(c) At the outer wall the vertical pressure change from its surface to the bottom is \(\rho g\eta(R)=1000(9.81)(0.080)=784.8\) Pa. The central bottom pressure is \(1000(9.81)(0.020)=196.2\) Pa, giving the radial rise 588.6 Pa, also \(\rho\Omega^2R^2/2\). These agree independently. The calculation gives a steady geometric limit only. Startup waves can spill below it, and the model supplies neither wave amplitude nor safety margin. A transient calculation or measured margin is needed before assigning an operating limit.

Exercise 3 (counterexample and normal stress). Two viscous fluids meet at the stationary plane \(y=0\), with no surface tension. On both sides take \(V_x=ax,V_y=-ay,V_z=0\), with \(a=5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), but use \(\mu_1=0.10\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\) above and \(\mu_2=0.020\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\) below. (a) Check incompressibility and the kinematic condition. (b) Find the pressure jump required by traction balance at the plane. (c) Explain why “flat interface implies equal pressure” fails, and state the additional assumption that gave equal pressure in (6.26). Do not assume this local construction automatically supplies a global two-fluid solution.

Solution

(a) The divergence is \(a-a=0\). At \(y=0\), both normal velocities are zero, and the tangential velocity \(ax\) is identical on the two sides. Thus a stationary material plane and velocity continuity are satisfied. The plane is not crossed even though \(\partial_yV_y=-a\ne0\). A derivative near a zero value need not vanish.

(b) The shear is \(\mu(\partial_yV_x+\partial_xV_y)=0\). The normal traction is \(T_{yy}=-p+2\mu(-a)=-p-2\mu a\). Equating it across the plane requires \(-p_1-2\mu_1a=-p_2-2\mu_2a\), or \(p_1-p_2=-2a(\mu_1-\mu_2)=-0.80\ \mathrm{Pa}\). This differs in sign from the positive normal-extension example in the text because this exercise uses normal compression.

(c) Zero curvature removes a capillary pressure term; it does not remove viscous normal stress. The developed reduction imposed \(\partial_xV_x=0\), which with incompressibility made \(\partial_yV_y=0\). Here \(\partial_xV_x=a\), so that hypothesis fails. This is a counterexample at the level of interface data. A global solution must also satisfy each bulk momentum equation, its body forces, density choices and remote boundaries. For example, equal densities and pressure fields with the same quadratic spatial part and the indicated constant jump can support this local strain field.

Exercise 4 (design a two-layer model). In the two-layer shear cell, double the upper-layer viscosity while retaining both thicknesses, the lower viscosity and \(U=0.060\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Find the new interface speed, traction and slopes. Then suppose a calculation reports a continuous velocity with the same slope in both layers. Can it satisfy the interface force balance? Explain how the direction of wall traction follows from the face convention.

Solution

The upper viscosity becomes \(0.20\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\). The compliance-like sum in (6.27) is \(H_2/\mu_2+H_1/\mu_1=0.001/0.020+0.002/0.20=0.060\ \mathrm{m/(Pa\,s)}\). Hence \(\tau_I=0.060/0.060=1.00\ \mathrm{Pa}\). The interface speed is \(\tau_IH_2/\mu_2=0.050\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The upper and lower slopes are 5 and 50 \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), respectively. Their velocity increments are 0.010 and 0.050 m/s, which add to the upper-wall speed.

A common slope \(s\) would give stresses \(\mu_1s\) and \(\mu_2s\). Their equality with unequal viscosities forces \(s=0\); that cannot join a fixed wall to one moving at 0.060 m/s. Continuous velocity alone is insufficient. With positive common \(T_{yx}=\tau_I\), the upper wall, lying on the fluid's positive-\(y\) side, pulls the fluid in \(+x\); the fluid pulls that wall in \(-x\). At the lower boundary the outward fluid normal is \(-\mathbf j\), so the wall-on-fluid traction is \(-\tau_I\mathbf i\), and the fluid pulls the fixed lower wall forward. The opposite wall tractions balance because there is no net axial acceleration or pressure drive.

Exercise 5 (specify compatible startup data). A programmer prescribes \(\mathbf V_0=(b x,0,0)\), with \(b=1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), in a closed stationary box of incompressible liquid. All walls are assigned zero velocity. The programmer also supplies an arbitrary uniform initial pressure and argues that four unknowns require four values everywhere on the boundary. Diagnose the defects. Construct a nonzero smooth initial velocity in a two-dimensional rectangle \(0\le x\le L,0\le y\le H\) that meets incompressibility and zero wall velocity, and say how initial pressure is to be obtained.

Solution

The proposed divergence is \(\partial_x(bx)=b\ne0\), contradicting incompressibility. Its normal velocity at the wall \(x=L\) is \(bL\ne0\), contradicting an impermeable stationary wall. Pressure cannot repair either incompatibility. Assigning arbitrary pressure in addition to all velocity data misunderstands the constraint: pressure follows from the coupled momentum and divergence conditions with the pressure boundary information implied by those data. A spatial reference may be fixed, but a uniform pressure field cannot generally be imposed when acceleration requires a gradient.

To construct valid velocity data, define a smooth scalar \(\Psi=C\sin^2(\pi x/L)\sin^2(\pi y/H)\), with \(C\) in \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\), and set \(V_{0x}=\partial_y\Psi,V_{0y}=-\partial_x\Psi,V_{0z}=0\). Mixed derivatives commute, so \(\partial_xV_{0x}+\partial_yV_{0y}=0\). At \(x=0,L\), both \(\sin^2(\pi x/L)\) and its derivative vanish, making both components zero; the same holds at \(y=0,H\). For \(C\ne0\) there is motion in the interior. This checks the essential velocity and mass compatibility, without proving all higher-order regularity conditions for a classical startup. Pressure is found from the incompressibility constraint, for example (6.4) with momentum-compatible boundary information and a chosen reference, rather than supplied as an independent initial field.

Exercise 6 (periodicity, driving and approximation). A repeated-channel calculation uses a cell of length \(L_p=0.020\ \mathrm m\), no-slip stationary walls, constant density and viscosity, no body force, and a velocity field periodic between the two end faces. (a) If pressure too is periodic and the state steady, show that a nonzero viscous flow has no energy supply. Use the dot product of (6.3) with velocity integrated over the cell. (b) Introduce \(p=-G_px+\widetilde p\) with periodic \(\widetilde p\). For \(G_p=100\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\) and flow rate \(Q=10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\), compute the mechanical power supplied per cell. (c) Two probes share a periodic signal. What evidence is still missing before using a single temporal cycle to represent the entire flow?

Solution

(a) For steady incompressible motion, \(\mathbf V\cdot(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V=\nabla\cdot(\mathbf V|\mathbf V|^2/2)\), and \(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla p=\nabla\cdot(p\mathbf V)\). Their volume integrals become boundary fluxes. Periodic end faces cancel in pairs when both velocity and pressure match; velocity is zero on the walls. Integrating the viscous term by parts also cancels its periodic surface contributions and gives \(-\mu\int\sum_{i,j}(\partial_iV_j)^2\,\mathrm d\forall\), with zero wall contribution. Therefore \(0=-\mu\int|\nabla\mathbf V|^2\,\mathrm d\forall\). For \(\mu>0\), smoothness forces \(\nabla\mathbf V=0\), and a stationary no-slip wall then makes the uniform velocity zero. A nonzero steady flow needs work from a pressure drop, moving wall or body force. The argument assumes the stipulated smooth periodic geometry and no other open boundaries.

(b) The imposed pressure drop is \(G_pL_p=2\) Pa. The pressure work supplied to a cell is \((p_{\mathrm{in}}-p_{\mathrm{out}})Q=2\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm W\). Periodicity of the pressure remainder is compatible with this drop; periodicity of total pressure is not. In a steady viscous solution the supplied work balances dissipation.

(c) Matching periodicity at two probes does not determine the field elsewhere, nor establish that the initial transient has decayed. One must check repetition of the relevant field and interface, or enough independent measurements for the intended prediction, over multiple cycles and with a stated tolerance. A dominant frequency in a spectrum does not imply exact periodicity: amplitudes, phases or additional modes can drift. A one-cycle model is an explicit assumption until that evidence supports it.
