Similitude and modelling

A 20 m vessel travels at 5 m/s. A towing tank can accommodate a geometrically similar model only 2 m long. Matching the balance between inertia and gravity requires a model speed of 1.581 m/s; matching the balance between inertia and viscosity in the same liquid requires 50 m/s. Both prescriptions follow correctly from the equations, yet they cannot both be satisfied. If the model's measured resistance is 40 N at 1.581 m/s, multiplying by the familiar length-scale factor of 1000 gives 40 kN for the vessel only if the unmatched viscous effects do not change the resistance coefficient. The purpose of a model test is to learn which dimensional changes preserve the physics, which dimensionless quantities must match, and what evidence is needed when complete similarity is impossible.

Grounded in four recordings of the NPTEL course Fluid Mechanics (Prof. Sanjay Mittal, IIT Kanpur): Lecture 19 (dimensional analysis and the steady flow equations), Lecture 20 (boundary conditions, force ratios and similarity), Lecture 21A (airship and pump models), and Lecture 21B (an aircraft with propellers and incomplete similarity). Lecture material: the aircraft drag problem; the reduction from dimensional variables to dimensionless groups; the Buckingham Pi theorem; the nondimensional continuity and momentum equations and boundary conditions; Reynolds, Froude, Euler and Strouhal numbers; the cylinder shedding-frequency illustration; geometric and dynamic similarity; drag, power, pump-speed and flow-rate scaling; the incompatibility of aircraft Reynolds and Mach similarity in an ordinary tunnel; propeller-speed scaling and extrapolation from a restricted Reynolds-number range. Research extensions: Buckingham's original discussion of a complete physical relation, dimensional homogeneity, independent groups and alternative group choices (1914, pp. 345–350). Original teaching constructions: the vessel running case, the dimension-matrix and null-space formulation, the explicit repeating-variable calculations, the gravity–viscosity incompatibility, the pressure-datum and thin-layer qualifications, the extrapolation counterexample, Figures 10.1–10.2, the executable checks and six exercises. The liquid properties in the vessel example are stipulated model inputs; the airship properties are the numerical data supplied in Lecture 21A.

The model test and its mathematical objects

A prototype is the full-size system whose behaviour is wanted. A scale model is another system used to obtain that information. Subscripts \(p\) and \(m\) denote prototype and model. For the vessel let \(L_p=20\ \mathrm m\), \(U_p=5\ \mathrm{m/s}\), and \(L_m=2\ \mathrm m\), where \(L\) is hull length and \(U\) is the speed relative to the undisturbed liquid. The length ratio is \(\lambda_L=L_m/L_p=0.1\). The liquid is specified to have constant density \(\rho=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), dynamic viscosity \(\mu=10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\), and kinematic viscosity \(\nu=\mu/\rho=10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) in both tests. These rounded values define an idealized liquid; they are not a temperature-dependent property table. Gravity has magnitude \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\).

The measured output is the resistance \(F_D\), the force opposing the vessel's motion, in newtons. The board uses \(D\) for drag; this chapter uses \(F_D\), reserving \(D\) for a diameter. At constant speed the useful mechanical towing power is \(\dot W=F_DU\), in watts. It excludes motor and transmission losses. A dimensionless resistance coefficient will compare forces after the density, speed and size have changed.

The formal model consists of a list of physical inputs, a relation between those inputs and the output, and the flow problem that justifies the list. Initially the relation will be \(F_D=\mathcal F(U,L,\rho,\mu)\), for a fixed shape in a fully submerged, incompressible flow. The vessel has a free surface, the moving liquid–air interface, so its relation must also allow gravity to influence the answer. Each added mechanism enlarges the input list. Dimensional analysis reduces a stated list; it cannot decide that the list contains every relevant mechanism.

The fluid is treated as a continuum, with velocity \(\mathbf V(\mathbf x,t)\) and pressure \(p(\mathbf x,t)\), where \(\mathbf x=(x,y,z)\) is position in metres and \(t\) is time in seconds. It is Newtonian, meaning that viscous stress is proportional to rate of deformation, with constant coefficient \(\mu\), and incompressible, with constant \(\rho\). The corresponding equations and the no-slip boundary condition will be restated before use. These are the models whose similarity is being tested. Similarity of the resulting dimensionless problems has physical content only while the continuum, constitutive and boundary models remain applicable.

Choosing the physical variables L19 0:53

For an aircraft moving through air, speed and overall length are not the whole specification. Two aircraft of equal length can have different wing shapes, angles to the incoming stream and surface roughness. Air density and viscosity matter. Altitude changes air properties, while sufficiently high speed makes density changes important. A statement that drag depends on four variables therefore describes a restricted family of tests, not every aircraft.

Take geometrically similar rigid bodies, a fixed orientation to a uniform incoming stream, a smooth surface, constant fluid properties and no nearby free surface. Suppose density changes are negligible, gravity has no influence on the velocity field, and there is no independently imposed time scale. Within this restricted model, write

\[ F_D=\mathcal F(U,L,\rho,\mu). \tag{10.1} \]

The symbols on the right are independent inputs: a test can in principle change them, and the drag is an output. Their physical independence is different from independence of their dimensions. Density and viscosity have different dimensions even when a laboratory cannot vary them freely without changing temperature. Conversely, two independent lengths have identical dimensions. Both distinctions matter when counting dimensionless groups.

Testing ten values of each input in (10.1) on a full Cartesian grid would require \(10^4=10{,}000\) combinations. A dimensionless reduction will replace this four-dimensional input space by one dimensionless input, under the assumptions just made. Ten points on that reduced axis are ten tests, but they do not guarantee accuracy: a transition or a narrow feature can require many more points, and replicates are needed to estimate scatter. The reduction concerns the number of independent coordinates, not a universal experiment budget.

Fixing shape means fixing all its length ratios. Fixing a roughness length while changing \(L\) would not do the same job: the relative roughness would change. If \(k_s\) denotes a representative roughness height, a more complete drag relation can contain \(k_s/L\). Likewise a wind-tunnel wall introduces a tunnel-size-to-model-size ratio. These are dimensionless geometric inputs, and they survive the analysis even though they bring no new fundamental dimension. Buckingham begins his original paper by distinguishing a representative length from ratios of other lengths, and by requiring that no relevant quantity be overlooked (Buckingham 1914, p. 345).

Dimensions, units and dimensional homogeneity L19 7:39

A dimension specifies the physical kind of a quantity. A unit supplies its numerical scale. Length has dimension \(\mathsf L\), whether it is expressed in metres or feet; time has dimension \(\mathsf T\), whether seconds or minutes are used. Use \(\mathsf M,\mathsf L,\mathsf T\) for mass, length and time dimensions, keeping the sans-serif dimension symbols distinct from the hull length \(L\). Brackets denote dimensions: for example \([U]=\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1}\). A dimensionless quantity has dimension 1; its numerical value is unchanged by a consistent change of the fundamental units.

Newton's law gives \([F_D]=\mathsf M\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-2}\). Density is mass per volume, so \([\rho]=\mathsf M\mathsf L^{-3}\). Pressure is force per area, hence \([p]=\mathsf M\mathsf L^{-1}\mathsf T^{-2}\). The Newtonian shear law equates stress to \(\mu\) times a velocity gradient; the latter has dimension \(\mathsf T^{-1}\). Therefore \([\mu]=\mathsf M\mathsf L^{-1}\mathsf T^{-1}\), and \([\nu]=\mathsf L^2\mathsf T^{-1}\). These derivations make the dimensional inputs independently checkable.

An equation is dimensionally homogeneous when its additive terms have the same dimensions. Thus \(F_D=\rho U^2L^2\) is dimensionally possible, while \(F_D=\rho UL^2\) is not: the right side of the latter has one fewer inverse-time factor. A numerical coefficient cannot repair this defect unless it carries dimensions, in which case that coefficient is another physical input. Homogeneity is necessary, not sufficient. Both \(F_D=\rho U^2L^2\) and \(F_D=100\rho U^2L^2\) pass this check, as does a dependence on an arbitrary dimensionless function.

The distinction can be tested by changing units. A speed of 5 m/s is 500 cm/s and a length of 20 m is 2000 cm. The ratio \(UL/\nu\) remains \(10^8\) if viscosity is converted from \(10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) to \(0.01\ \mathrm{cm^2/s}\). Using 500 and 2000 with the unconverted \(10^{-6}\) gives a spurious factor of \(10^4\). Dimensional homogeneity does not excuse mixing units. The numerical program below tests a simultaneous change of mass, length and time units for every group.

Arguments of logarithms and trigonometric functions must also be dimensionless. A fit involving \(\log L\) alone changes when metres replace feet; \(\log(L/L_0)\), with a specified reference length \(L_0\), does not. Buckingham makes this restriction explicit alongside the homogeneity principle (1914, p. 346). It is particularly useful when checking a fitted correlation that appears harmless in one unit system.

The Pi theorem as a dimension-matrix calculation L19 8:02

Suppose a complete, unit-independent physical relation involves \(n\) positive dimensional quantities \(q_1,\ldots,q_n\). Positivity permits real powers and logarithms; signed outputs can instead be treated within a fixed-sign branch, with their sign retained separately. Each quantity has a column of exponents in the chosen fundamental dimensions. Assemble those columns into a dimension matrix \(\mathbf A\). Its rank \(r_d\) is the number of independent dimensional directions present in the quantities, not automatically the number of rows used to write them.

A monomial group \(\Pi=\prod_{j=1}^nq_j^{a_j}\) is dimensionless precisely when each fundamental exponent vanishes. If \(\mathbf a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^{\mathsf T}\), this condition is

\[ \mathbf A\mathbf a=\mathbf0,\qquad \text{number of independent exponent vectors}=n-r_d. \tag{10.2} \]

The second statement is the rank–nullity theorem of linear algebra: solving a rank-\(r_d\) homogeneous linear system with \(n\) unknown exponents leaves \(n-r_d\) free parameters. Independence here means linear independence of exponent vectors, or equivalently that no selected monomial is a product of powers of the others. It does not mean that measured outputs can be varied independently of the inputs.

For the five quantities of (10.1), with columns ordered \((F_D,U,L,\rho,\mu)\) and rows \((\mathsf M,\mathsf L,\mathsf T)\),

\[ \mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&1&1\\1&1&1&-3&-1\\-2&-1&0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}. \tag{10.3} \]

The columns of \((\rho,U,L)\) form a square matrix of determinant 1, so its rank is 3. The full matrix has only three rows and therefore also has rank 3. There are \(5-3=2\) independent groups. Counting \(5-3\) is justified by this rank check; it is not a rule to apply before checking independence. For example, three speeds and no other quantities have only one dimensional direction, because their columns are all proportional to \((0,1,-1)^{\mathsf T}\). They have two independent speed ratios, despite the presence of three conventional base dimensions on paper.

Why a relation can be written in these groups. Let new mass, length and time units be positive multiples \(b_M,b_L,b_T\) of the old units. If the \(j\)-th column is \((m_j,\ell_j,t_j)^{\mathsf T}\), its numerical value becomes \(q'_j=q_jb_M^{-m_j}b_L^{-\ell_j}b_T^{-t_j}\). Taking logarithms turns a change of units into addition of a vector in the row space of \(\mathbf A\). Dotting with any null vector \(\mathbf a\) removes this addition, so \(\log\Pi=\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\log\mathbf q\) is unchanged. A basis of the null space supplies the remaining coordinates after changes of units have been removed. A complete relation invariant under those changes can therefore be expressed as

\[ \Phi(\Pi_1,\ldots,\Pi_{n-r_d})=0. \tag{10.4} \]

This is the Buckingham Pi theorem in a linear-algebra form. Buckingham derives the reduction and the repeating-variable equations in his original pp. 347–348; the matrix formulation here makes the independence condition explicit. The theorem says nothing about the unknown function \(\Phi\), its coefficients, or whether a chosen output is single-valued. Those require a flow solution, measurements or additional physical assumptions. The assumed completeness is essential: omitting gravity from a wave-making hull does not make gravity cease to matter.

Constructing groups and choosing their normalization L19 13:16

Repeating variables are \(r_d\) quantities whose dimension columns are independent. Use \(\rho,U,L\), all nonzero in the tests under discussion. Combine each remaining quantity with powers of these three. For drag, set \(\Pi_D=F_D\rho^aU^bL^c\). The mass, time and length equations are respectively \(1+a=0\), \(-2-b=0\), and \(1-3a+b+c=0\). Thus \(a=-1,b=-2,c=-2\), giving \(\Pi_D=F_D/(\rho U^2L^2)\).

For viscosity, set \(\Pi_\mu=\mu\rho^aU^bL^c\). Vanishing mass exponent gives \(a=-1\), vanishing time exponent gives \(b=-1\), and the length equation \(-1-3a+b+c=0\) gives \(c=-1\). The group is \(\mu/(\rho UL)\). Its reciprocal is conventionally called the Reynolds number. Define the two conventional quantities by

\[ C_D=\frac{F_D}{\tfrac12\rho U^2L^2},\qquad \mathrm{Re}_L=\frac{\rho UL}{\mu}=\frac{UL}{\nu}. \tag{10.5} \]

Here \(C_D\) is the drag coefficient based on reference area \(L^2\); \(\tfrac12\rho U^2\) is dynamic pressure. If a measured coefficient uses wing area or frontal area \(A_{\rm ref}\) instead, its definition must be stated and converted: \(C_{D,L^2}=C_{D,A}A_{\rm ref}/L^2\). The factor \(1/2\) is a convention, not an output of dimensional analysis. The exponent vectors \((1,-2,-2,-1,0)\) and \((0,-1,-1,-1,1)\) satisfy (10.3) independently; they are the two groups before the factor 2 and reciprocal are introduced.

If the physical response is a single-valued drag for each set of inputs, (10.4) becomes \(C_D=\mathcal C(\mathrm{Re}_L)\). Every test in the restricted family that has the same Reynolds number has the same dimensionless problem, provided its boundary conditions and flow state also correspond. The function \(\mathcal C\) is still unknown. A constant coefficient, an inverse Reynolds law and a sharply changing coefficient can all satisfy this form.

Groups are not unique. One could use \(1/\mathrm{Re}_L\), \(\mathrm{Re}_L^2\), or \(C_D\mathrm{Re}_L\) with a second independent group. Such replacements preserve the information if the transformation can be inverted on the domain. By contrast, choosing \(\mathrm{Re}_L\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_L^2\) loses the drag information because the two exponent vectors are dependent. Buckingham illustrates different repeating triads and different-looking equivalent relations in pp. 349–350. Choosing familiar groups makes the physical interpretation easier; it does not strengthen the theorem.

Changing the characteristic length has a similar bookkeeping effect. If \(L'=L/2\), then \(\mathrm{Re}_{L'}=\mathrm{Re}_L/2\) and a coefficient based on \(L'^2\) is four times \(C_D\). The drag reconstructed from either convention is the same. A Reynolds number without its speed and length is incomplete information. For the running prototype the specified length and speed give \(\mathrm{Re}_{L,p}=5\times20/10^{-6}=10^8\), but a fully submerged relation based only on this number cannot yet describe its free-surface waves.

Recovering the same parameter from the flow equations L19 17:06

The governing equations explain why Reynolds number appears and which boundary data a dimensional list might have missed. For a steady, incompressible Newtonian fluid of constant density and viscosity without body forces, mass conservation and momentum balance are

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0,\qquad \rho(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V+\nabla p=\mu\nabla^2\mathbf V. \tag{10.6} \]

These equations are restated results of the continuum model, as in Chapter 6, “The balance laws and what closes them”; no solution is assumed here. The convective derivative \((\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V\) is the acceleration experienced by a fluid element moving through a spatially varying steady field. The pressure gradient enters with a plus sign on the left, corresponding to the physical force \(-\nabla p\). The Laplacian \(\nabla^2\) sums second derivatives with respect to the three Cartesian coordinates.

Place the observer on a fixed body, with uniform flow \(U\mathbf i\) far away; \(\mathbf i\) is the unit vector along \(x\). Set \(\mathbf x^*=\mathbf x/L\), \(\mathbf V^*=\mathbf V/U\), and \(p^*=(p-p_\infty)/(\rho U^2)\), with \(p_\infty\) a constant reference pressure. A star denotes a dimensionless variable, and \(\nabla^*\) differentiates with respect to \(\mathbf x^*\). The chain rule gives \(\partial/\partial x=L^{-1}\partial/\partial x^*\), with the same factors for \(y,z\). Consequently,

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf V=\frac UL\nabla^*\cdot\mathbf V^*,\quad \rho(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V=\frac{\rho U^2}{L}(\mathbf V^*\cdot\nabla^*)\mathbf V^*,\quad \mu\nabla^2\mathbf V=\frac{\mu U}{L^2}\nabla^{*2}\mathbf V^*. \]

The pressure gradient has coefficient \(\rho U^2/L\), because the constant \(p_\infty\) disappears under differentiation. Divide continuity by \(U/L\) and momentum by \(\rho U^2/L\):

\[ \nabla^*\cdot\mathbf V^*=0,\qquad (\mathbf V^*\cdot\nabla^*)\mathbf V^*+\nabla^*p^*=\frac1{\mathrm{Re}_L}\nabla^{*2}\mathbf V^*. \tag{10.7} \]

The material constants have combined into a single coefficient. This is a derivation from the equations, whereas the Pi argument reduced a proposed variable list without using the equations. Their agreement is useful evidence that the restricted list is sensible. Neither argument computes the dimensionless velocity or drag.

A check is to rescale the same-fluid submerged problem from \((L_p,U_p)=(10\ \mathrm m,40\ \mathrm{m/s})\) to \(L_m=1\ \mathrm m\). Equality of Reynolds numbers requires \(U_m=400\ \mathrm{m/s}\), since \(U_mL_m=U_pL_p\). Both problems then have the same coefficient in (10.7). This algebra checks the scale law; it also raises a physical alarm for an air test, because density changes cannot generally be neglected at that model speed. Correct nondimensional equations cannot license leaving their hypotheses.

Boundary conditions and the limits of a force-ratio estimate L20 4:48

At a stationary solid the no-slip condition is \(\mathbf V=\mathbf0\); it includes no penetration. Dividing by nonzero \(U\) gives \(\mathbf V^*=\mathbf0\) on the dimensionless surface. Far from the body, \(\mathbf V\to U\mathbf i\), so \(\mathbf V^*\to\mathbf i\). A body of length \(L\) becomes length 1. Geometrically similar bodies therefore occupy the same dimensionless domain, with the same wall and far-field data. A moving wall instead requires equality of its velocity divided by \(U\), and an unsteady initial-value problem requires corresponding dimensionless initial data too.

For the smooth fixed body in (10.7), pressure forces scale as \(\rho U^2\) times area. Viscous stress scales as \(\mu U/L\), from one velocity derivative; divided by \(\rho U^2\) it carries the factor \(1/\mathrm{Re}_L\). Integrating these stresses over a corresponding area proportional to \(L^2\) therefore gives a force proportional to \(\rho U^2L^2\), multiplied by a dimensionless functional of the solution. If the corresponding solution is the same, the coefficient in (10.5) is the same. The connection from matching equations to matching a measured force includes the solution and its boundary data.

Reynolds number estimates the ratio of inertial to viscous force scales: \((\rho U^2/L)/(\mu U/L^2)=\mathrm{Re}_L\). This estimate assumes velocity changes of order \(U\) over distances of order \(L\). Nondimensionalization does not prove that every dimensionless derivative is of order 1. For a velocity change across a layer of thickness \(\delta\ll L\), the transverse second derivative is of order \(U/\delta^2\). Its ratio to an inertial term of scale \(\rho U^2/L\) is then of order \((L/\delta)^2/\mathrm{Re}_L\). If \(\delta/L=\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/2}\), the viscous contribution remains order 1 even at large Reynolds number.

This thin-layer check prevents an incorrect inference: a small coefficient \(1/\mathrm{Re}_L\) does not make viscosity negligible at a no-slip wall. Nor does a large Reynolds number guarantee a particular flow regime or eliminate dependence on roughness and incoming disturbances. Even with identical parameters, an unstable steady solution need not be the observed state. For fluctuating flows, a comparison of measured mean drag requires comparable disturbance conditions and a stated averaging procedure. Similarity is a correspondence of physical problems, not merely equality of two printed numbers.

Gravity, pressure and an independent time scale L20 9:31

Restore the unsteady acceleration and gravity. Let \(z\) point upward and \(\mathbf k\) be the upward unit vector. The dimensional model now reads

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0,\qquad \rho\frac{\partial\mathbf V}{\partial t}+\rho(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V+\nabla p =\mu\nabla^2\mathbf V-\rho g\mathbf k. \tag{10.8} \]

Choose a positive characteristic time \(\mathcal P\), in seconds, and a positive pressure-difference scale \(P_c\), in pascals. The board writes \(T\) for this time and \(P\) for the pressure scale; \(\mathcal P\) avoids the book's stress symbol. Set \(t^*=t/\mathcal P\), \(p^*=(p-p_0)/P_c\), with \(p_0\) constant, and keep the previous length and velocity scales. The unsteady, convective, pressure, viscous and gravitational terms have respective coefficients

\[ \frac{\rho U}{\mathcal P},\quad \frac{\rho U^2}{L},\quad \frac{P_c}{L},\quad \frac{\mu U}{L^2},\quad \rho g. \]

All five have dimensions of force per volume. Comparing them therefore gives dimensionless ratios, with the qualifications about actual gradients made in the preceding section. Define

\[ \mathrm{Fr}_L=\frac U{\sqrt{gL}},\qquad \mathrm{Eu}_{I/P}=\frac{\rho U^2}{P_c},\qquad \mathrm{St}_L=\frac{f_rL}{U}=\frac{L}{U\mathcal P},\quad f_r=\frac1{\mathcal P}. \tag{10.9} \]

The Froude number \(\mathrm{Fr}_L\) is defined so that its square is the inertia-to-gravity ratio. The Euler convention \(\mathrm{Eu}_{I/P}\) follows the board: inertia divided by the pressure-force scale. Some conventions use the reciprocal; writing the ratio prevents ambiguity. The Strouhal number \(\mathrm{St}_L\) is the unsteady-to-convective inertia ratio for a periodic phenomenon of frequency \(f_r\), in cycles per second. The board's frequency subscript distinguishes it from body force. A nonperiodic problem can still use \(L/(U\mathcal P)\) with a specified event time, but it is then a time-scale ratio rather than a shedding frequency.

After division by \(\rho U^2/L\), (10.8) becomes

\[ \mathrm{St}_L\frac{\partial\mathbf V^*}{\partial t^*} +(\mathbf V^*\cdot\nabla^*)\mathbf V^* +\frac1{\mathrm{Eu}_{I/P}}\nabla^*p^* =\frac1{\mathrm{Re}_L}\nabla^{*2}\mathbf V^* -\frac1{\mathrm{Fr}_L^2}\mathbf k. \tag{10.10} \]

For example, if \(U=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(L=1\ \mathrm m\), and the forcing period is 0.25 s, then \(\mathrm{St}_L=2\). Local acceleration is not small compared with convective acceleration on those scales. If the period increases to 25 s with the same \(U,L\), this ratio falls to 0.02, a candidate for a slowly varying approximation. A calculation must still inspect the solution's derivatives; a rapid transient can invalidate the estimate even when the imposed forcing is slow.

Choosing the advective time \(\mathcal P=L/U\) makes the coefficient of the unsteady term unity. It does not remove a genuinely imposed frequency: that frequency then appears in the boundary motion as \(f_rL/U\). Similarly, choosing \(P_c=\rho U^2\) makes the Euler coefficient unity but cannot remove an imposed inlet-to-outlet pressure difference divided by \(\rho U^2\). Dimensionless parameters may move from differential equations to boundary data; they do not disappear by a choice of notation.

The same coefficients distinguish small Reynolds number from slow time dependence. In (10.10), the convective-to-viscous scale ratio is \(\mathrm{Re}_L\), while the unsteady-to-viscous scale ratio is \(\mathrm{Re}_L\mathrm{St}_L=L^2/(\nu\mathcal P)\). Small Reynolds number therefore makes convective inertia small under the assumed spatial scaling, but does not by itself remove rapid local acceleration. Neglecting both inertial terms requires both ratios to be small on the scales of the solution.

For a constant-density liquid, define a modified pressure \(p_h=p+\rho gz\). Its gradient absorbs gravity in (10.8). This removes the explicit gravitational term from the differential equations, but gravity can remain in the boundary data. At a free surface exposed to uniform atmospheric pressure, the simplified condition \(p=p_{\rm atm}\) applies when capillary pressure and viscous normal-stress corrections can be neglected. In that approximation, \(p_h=p_{\rm atm}+\rho gz\) on the interface whose height is unknown, so gravity still enters the boundary-value problem and controls the surface shape and waves. The fully submerged airship example may omit Froude similarity under its stated assumptions of negligible free-surface influence. The vessel cannot omit gravity merely by redefining pressure.

The absolute pressure becomes relevant if the liquid can form vapour cavities. If \(p_v\) is vapour pressure and \(p_\infty\) the undisturbed absolute pressure, a dimensionless available pressure margin is \(\Sigma_c=(p_\infty-p_v)/(\tfrac12\rho U^2)\), here called the cavitation number. With pressure coefficient \(C_p=(p-p_\infty)/(\tfrac12\rho U^2)\), the simple pressure criterion \(p\le p_v\) is \(C_p\le-\Sigma_c\). This criterion identifies the pressure margin, not a full nucleation model. Adding a constant to a single-phase incompressible pressure field does not change its velocity solution, but it changes this margin. Treating cavitation as irrelevant is therefore a physical assumption about absolute pressure, not a consequence of setting an Euler coefficient to 1.

Strouhal number: an imposed frequency or an observed output L20 29:42

A fixed circular cylinder in a uniform stream can shed vortices periodically into its wake. Let \(D\) now denote the cylinder diameter, and let \(f_s\) be the repetition frequency of one complete shedding cycle, defined consistently for every measurement. A signal counting alternating events on both sides must be converted to that cycle definition. The dimensionless frequency is \(\mathrm{St}_D=f_sD/U\), and the Reynolds number is \(\mathrm{Re}_D=UD/\nu\). For a smooth long cylinder with fixed inflow and end conditions, the shedding frequency is an output: one seeks \(\mathrm{St}_D=\mathcal S(\mathrm{Re}_D)\), rather than independently prescribing both numbers.

The illustrative cylinder sketch in Lecture 20 shows a nearly linear frequency–speed curve and a nearly flat Strouhal curve at about 0.2 over a shedding regime. This is a qualitative empirical illustration, not a universal correlation or a claim that every Reynolds number sheds vortices. Taking 0.2 as a stipulated illustrative value, a 0.05 m cylinder at 2 m/s has \(f_s=0.2U/D=8\ \mathrm{Hz}\). A 0.10 m cylinder at 1 m/s in the same fluid has the same \(UD\), hence the same Reynolds number; the corresponding illustrative frequency is 2 Hz. The factor-four frequency change agrees with the time-scale ratio \((L_m/U_m)/(L_p/U_p)\).

A rotating pump or propeller is different. Its shaft rotation is an imposed input, and \(f_rL/U\) must be matched when that motion matters. A measured shedding frequency cannot generally be forced to an arbitrary value without changing the problem. Calling both frequencies “Strouhal numbers” does not give them the same experimental role. Before matching any group, decide whether it specifies the input problem or describes the response.

Geometric, kinematic and dynamic similarity L20 35:43

Geometric similarity means that corresponding lengths have one common ratio \(\lambda_L\) and all corresponding angles are equal. Areas then scale as \(\lambda_L^2\) and volumes as \(\lambda_L^3\). A model of a railway coach that scales the main body but leaves door handles at their full-size thickness is not geometrically similar at those details. The practical question is whether the omitted details affect the observable of interest; dimensional analysis alone cannot answer it. For the vessel, hull proportions, immersion depth divided by length, and the tank dimensions relative to the hull all belong to the geometric specification.

Kinematic similarity means that the velocity fields have the same direction and dimensionless magnitude at corresponding points and times. If \(\lambda_U=U_m/U_p\), corresponding velocities differ by \(\lambda_U\), and corresponding trajectories require a time ratio \(\lambda_t=\lambda_L/\lambda_U\). To derive the last relation, use particle displacement \(\mathrm d\mathbf x=\mathbf V\,\mathrm dt\): scaling displacement by \(\lambda_L\) and velocity by \(\lambda_U\) forces time to scale by their ratio. A geometrically faithful model with an unmatched rotating-wall speed need not have kinematic similarity.

Dynamic similarity means that the corresponding force balances have the same dimensionless form and data, so the relevant force ratios match. Within the same continuum and constitutive models, matching all independent input groups, dimensionless geometry, boundary conditions and initial conditions makes the dimensionless flow problems identical. Corresponding solutions then give kinematic similarity and the same dimensionless force outputs. When there are multiple possible states or turbulent fluctuations, the state selection and statistical comparison require corresponding conditions as well. Output equality is conditional on that correspondence.

Under complete similarity, the drag and useful towing-power ratios follow directly from (10.5):

\[ \frac{F_{D,m}}{F_{D,p}}=\frac{\rho_m}{\rho_p}\lambda_U^2\lambda_L^2,\qquad \frac{\dot W_m}{\dot W_p}=\frac{\rho_m}{\rho_p}\lambda_U^3\lambda_L^2,\qquad C_{D,m}=C_{D,p}. \tag{10.11} \]

The force equation is obtained by cancelling the equal coefficients and the common \(1/2\); the power equation multiplies by one more speed ratio. If the scales are all unity, both ratios are unity. If the liquid density and speed are unchanged while lengths halve, force and towing power both fall by a factor of 4, provided the coefficient equality remains justified. It usually does not remain justified automatically, because Reynolds number has also halved. Equation (10.11) is a consequence of similarity, not a substitute for establishing it.

A related aircraft test question specifies a 10 m prototype at 100 m/s and 30,000 ft altitude, and a 1 m laboratory model. A length ratio alone does not determine the test speed when the fluid states differ. Reynolds similarity requires \(U_m=(L_p/L_m)(\nu_m/\nu_p)U_p=1000(\nu_m/\nu_p)\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The necessary altitude and laboratory viscosities must be supplied before this becomes a numerical prescription. Setting their ratio to 1 would give 1000 m/s, but that substitutes an extra assumption and requires a compressibility check. Altitude belongs in the test through the relevant fluid properties and other environmental data; fixing it in an initial variable list does not authorize ignoring a different prototype state.

The vessel: Reynolds and Froude requirements conflict

The running hull has both a no-slip wetted surface and free-surface waves. At a minimum its reduced resistance relation has the form \(C_D=\mathcal H(\mathrm{Re}_L,\mathrm{Fr}_L)\) for fixed dimensionless geometry, when surface tension, compressibility and other effects are negligible. This follows by adding \(g\) to the five-variable list: there are six quantities of rank 3 and hence three groups, one output and two inputs. The repeating-variable calculation for \(g\rho^aU^bL^c\) gives \(a=0,b=-2,c=1\), so its group is \(gL/U^2=1/\mathrm{Fr}_L^2\).

Matching Reynolds number requires \(U_mL_m/\nu_m=U_pL_p/\nu_p\), while matching Froude number requires \(U_m^2/(g_mL_m)=U_p^2/(g_pL_p)\). Written in scale ratios,

\[ \lambda_U=\frac{\nu_m/\nu_p}{\lambda_L}\quad(\mathrm{Re}),\qquad \lambda_U=\sqrt{\frac{g_m}{g_p}\lambda_L}\quad(\mathrm{Fr}). \tag{10.12} \]

In the same liquid under the same gravity, the two conditions would give \(1/\lambda_L=\sqrt{\lambda_L}\). With positive lengths this requires \(\lambda_L=1\). A reduced model cannot satisfy both by adjusting speed alone. This is an incompatibility of constraints, not an algebraic defect in either scaling rule.

For the 1:10 hull, \(\mathrm{Fr}_{L,p}=5/\sqrt{9.81\times20}=0.3570\). The Froude-matched speed is \(U_m=5\sqrt{0.1}=1.5811\ \mathrm{m/s}\), and \(\mathrm{Re}_{L,m}=1.5811\times2/10^{-6}=3.1623\times10^6\). This is only \(0.1^{3/2}=0.031623\) of the prototype Reynolds number \(10^8\). Reynolds matching instead demands \(U_m=50\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Its Froude number is 11.288, 31.623 times the prototype value. Figure 10.1 exposes the two speed prescriptions before any force extrapolation is attempted.

If gravity is fixed, simultaneous similarity could be recovered mathematically by choosing \(\nu_m/\nu_p=\lambda_L^{3/2}=0.031623\), or \(\nu_m=3.1623\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). This is a required property, not a claim that an appropriate safe liquid is available. Changing the fluid may also change density, surface tension and other mechanisms. Alternatively changing gravity introduces its own experimental constraints. Counting available controls against active groups is part of model design.

At Froude similarity in equal-density liquids, \(\lambda_U=\sqrt{\lambda_L}\), so the force scale in (10.11) is \(\lambda_L^3=0.001\) and the power scale is \(\lambda_L^{7/2}=0.00031623\). A stipulated 40 N model measurement would therefore imply 40 kN and 200 kW if the total coefficient matched; the model towing power is only 63.25 W. The exact accounting is instead

\[ F_{D,p}=40{,}000\ \mathrm N\;\frac{C_{D,p}}{C_{D,m}}. \tag{10.13} \]

The Reynolds numbers differ by a factor of 31.6, so setting this coefficient ratio to 1 needs evidence about viscous resistance and its interaction with the waves. A wave-dominated observable may be well represented while a total resistance is not. Separate measurements, controlled Reynolds-number variation or an independently justified correction can help; the two-group relation itself supplies no correction coefficient. The 40 kN number is a conditional extrapolation, not a result established by the stipulated measurement.

Small models can acquire additional active groups. If \(\sigma\) is surface tension in N/m, balancing capillary pressure of scale \(\sigma/L\) with inertia gives the Weber number \(\mathrm{We}_L=\rho U^2L/\sigma\). Its inverse follows from repeating variables: \(\sigma\rho^{-1}U^{-2}L^{-1}\) is dimensionless. In the same liquid under Froude scaling, \(\mathrm{We}_{L,m}/\mathrm{We}_{L,p}=\lambda_L^2=0.01\); capillarity is relatively 100 times stronger in the model. Both Weber numbers might still be large enough for the chosen observable, but that is a second scale check. Matching Reynolds and Froude numbers would not automatically match a capillary-sensitive contact line or a compressible flow.

A submerged airship model: changing the test fluid L21A 9:54

The airship example supplies a case where complete similarity is possible within the stated restrictions. The prototype travels in air at \(U_p=10\ \mathrm{m/s}\); a 1:20 model is towed in water, sufficiently far from the free surface and tank walls for their influence to be neglected. Assume incompressibility, smooth geometrically similar surfaces, steady relative motion and no cavitation. Gravity then changes hydrostatic pressure but does not supply a free-surface force balance. The only active dynamic input in this model is Reynolds number.

The numerical fluid data supplied with the problem are \(\rho_m=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(\mu_m=1.14\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\), \(\rho_p=1.226\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), and \(\mu_p=1.78\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\). Their temperatures are not specified, so use them as problem data rather than general water and air reference values. Reynolds matching gives

\[ U_m=\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_m}\frac{\mu_m}{\mu_p}\frac{L_p}{L_m}U_p =\frac{1.226}{1000}\frac{1.14\times10^{-3}}{1.78\times10^{-5}}\,20\,(10\ \mathrm{m/s}) =15.7038\ \mathrm{m/s}. \tag{10.14} \]

The model must travel faster even though water has the smaller kinematic viscosity: reducing the length by 20 is larger than the compensating viscosity ratio. The computation can be checked without knowing the actual airship length. The ratio \((U_m/U_p)(L_m/L_p)(\nu_p/\nu_m)\) is exactly 1 with the unrounded speed, so the two Reynolds numbers coincide.

With equal drag coefficients, (10.11) yields \(\dot W_m/\dot W_p=(1000/1.226)(15.7038/10)^3/20^2=7.89706\). Thus \(\dot W_p/\dot W_m=0.126629\), which rounds to the supplied answer 0.127. The corresponding model-to-prototype drag ratio is 5.02875. A smaller model does not necessarily require a smaller towing force or towing power: the liquid is much denser, and speed enters power cubically. These ratios predict useful drag power only. Propulsive efficiency and mechanical losses are separate quantities absent from this test.

The relatively high water speed makes the no-cavitation assumption consequential. Depth, absolute pressure and the model's lowest local pressure must be checked before the test is accepted as a single-phase submerged flow. The problem gives no pressure margin or model dimensions, so it cannot establish that check numerically. The scaling answer is conditional on the stated absence of cavitation and free-surface influence.

A pump model: pressure and rotation set the scales L21A 22:51

A prototype pump delivers \(Q_p=5\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\) against a head \(H_p=250\ \mathrm m\) at 500 revolutions per minute. A geometrically similar 1:9 model uses the same liquid and is tested against \(H_m=10\ \mathrm m\). Here \(Q\) is volume flow per time, and head \(H\) is mechanical energy supplied per unit weight of fluid. For this idealized test, compare inlet and outlet sections of equal elevation and equal mean speed, so the prescribed head is represented by a pressure rise \(\Delta p=\rho gH\). Neglect viscous losses and gravity's variation across the pump itself, as the idealized problem specifies. The external head remains important: neglecting gravitational force within the pump does not set its pressure rise to zero.

Let \(U\) be the mean speed through a corresponding passage and \(L\) a corresponding pump length. Match the pressure ratio \(\rho U^2/\Delta p\). Because both tests use the same \(\rho\) and \(g\),

\[ \frac{U_m}{U_p}=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta p_m}{\Delta p_p}}=\sqrt{\frac{H_m}{H_p}}=\frac15. \tag{10.15} \]

Let \(f_r\) be shaft revolutions per second and \(\Omega=2\pi f_r\) angular speed in rad/s. Equal \(f_rL/U\) is equivalent to equal \(\Omega L/U\), since the factor \(2\pi\) cancels. The board writes the shaft rate with \(\omega\); \(\Omega\) keeps it distinct from vorticity in this book. The two shaft rates can be compared in rpm because the conversion to inverse seconds cancels in their ratio. Hence

\[ \frac{\Omega_m}{\Omega_p}=\frac{U_m}{U_p}\frac{L_p}{L_m}=\frac95, \qquad \text{model rate}=900\ \mathrm{rpm}. \tag{10.16} \]

At corresponding sections the area ratio is \(1/81\). Since \(Q=U A\) for a section-mean speed, \(Q_m/Q_p=(1/5)(1/81)=1/405\), and

\[ Q_m=\frac5{405}=0.0123457\ \mathrm{m^3/s}. \tag{10.17} \]

The area-ratio argument applies to every pair of corresponding cross-sections, because geometric similarity scales their areas by \(L^2\). As checks, the dimensionless flow \(Q/(\Omega L^3)\) and dimensionless head \(gH/(\Omega^2L^2)\) are the same in both tests. Substitution gives ratios \((1/405)/[(9/5)/9^3]=1\) and \((1/25)/[(9/5)^2/9^2]=1\). These are two independent ways to express the same pressure and rotation scaling.

The Reynolds-number ratio is \((U_m/U_p)(L_m/L_p)=1/45\) in the same liquid. Complete similarity of the viscous pump has therefore not been achieved. The calculation is complete similarity of the stipulated inertia–pressure–rotation approximation. Real pump efficiency, leakage, clearance effects and cavitation can change as the model gets smaller; this problem supplies no evidence that their influence is negligible. Pressure head and rpm determine a useful test condition, but do not themselves verify an efficiency prediction.

The aircraft: matching Reynolds number can activate compressibility L21B 0:16

The aircraft example has a fuselage length of 10 m, a wingspan of 15 m and speed \(U_p=100\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Two propellers each have diameter 1 m and rotate at 2000 rpm. A 1:10 model has a 1 m fuselage, 1.5 m span and 0.1 m propellers. All lengths, not only the selected Reynolds-number length, obey the common ratio. The test uses the same air properties as the prototype. These are the stipulated data of the example, not specifications for a named aircraft.

With Reynolds number based on fuselage length and free-stream speed, equality requires \(U_m=U_p/\lambda_L=1000\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Define Mach number \(\mathrm{Ma}=U/w\), where \(w\) is speed of sound; the board writes \(a\) for it. With the example's rounded \(w=330\ \mathrm{m/s}\), prototype Mach number is 0.303 and model Mach number is 3.03. The latter is supersonic and outside the incompressible equations used to derive the scaling. A same-air Mach match instead demands \(U_m=U_p\), which gives only one tenth of the desired Reynolds number.

The often-used \(\mathrm{Ma}\approx0.3\) incompressible criterion is an approximate engineering guide, not a sharp boundary or a guarantee about every local speed. Here the prototype is already near that guide, and the propeller tips can move faster than the free stream. The tenfold Mach increase is nevertheless sufficient to show the failure: a test at 1000 m/s is not a similar low-speed problem merely because its Reynolds number agrees. The missing input, sound speed, has become active. A compressible model needs thermodynamic relations and appropriate thermal data in addition to Mach number; the incompressible momentum equation alone does not derive that model.

Suppose, as the example specifies, the tunnel speed is limited to 80 m/s. This is a constraint of that illustrative facility, not a statement about its present-day performance. Tests at 60, 70 and 80 m/s give Reynolds-number ratios of 0.06, 0.07 and 0.08 relative to the prototype. To preserve the propeller's imposed motion relative to the stream, match \(f_rD_{\rm prop}/U\), with \(D_{\rm prop}\) its diameter. Geometric similarity makes using the fuselage length instead equivalent for the ratio. Thus

\[ \frac{f_{r,m}}{f_{r,p}}=\frac{U_m}{U_p}\frac{D_{{\rm prop},p}}{D_{{\rm prop},m}}, \qquad (60,70,80\ \mathrm{m/s})\longrightarrow(12{,}000,14{,}000,16{,}000\ \mathrm{rpm}). \tag{10.18} \]

The advance ratio \(J=U/(f_rD_{\rm prop})\) is the forward distance travelled per revolution divided by propeller diameter. It is the reciprocal of this rotation-based Strouhal group. The prototype has \(f_{r,p}=2000/60=33.333\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), so \(J=3\); the 80 m/s model has \(f_{r,m}=266.667\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and also \(J=3\). This checks the conversion from rpm to frequency and explains physically why model rpm must change with test speed.

Rotation also affects compressibility. The prototype tip speed is \(\Omega_pD_{{\rm prop},p}/2=104.72\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Combining it orthogonally with the axial speed gives a kinematic relative-speed estimate \(\sqrt{100^2+104.72^2}=144.80\ \mathrm{m/s}\), or Mach 0.439 with the stipulated sound speed. The corresponding 80 m/s model estimate is Mach 0.351, exactly 0.8 of the prototype value. This is a velocity-triangle estimate without induced flow, not a blade aerodynamic solution. It shows why a free-stream Mach check alone does not establish incompressibility everywhere in a propeller test.

Incomplete similarity and the evidence needed for extrapolation L21B 16:45

Incomplete similarity describes a test in which some active dimensionless inputs differ from the target. The aircraft tests preserve geometry and advance ratio but miss prototype Reynolds number and do not exactly match Mach number. The vessel tests preserve Froude number but miss Reynolds number. Such experiments can still be informative, but output coefficients are then evaluated at different points of a multidimensional response function. Equality is no longer a consequence of the similarity argument.

A proposed remedy is to measure the coefficient over an accessible Reynolds-number range and extrapolate toward the prototype. The illustrative aircraft sketch suggests a coefficient that falls and then approaches a plateau. That shape is a hypothesis to test for the particular geometry, flow state and observable. It is not a consequence of the Pi theorem, and drag coefficients need not fall monotonically or approach the sketched value over the range of interest. Transition, separation and roughness can change the trend. Three data points in a narrow range cannot establish behaviour a factor of 12.5 farther along the Reynolds axis.

Figure 10.2 gives a counterexample with synthetic data. Let \(s=\mathrm{Re}_L/\mathrm{Re}_{L,p}\). Suppose measured coefficients at \(s=0.06,0.07,0.08\) are fitted exactly by \(C_A(s)=0.020+0.0008/s\). Another response \(C_B(s)=C_A(s)+0.02(s-0.06)(s-0.07)(s-0.08)\) passes through the same three points. At the prototype \(s=1\), however, they give 0.020800 and 0.036885. Both are dimensionless functions with positive values on the plotted domain. The second is not proposed as an aerodynamic law; it proves that agreement at the available points and dimensional correctness do not identify the extrapolation.

The force conversion for the aircraft must retain that uncertainty. At 80 m/s, the equal-density size-and-speed factor is \((U_p/U_m)^2(L_p/L_m)^2=(100/80)^2\times10^2=156.25\). The valid expression is

\[ \frac{F_{D,p}}{F_{D,m}}=156.25\frac{C_{D,p}}{C_{D,m}}. \tag{10.19} \]

The factor 156.25 alone applies only if the coefficient ratio is 1. Using the synthetic 80 m/s value \(C_{D,m}=0.030\), model A gives a drag ratio of 108.333; model B gives 192.111. These numbers are demonstrations of underdetermination, not aircraft predictions. Once Reynolds similarity has been sacrificed, cancelling the coefficients would erase the very dependence the experiment is trying to estimate.

A defensible incomplete-similarity programme states the observable, the groups actually matched, the groups left unmatched, the available range and the correction model. It checks sensitivity to geometry, surface condition and inflow disturbances; widens the range where practical; compares more than one plausible extrapolation; and seeks independent evidence near the target. A pressurized tunnel can, in principle, change \(\rho/\mu\) and increase Reynolds number without increasing speed in the same proportion, but its fluid state, Mach number and other groups must then be recalculated. An improved control does not remove the need to account for all active mechanisms.

Executable dimensional and scaling checks

The listing uses only the Python standard library. It first computes the exact rank and a null-space basis of a seven-column dimension matrix for \((F_D,U,L,\rho,\mu,g,\sigma)\). It checks four physically chosen independent group vectors against that matrix: \(F_D/(\rho U^2L^2)\), \(1/\mathrm{Re}_L\), \(1/\mathrm{Fr}_L^2\), and \(1/\mathrm{We}_L\). It then verifies invariance under an arbitrary simultaneous change of mass, length and time units. A deliberate mutation replaces the squared speed in the force group by a single speed; its nonzero dimension vector must raise ValueError. The final calculations reproduce the running case and all numerical lecture examples, and compare the two extrapolations.

Inputs are the dimension exponents and the stipulated SI data in the listing; outputs are the printed diagnostics and assertions. The functions handle the small finite matrices used here; exact rational elimination avoids a floating-point rank tolerance. Group evaluation assumes strictly positive quantities, as in the monomial construction. The code reads no files and has no effect except printing. An unexpected dimensional failure, rank mismatch, invariance error or arithmetic mismatch stops execution with an exception. Passing these checks establishes dimensional algebra and finite-case arithmetic, not the completeness of the input list or the validity of a model test.

from fractions import Fraction
from math import isclose, prod, sqrt, pi


def reduce_rows(rows):
    a = [[Fraction(v) for v in row] for row in rows]
    pivots = []
    r = 0
    for c in range(len(a[0])):
        pivot = next((i for i in range(r, len(a)) if a[i][c]), None)
        if pivot is None:
            continue
        a[r], a[pivot] = a[pivot], a[r]
        divisor = a[r][c]
        a[r] = [v/divisor for v in a[r]]
        for i in range(len(a)):
            if i != r:
                factor = a[i][c]
                a[i] = [x - factor*y for x, y in zip(a[i], a[r])]
        pivots.append(c)
        r += 1
        if r == len(a):
            break
    return a, pivots


def null_basis(rows):
    a, pivots = reduce_rows(rows)
    basis = []
    for c in range(len(a[0])):
        if c not in pivots:
            v = [Fraction(0)]*len(a[0])
            v[c] = Fraction(1)
            for r, pivot in enumerate(pivots):
                v[pivot] = -a[r][c]
            basis.append(v)
    return basis


def residual(matrix, vector):
    return tuple(sum(x*y for x, y in zip(row, vector)) for row in matrix)


def require_group(matrix, vector):
    dimensions = residual(matrix, vector)
    if any(dimensions):
        raise ValueError(f"dimension residual {dimensions}")


def evaluate(values, vector):
    if any(v <= 0 for v in values):
        raise ValueError("monomial inputs must be positive")
    return prod(q**a for q, a in zip(values, vector))

What a model test can establish

Dimensional analysis identifies invariant combinations of a complete list of quantities. The rank calculation determines their number, and a repeating-variable construction finds a basis. The equations provide a second route to the groups and reveal whether they occur in the differential equations, boundary conditions, geometry or imposed motion. Neither route supplies the unknown coefficient function. A measurement or a solution is still needed.

Complete similarity transfers a corresponding dimensionless solution between systems satisfying the same physical models. It includes geometric ratios, all active independent inputs and appropriate boundary and initial data. A very small or very large unmatched group can sometimes be neglected in a controlled approximation, but the observable must be insensitive to the omitted term on the scales that actually occur. A no-slip layer, a free surface, a phase change or a local high speed can defeat an estimate made from a single global scale.

The vessel decision is therefore specific. A 2 m model at 1.581 m/s matches the prototype Froude number but has only 3.16 % of its Reynolds number. Its 40 N resistance supports a conditional 40 kN estimate multiplied by the unknown coefficient ratio in (10.13). It directly informs a wave-sensitive test at the chosen Froude number; it does not by itself measure full-scale total drag. The airship and pump examples show why changing the fluid or choosing a justified reduced model can make other tests tractable. The aircraft example shows why a formally correct Reynolds prescription can cross into a different physical regime. Model design consists of making those choices explicit and then testing their consequences.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive and diagnose a repeating set; sections Pi theorem and group construction). For the drag list \((F_D,U,L,\rho,\mu)\), a student proposes \((U,L,\nu)\) as the three repeating quantities, where \(\nu=\mu/\rho\). Explain why that choice cannot remove all dimensions of drag. Construct a valid alternative using \((\rho,\mu,L)\), and relate its two groups to \(C_D\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_L\).

Solution

The columns of \(U,L,\nu\) all have zero mass exponent. They span only the length–time subspace, so their rank is 2. No powers of them cancel the mass dimension in \(F_D\). Introducing \(\nu\) also changes the original variable list; it cannot replace both \(\rho\) and \(\mu\) when force is the output. For \((\rho,\mu,L)\), the columns have determinant 1 and rank 3, so they form a valid repeating set.

Write the speed group as \(U\rho^a\mu^bL^c\). Mass gives \(a+b=0\), time gives \(-1-b=0\), and length gives \(1-3a-b+c=0\), hence \(a=1,b=-1,c=1\). The group is \(\rho UL/\mu=\mathrm{Re}_L\). For \(F_D\rho^a\mu^bL^c\), the equations are \(1+a+b=0\), \(-2-b=0\), and \(1-3a-b+c=0\). They give \(a=1,b=-2,c=0\), hence \(\rho F_D/\mu^2\). By (10.5), this is \(\tfrac12C_D\mathrm{Re}_L^2\). It is independent of the speed group because it contains drag, and the transformation back to \(C_D\) is invertible at positive Reynolds number. A valid basis need not look like the conventional one.

Exercise 2 (counterexample to a force-ratio shortcut; section boundary conditions). Consider steady fully developed flow between fixed parallel plates at \(y=\pm h\), with streamwise velocity \(V_x(y)=V_0[1-(y/h)^2]\), \(V_y=V_z=0\). Here \(V_0\) is the centre speed. Show that the convective acceleration is zero for every \(V_0\). Derive the pressure gradient required by the momentum equation. Does the Reynolds number \(V_0h/\nu\) equal the actual inertia-to-viscous-force ratio in this solution?

Solution

Only \(V_x\partial/\partial x\) survives in \(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla\), but the profile is independent of \(x\). The convective acceleration is therefore exactly zero. Steady flow has no local acceleration either. The streamwise momentum equation reduces to \(\mathrm dp/\mathrm dx=\mu\,\mathrm d^2V_x/\mathrm dy^2=-2\mu V_0/h^2\). For forward \(V_0\), pressure falls along the flow. The pressure force balances the viscous force.

The actual inertia-to-viscous ratio here is zero, even when \(V_0h/\nu\) is large. Reynolds number is a ratio of characteristic scales, not the value of two evaluated terms in every solution. This counterexample is the plate solution of Chapter 7, restated locally. It does not show that arbitrarily large-Reynolds-number experiments will preserve the steady profile; stability and disturbances determine whether that solution is observed.

Exercise 3 (inverse design with conflicting groups; section vessel scaling). A model liquid can be selected with \(\nu_m=0.20\nu_p\), and gravity is unchanged. Determine the length ratio and speed ratio that match both Reynolds and Froude numbers. For \(L_p=20\ \mathrm m\), \(U_p=5\ \mathrm{m/s}\), find the model size and speed. If density is also unchanged, find the force ratio. Identify a further property that must be checked for a free surface.

Solution

Set the two speed prescriptions of (10.12) equal: \(0.20/\lambda_L=\sqrt{\lambda_L}\). Thus \(\lambda_L^{3/2}=0.20\), giving \(\lambda_L=0.20^{2/3}=0.341995\) and \(\lambda_U=0.20^{1/3}=0.584804\). The model is 6.8399 m long and travels at 2.9240 m/s. Substituting gives \(\lambda_U\lambda_L/0.20=1\) for Reynolds and \(\lambda_U^2/\lambda_L=1\) for squared Froude, independent checks on the inverse design.

At equal density the force ratio is \(\lambda_U^2\lambda_L^2=\lambda_L^3=0.04\), conditional on all active groups matching. Surface tension must also be considered. Weber matching requires \(\sigma_m/\sigma_p=(\rho_m/\rho_p)\lambda_U^2\lambda_L=\lambda_L^2=0.116961\) at equal density. A liquid chosen solely for its viscosity need not have this surface tension. The two-group inverse solution is not automatically a complete free-surface test.

Exercise 4 (compute and assess an approximation; section pump model). Keep the 1:9 pump and the same liquid, but vary the model test head \(H_m\) from 2.5 to 40 m while the prototype remains at 250 m, 500 rpm and 5 m³/s. Derive the model rpm, flow rate and Reynolds ratio as functions of \(H_m\). Evaluate the endpoints and the 10 m case. Can this head range test the assumption that viscosity is irrelevant?

Solution

Pressure similarity gives \(\lambda_U=\sqrt{H_m/250}\). Rotation similarity gives model rpm \(=4500\sqrt{H_m/250}\); area scaling gives \(Q_m=(5/81)\sqrt{H_m/250}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\). In the same liquid \(\mathrm{Re}_m/\mathrm{Re}_p=(1/9)\sqrt{H_m/250}\). At 2.5 m the three values are 450 rpm, 0.00617284 m³/s and 0.0111111; at 10 m they are 900 rpm, 0.0123457 m³/s and 0.0222222; at 40 m they are 1800 rpm, 0.0246914 m³/s and 0.0444444.

The range changes model Reynolds number by a factor of 4 while keeping the intended pressure and rotation ratios. Comparing measured dimensionless performance over this sweep can reveal viscosity sensitivity. A systematic change would refute the approximation over the tested range. A flat response supports insensitivity over that range but does not prove it up to the prototype, whose Reynolds number is still at least 22.5 times larger. Absolute Reynolds numbers require the missing pump size or passage speed.

Exercise 5 (design under two operating limits; section aircraft model). The model propeller drive is limited to 15,000 rpm and the tunnel to 80 m/s. For the aircraft example, choose the largest admissible speed that preserves advance ratio. Compute its Reynolds ratio and the equal-coefficient drag scale factor. What coefficient ratio remains if only incomplete similarity is available? Assess the proposal to use 80 m/s while holding 15,000 rpm.

Solution

Equation (10.18) gives model rpm \(=200U_m\) with \(U_m\) in m/s. The rpm limit therefore requires \(U_m\le75\ \mathrm{m/s}\), stricter than the tunnel limit. Choose 75 m/s and 15,000 rpm. The Reynolds ratio is \((75/100)(1/10)=0.075\); the prototype is at 13.333 times the model Reynolds number. The density–speed–size drag factor is \((100/75)^2(10)^2=177.7778\), so the valid relation is \(F_{D,p}/F_{D,m}=177.7778(C_{D,p}/C_{D,m})\).

At 80 m/s and 15,000 rpm, the shaft frequency is 250 s⁻¹ and model propeller diameter is 0.1 m. The advance ratio is \(80/(250\times0.1)=3.2\), compared with prototype 3. The 6.67 % mismatch changes the rotational boundary data as well as the already unmatched Reynolds number. A larger speed does not improve the test uniformly; it trades one mismatch against another. The missing coefficient correction would then depend on both mismatches.

Exercise 6 (computational identifiability; section incomplete similarity). Use the three synthetic measurements at \(s=0.06,0.07,0.08\) and the two functions of Figure 10.2. Generalize the second to \(C_K(s)=C_A(s)+K(s-0.06)(s-0.07)(s-0.08)\). Derive the prototype coefficient and prototype-to-model drag ratio at the 80 m/s test as functions of \(K\). If independent evidence restricts \(0\le K\le0.01\), quantify the remaining range. Why can repeated measurements at the same three points not determine \(K\)?

Solution

The cubic factor is zero at every measurement point, so every \(K\) reproduces all three measurements exactly. At \(s=1\) its value is \(0.94\times0.93\times0.92=0.804264\). Thus \(C_K(1)=0.0208+0.804264K\). At the 80 m/s point \(s=0.08\), the coefficient is always \(0.030\), so (10.19) gives drag ratio \(156.25(0.0208+0.804264K)/0.030\).

For \(0\le K\le0.01\), the prototype coefficient ranges from 0.020800 to 0.02884264, and the drag ratio from 108.333 to 150.222. These bounds are conditional on the asserted family and the independent bound on \(K\). Repetition can reduce measurement uncertainty at the sampled points, but the response there has zero sensitivity to \(K\); it supplies no information about this direction of model uncertainty. A measurement at a different \(s\), especially nearer the target, or an independently supported physical restriction on the response is needed. Dimensional consistency supplies neither.
