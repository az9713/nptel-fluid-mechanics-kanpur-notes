Fluid mechanics, rate processes and the continuum hypothesis

Two flat plates 1 mm apart enclose water at 300 K. The upper plate moves at 0.1 m/s and the lower plate is fixed. The law of viscosity that this chapter introduces predicts that a steady shear stress of 0.0854 Pa acts on each plate, and the diffusion equation derived from it predicts that the water takes 0.57 s to settle to within 1 % of that steady motion at the mid-plane. With air at 300 K in the same gap the figures are 1.85 mPa and 0.031 s. Both predictions treat the fluid as a continuum, a medium whose density and velocity have a value at every point. That treatment fails when the mean free path \(\lambda\) of the molecules is not small compared with the length \(L\) of the flow: the ratio \(\mathrm{Kn}=\lambda/L\) is \(6.9\times10^{-5}\) for air in the 1 mm gap, 0.069 for air in a gap of 1 µm, and 0.88 for a vehicle 10 m long at an altitude of 130 km, where the tabulated mean free path is 8.8 m. This chapter defines a fluid, states the continuum hypothesis with a number attached to it, defines density and viscosity as properties of a continuum, derives how fast viscosity spreads motion across a gap, and introduces the stress tensor that the later equations of motion need.

Grounded in two recordings of the NPTEL course Fluid Mechanics (Prof. Sanjay Mittal, IIT Kanpur): Lecture 1 (introduction, with projected slides) and Lecture 2 (the continuum hypothesis and properties, at the chalkboard). Lecture material: the three engineering aspects of fluid flow and the application slides (cooler, dam, aircraft, parachute, bridge and cable oscillations, blood flow, respiratory and kidney devices, drug delivery, face masks, washing machine, sports, Formula 1 cars); the slides of flow past a cylinder with their Reynolds numbers, laminar and turbulent boundary layers, flow past a sphere, bluff and streamlined bodies and the relative-drag slide, streamlining of bicycles, laminar and turbulent jets and other flow phenomena; the definition of a fluid by continuous deformation under shear; the microscopic and macroscopic descriptions, the mean free path, the Knudsen number and the continuum approach; the classification of properties; the estimate of density from a volume of molecules and its dependence on the volume; viscosity, the no-slip condition, Newton's law of viscosity, the table of density and viscosity of four fluids, the Newtonian and non-Newtonian curves; Newton's second law for a fluid element, body and surface forces, the stress as a tensor and its symmetry. Research extensions: the U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 (mean free path, collision diameter, Sutherland viscosity, sea-level values and the 130 km table row); the IAPWS formulation of the viscosity of water; the Knudsen-number regimes as quoted in a 2016 preprint; the critical Reynolds number of the cylinder wake from a 2025 preprint that tabulates earlier studies. The running case and the objects of the chapter

The running case is a fluid between two parallel plates. Let the plates be flat, infinite in the \(x\) and \(z\) directions, and a distance \(H\) apart. Put the lower plate at \(y=0\) and the upper plate at \(y=H\). The lower plate is fixed. The upper plate moves in the \(+x\) direction at a constant speed \(U\) from the time \(t=0\), when the fluid is at rest. The lecture draws this arrangement with a plate speed \(U\) and the fluid velocity component \(V_x\) (the board writes velocity components as \(V_x\), \(V_y\) and the velocity vector with an under-tilde; this book writes the vector in bold type). Unless stated otherwise \(H=1\ \mathrm{mm}\) and \(U=0.1\ \mathrm{m/s}\).

Two fluids fill the gap. The first is liquid water at absolute temperature \(\Theta=300\ \mathrm K\) and a pressure of about 0.1 MPa, with density \(\rho=996.556\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) (IAPWS R6-95(2018), Table 7, p. 15) and dynamic viscosity \(\mu=8.537\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\), computed from Eqs. (10)–(12) of the IAPWS R12-08 viscosity formulation with its critical-enhancement factor set to 1 (section 2.8, p. 8). A program that evaluates those equations reproduces all eleven check values of the release's Table 4 (p. 8), for example 889.735100 µPa·s at 298.15 K and 998 kg/m³. The second fluid is air at 300 K and 101325 Pa, with properties from the U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 (USSA-1976) as section "Mean free path and the Knudsen number in numbers" and the table in section "Viscosity, the no-slip condition and Newton's law" show.

The formal model has three parts. The medium is a continuum: a set of properties, each a function of position \(\mathbf x\) and time \(t\), defined in section "Properties of a continuum and rate processes". The laws are Newton's second law applied to a fluid element, and a constitutive law, a relation between stress and rate of deformation that characterises the fluid; the lecture gives one, Newton's law of viscosity. The geometry and boundary data are the plate positions and speeds, with the no-slip condition at each wall: fluid touching a wall has the wall's velocity. Each section below changes one part of this model: it defines the medium, tests when the medium description holds, adds a law, or derives a consequence for the two plates. Three questions organise the chapter. What is a fluid? When may it be treated as a continuum? What does the continuum model predict for the plates?

Flow past a cylinder: the Reynolds number and what the flow does L1 19:37

The second half of Lecture 1 shows pictures that the speaker takes from Milton Van Dyke's An Album of Fluid Motion (the slide titled "Fascinating Flow Phenomena" says "most pictures taken from" it; the slides give no plate or page numbers, and the album is cited here only through the slide captions). The first sequence is the flow of a uniform stream past a circular cylinder of diameter \(D\). The speaker chooses it because the shape has a single geometric parameter and no sharp edge. He says that most people would expect a simple flow, and that the flow is "extremely rich".

The drag on a body is the component of the force exerted by the fluid along the direction of the undisturbed stream. Its drag coefficient is \(C_D=F/(\tfrac12\rho V^2A)\), where \(F\) is drag, \(\rho\) is density, \(V\) is stream speed, and \(A\) is a specified reference area. For a cylinder of diameter \(D\) and length \(\ell\), take \(A=D\ell\). The denominator has units \(\mathrm{(kg/m^3)(m^2/s^2)(m^2)=N}\), so \(C_D\) is dimensionless. This normalization is a definition, not a law predicting drag.

The wake is the disturbed flow downstream of a body. Separation occurs when the flow close to a surface turns away from it, leaving a detached region; the cylinder pictures show recirculating fluid within that region. A laminar flow has smooth, ordered motion, whereas a turbulent flow has irregular velocity fluctuations and vortices over many scales that enhance mixing. These are qualitative descriptions here: the cylinder examples below distinguish a periodic wake from a turbulent one, so time dependence alone does not define turbulence.

The sequence is organised by the Reynolds number. For a stream of speed \(V\) far from the cylinder, a fluid of density \(\rho\) and dynamic viscosity \(\mu\), and the cylinder diameter \(D\) as the length, the lecture defines

\[ \mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho V D}{\mu}=\frac{V D}{\nu}, \qquad \nu=\frac{\mu}{\rho}, \tag{1.1} \]

where \(\nu\) is the kinematic viscosity defined in section "Viscosity, the no-slip condition and Newton's law". The units cancel: \(\mathrm{(kg/m^3)(m/s)(m)}\) divided by \(\mathrm{Pa\,s}=\mathrm{kg/(m\,s)}\) is dimensionless. The speaker calls \(\mathrm{Re}\) "a non-dimensional speed": for a fixed diameter and a fixed fluid, \(\mathrm{Re}\) is proportional to \(V\). Table 2 converts the Reynolds numbers of the slides into speeds for a cylinder of diameter 1 cm. The conversion shows how slow the flows at the start of the sequence are: \(\mathrm{Re}=41\) is 6.4 cm/s in air at 300 K and 3.5 mm/s in water at 300 K.

The lecture's slide states that the drag of a streamlined body is an order of magnitude lower than that of a bluff body, and illustrates the rule on the slide "Streamlining: Relative Drag" (shown from about 36 min). What a fluid is L2 0:19

Lecture 2 begins with the question of what a fluid is, and answers by comparing a solid and a fluid under a shear force. A shear force on a surface is a force parallel to the surface, and the force per unit area is a shear stress. The board shows two sketches. In the first, a solid is held between a fixed lower plate and an upper plate that is pulled sideways by a force \(F\); the solid deforms into a sheared shape, labelled "deformed solid". The deformation is a fixed angle for a given force. In the second, the same plates enclose a fluid. The upper plate is pulled by the force \(F\), and the board draws the shape of the fluid at \(t=0\) and at later times \(t_1,t_2,t_3\), each more sheared than the previous one. The board writes: "Continuous deformation on application of a shear force \(\Rightarrow\) deformation rate. Flows. A fluid cannot resist a shear force."

The statement can be made quantitative with the geometry of the plates. Let the upper plate be displaced by \(\Delta x\) relative to the lower plate. The shear strain is \(\Delta x/H\), the lateral displacement divided by the gap, which for a small angle equals the angle of the board's sketch. For the solid, the strain reaches a fixed value and stays there while the force is applied, and it returns to zero if the force is removed, as the speaker says the solid "may go back to its original state". For the fluid, the strain has no limit. If the upper plate moves at a steady speed \(U\), then \(\Delta x=Ut\) and the strain rate, the quantity the board calls the deformation rate, is \(U/H\), in units of \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). For \(U=0.1\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(H=1\ \mathrm{mm}\) it is 100 s\(^{-1}\); after 10 s the upper plate has moved 1 m, a strain of 1000. A solid cannot be sheared to a strain of 1000 and remain a solid, but a fluid can. This is the content of the speaker's remark that a solid deforms and "stays there" under a force, and that a fluid "goes through a deformation rate".

The definition used in this chapter is therefore: a fluid is a material that deforms continuously, without a limit, for as long as a shear stress acts on it. The board states "cannot resist a shear force"; the words "for any nonzero shear stress" make the meaning precise, and they are this chapter's reading of the board, not words on it. One consequence follows directly from the definition. A fluid at rest cannot carry a shear stress, because a shear stress would make it deform. The statement is a consequence of the definition and not an independent law.

The lecture divides fluids into liquids and gases, notes that the intermolecular forces in a gas are much smaller than in a liquid so that a gas takes the shape of its container and a liquid does not, and states that the course will not distinguish them. The vocabulary differs: the mechanics of water is called hydromechanics or hydrodynamics, and for air the same principles apply under the name aerodynamics. The speaker names a submerged submarine and an aircraft or airship as examples whose analysis uses the same principles.

The definition has a boundary that the lecture reaches at its end, when the stress–rate curves are drawn (section "Fluids that are not Newtonian"). A material that sustains a shear stress below a threshold and flows above it, which the board names an ideal plastic, resists shear force in the board's own sense below the threshold. By the definition above it is a solid below the threshold and a fluid above it. The lecture lists it among the curves it does not treat. Exercise 4 works through such a material.

The continuum approach and the Knudsen number L2 9:05

The lecture states that fluid mechanics is studied by experiment, or by theoretical and computational analysis, and that the course follows the second route, applying balance laws of mass, force (momentum) and energy. Two kinds of analysis are available. A microscopic analysis follows every particle. A macroscopic analysis averages over the particles, and the lecture names the second the continuum approach: the fluid is treated "as if it fills up this room", and the balance laws are applied to the medium without distinguishing the particles.

The reason for the choice is the number of particles. Air at 300 K and 101325 Pa holds \(2.446\times10^{25}\) molecules in every cubic metre (the program below computes this from the ideal-gas relation \(n=p/(k_B\Theta)\) with Boltzmann's constant \(k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}\)). The speaker calls following all of them in a room "a huge task".

The lecture then asks when the averaging is allowed, with an image: a tray of marbles in which one marble is struck by a force \(F\). The struck marble hits others, and the collisions transfer mass, momentum and energy through the tray. The distance that a marble travels on average before it collides is the mean free path, written \(\lambda\) on the board. Let \(L\) be the length of the tray over which the motion is of interest. The board writes the dimensionless ratio

\[ \mathrm{Kn}=\frac{\lambda}{L}, \tag{1.2} \]

the Knudsen number, and the condition "if \(\mathrm{Kn}\ll1\)" for the continuum approach. The speaker's reasoning: when the marbles are close together, a disturbance reaches the others after a short time and one need not follow the spacing; when they are far apart "the dynamics would get very complicated".

The chapter states the lecture's criterion as a hypothesis: the continuum hypothesis says that when \(\mathrm{Kn}\ll1\), the medium may be described by properties, such as density and velocity, that are functions of position and time, and these properties obey the balance laws of mass, momentum and energy. The words "\(\ll1\)" are the lecture's. Section "Mean free path and the Knudsen number in numbers" replaces them with numbers.

One qualification concerns the image of the tray. The marbles are close together in the image, and the lecture's criterion compares \(\lambda\) with \(L\), not the spacing of the particles with \(L\). In air at 300 K and 101325 Pa the mean spacing of molecules, \(n^{-1/3}\), is 3.44 nm and the mean free path is 69 nm, a factor of 20 larger. A gas molecule travels twenty spacings between collisions. The image of a crowded tray therefore describes a liquid better than a gas; the condition that matters for a gas is the number of collisions during the passage across the region, which is about \(L/\lambda\) for each molecule.

Properties of a continuum and rate processes L2 14:37

Once the particles are replaced by a medium, the lecture says, the key idea is that of a property: instead of looking at the properties of each particle, one looks at the property of the medium that fills the space. A property in this book is a quantity assigned to every point of the continuum at each time and obtained by averaging over the molecules in a small volume around the point. The lecture does not define the word; the definition is this chapter's, and section "Density at a point: the volume must be neither too small nor too large" shows how the average is taken.

The speaker says that kinematic quantities are not in a true sense fluid properties and groups them with the others for convenience. This statement is read here as a statement about definitions: velocity, displacement, acceleration and strain rate are defined by motion alone, and their definitions do not contain the density or the viscosity. In a given flow their values still depend on the fluid, because the fluid decides the motion that forces produce; the statement concerns what is needed to define them.

The classification has one inconsistency. When the speaker turns to density as an example, he calls it "a transport property". The board's lists do not contain density, and density is not a transport property in the board's sense: it does not help to transport anything. This chapter treats density as a state (thermodynamic) property, since it is fixed by the state of the material, and notes the discrepancy here.

The speaker says that the transport properties help to transport mass, momentum and energy in a continuum. The course title joins fluid mechanics with rate processes, and the lectures do not define the second term; its only appearances in the two recordings are the title and the slide that says that fluid motion modifies the "rate of transfer of heat/vapour from a surface". The definition used in this book is: a rate process is a process by which momentum, energy or the mass of a chemical species is carried through a medium at a rate that depends on the gradient of a related field, with a transport property as the coefficient. The lecture supplies one such law, Newton's law of viscosity (1.8) below, in which the gradient of velocity gives the transport of momentum. The conductivity and the mass diffusion of the board play the same role for energy and for mass. The lecture lists them and writes no law for them, and this chapter uses none of the two.

The units show that Newton's law is a statement about the transport of momentum. A stress is a force per unit area, \(\mathrm{Pa}=\mathrm{N/m^2}\), and a newton is \(\mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}\), so \(\mathrm{Pa}=(\mathrm{kg\,m/s})/(\mathrm{m^2\,s})\): a shear stress is also the rate at which momentum crosses a unit area. A velocity gradient has units \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), so the coefficient \(\mu\) of (1.8) has units \(\mathrm{Pa\,s}\). The section on start-up shows that the ratio \(\nu=\mu/\rho\), in \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\), controls how fast momentum spreads.

Mean free path and the Knudsen number in numbers L2 19:49

The lecture asks how good the continuum approximation is and answers with two values of \(\lambda\) for air. The board reads \(\lambda\approx6\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\) for air at 300 K, and writes "\(\lambda\sim102\) m" for an altitude of 130 km, with no decimal point visible; the speaker says "something like 10.2 meters". The speaker's comparison: for a body 1 cm long, the first value gives "enough particles to give me collisions"; for an aircraft 10 m long at 130 km, a particle that hits the nose and another that hits the tail are so few that the continuum approximation is not valid. The board shows a tick for the first case and a cross for the second.

A formula for \(\lambda\). Model the molecules as hard spheres of diameter \(\sigma\), placed independently at random with number density \(n\). A molecule that travels a path of length \(\ell\) sweeps a cylinder of cross-section \(\pi\sigma^2\): any other molecule whose centre lies within \(\sigma\) of the path is hit. The expected number of centres in the swept volume is \(n\pi\sigma^2\ell\). The path length at which this number equals one is the mean free path if the other molecules stand still:

\[ \lambda_0=\frac{1}{n\pi\sigma^2}. \tag{1.3} \]

The other molecules move, and the rate of collision depends on the relative speed. For molecules whose velocities follow the Maxwell distribution, the mean relative speed is \(\sqrt2\) times the mean speed, which divides the free path by \(\sqrt2\) (a result of kinetic theory, taken as given here). The result is the form in the U.S. Standard Atmosphere (USSA-1976, Eq. (47), p. 17), which the ideal-gas relation \(n=p/(k_B\Theta)\) turns into the second form:

\[ \lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt2\,\pi\sigma^2 n}=\frac{k_B\Theta}{\sqrt2\,\pi\sigma^2\,p}. \tag{1.4} \]

The Standard Atmosphere adopts the effective collision diameter \(\sigma=3.65\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm m\) and says that this value is suitable for the part of the atmosphere below about 86 km, which is dominated by nitrogen and oxygen; above that height \(\sigma\) "begins to change significantly" (p. 17). Equation (1.4) with this \(\sigma\), at 288.15 K and 101325 Pa, gives \(6.633\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\), against \(6.6328\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\) in the sea-level table (Table 10, p. 20). At 300 K the same equation gives \(6.906\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\). The board's \(6\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\) has one significant figure and no stated pressure. It is 13 % below the computed value; \(6.9\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\) would round to \(7\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\). The values agree in order of magnitude, which is sufficient for the length-scale comparisons below. The program of section "Executable check of the numbers and the derived equations" uses the exact 2019 value of \(k_B\) where the Standard Atmosphere uses its own constants; the check against the table agrees within 0.01 %.

Density at a point: the volume must be neither too small nor too large L2 22:18

The lecture asks how a property is measured, and chooses density. Take a small box of volume \(\Delta\forall\) around a point; it contains particles of total mass \(\Delta m\). The board defines

\[ \bar\rho(\Delta\forall)=\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta\forall}. \tag{1.5} \]

The natural next step is the limit \(\Delta\forall\to0\). The speaker shows why it fails: as the box shrinks it holds fewer particles, and in the end it holds none, "and then I would get a very strange value for the density". The board draws the estimate of \(\rho\) against \(\Delta\forall\): large fluctuations "at the microscopic level" for the smallest volumes, then a range that gives a "good estimate", then a departure that the board labels "material inhomogeneity", where the volume is large enough that the density of the continuum itself changes inside it. The speaker concludes that there is a suitable range of \(\Delta\forall\) and that the statement holds for every property, viscosity included.

Two errors bound the averaging volume. Molecular fluctuations set a lower bound, while variation of the mean density across the box sets an upper bound.

The lower bound. Assume that the molecules are independent, with positions uniform in the region, as in a dilute gas. The number \(N\) in a fixed box of volume \(\Delta\forall\) then has a Poisson distribution, whose mean is \(\bar N=n\Delta\forall\) and whose standard deviation is \(\sqrt{\bar N}\) (a standard result of statistics, taken as given). The mass estimate \(\bar\rho=Nm/\Delta\forall\), with \(m\) the mass of one molecule, has a relative standard deviation

\[ \frac{\Delta\rho_{\mathrm{rms}}}{\bar\rho}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar N}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\,\Delta\forall}}. \tag{1.6} \]

The relative error falls as the box grows. For an error of 1 % one needs \(\bar N=10^4\). For air at 300 K and 101325 Pa, with \(n=2.446\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), that is \(\Delta\forall=4.1\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{m^3}\), a cube of side 74 nm, which is 1.07 mean free paths. A cube of side 0.1 µm holds \(2.4\times10^4\) molecules and has a relative error 0.64 %; a cube of side 1 µm holds \(2.4\times10^7\) and has 0.020 %. The assumption of independent positions describes a dilute gas; in a liquid the positions are correlated, and the number is a model prediction and not a measured limit (Exercise 3).

The upper bound. Suppose that the density of the continuum varies along \(x\) over a length \(L\), \(\rho(x)=\rho_0\,(1+\varepsilon\cos(2\pi x/L))\), with \(\rho_0\) the spatial mean density and \(\varepsilon\) the relative amplitude of the variation. A box of side \(\ell\) centred at \(x=0\) averages the density over \(-\ell/2\le x\le\ell/2\). The average of \(\cos(2\pi x/L)\) over that interval is \(\sin(\pi\ell/L)/(\pi\ell/L)\), which is smaller than the value 1 at the centre. The box underestimates the density at the centre by the relative amount

\[ b(\ell)=\frac{\varepsilon\,\bigl[1-\operatorname{sinc}(\ell/L)\bigr]}{1+\varepsilon}\approx\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\,\frac{(\pi\ell/L)^2}{6}, \qquad \operatorname{sinc}(a)=\frac{\sin\pi a}{\pi a}, \tag{1.7} \]

for small \(\ell/L\). The bias grows as \(\ell^2\) and does not depend on the number of molecules. Here \(n\) is the mean number density, so the mean count in the box is \(\bar N=n\ell^3(1+\varepsilon\operatorname{sinc}(\ell/L))\). The total relative root-mean-square error, measured against the density at the centre, is \(\sqrt{b^2+\bar N/[n\ell^3(1+\varepsilon)]^2}\), which tends to \(\sqrt{b^2+1/\bar N}\) for small \(\varepsilon\). The bias term rises with box size while the noise term falls. Their competition produces a minimum between the two extremes.

The window. A window of acceptable box sizes exists only when the smallest size from (1.6) is below the largest size from (1.7). For a bias of 1 % with \(\varepsilon=0.2\), (1.7) gives \(\ell\lt0.193\,L\). With \(L=1\ \mathrm{mm}\), the length of the plate gap, that is 0.193 mm, against a lower bound of 74 nm: the window spans a factor of about 2600 in length.

The window and the Knudsen number are related but they are two conditions. The lower bound of the box is \(21.5\) molecular spacings for a 1 % error, because \(\bar N^{1/3}=21.5\) for \(\bar N=10^4\). In air at 101325 Pa the mean free path is 20 spacings, so the lower bound (74 nm) is close to \(\lambda\) (6.9 × 10⁻⁸ m). At lower density the spacing grows as \(p^{-1/3}\) and \(\lambda\) as \(p^{-1}\), so \(\lambda\) grows faster. At 130 km (\(n=1.930\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\)) the spacing is 1.73 µm and the lower bound is 37 µm, 4.2 × 10⁻⁶ times the mean free path of 8.8 m. A density can therefore be defined at a length well below \(\lambda\) in a rarefied gas, while \(\mathrm{Kn}\) is not small. A well-defined density is necessary for the continuum description and does not make \(\mathrm{Kn}\) small; the lecture's condition \(\mathrm{Kn}\ll1\) is a separate requirement on the collisions.

Viscosity, the no-slip condition and Newton's law L2 27:48

Viscosity resists relative motion between neighbouring layers of fluid. In the running case the lower plate is fixed and the upper plate moves at speed \(U\). If the fluid were an ideal fluid, one without viscosity, the speaker says, one could move the plate and the fluid would not even feel it; he compares the plate to a skater who glides on ice without friction. With viscosity, the fluid next to the upper plate moves with the plate. The board draws velocity profiles at increasing times, each a curve from zero at the lower plate to \(U\) at the upper plate that includes more of the gap as time passes: "more fluid gets energized because of the motion of the upper plate".

The no-slip condition. The fluid in contact with a wall has the velocity of the wall; the board writes "no-slip condition on velocity" in a bracket beside viscosity. The speaker says that the condition "arises essentially out of viscosity" and that a fluid without viscosity "may not" obey it. This is the speaker's explanation, and it is not derived in the lecture. This chapter takes no slip as a boundary condition supported by experiment for a continuum at small Knudsen number. The classification of section "Mean free path and the Knudsen number in numbers" supports that reading: Liu and He's slip regime, which begins at \(\mathrm{Kn}=0.001\), is the range in which the no-slip condition has to be replaced. No slip is therefore also a statement about the continuum limit, and not only about viscosity.

Newton's law of viscosity. Let \(\tau\) be the shear stress in the fluid at a height \(y\), defined as the \(x\)-component of the force per unit area that the fluid on the \(+y\) side of the plane exerts on the fluid on the \(-y\) side. The board's law is

\[ \tau=\mu\,\frac{\mathrm dV_x}{\mathrm dy}, \tag{1.8} \]

where \(\mu\) is the coefficient of viscosity, also called the coefficient of dynamic viscosity. The law is a model of the fluid and not a derivation: the stress is proportional to the velocity gradient, which is the rate of deformation of section "What a fluid is" (for a velocity \(V_x(y)\), the shear strain rate is \(\mathrm dV_x/\mathrm dy\)). The speaker explains that \(\mu\) is a property of the fluid: a more viscous fluid generates more shear stress for the same gradient, just as the coefficient of friction depends on the pair of surfaces. The sign follows the definition of \(\tau\). When \(V_x\) increases with \(y\), the fluid above moves faster than the fluid below and drags it forward, so \(\tau\gt0\). The board writes the law with no sign convention; the convention here is this chapter's. The units of \(\mu\) are \(\mathrm{Pa\,s}\), as found in section "Properties of a continuum and rate processes".

Kinematic viscosity. Dividing the dynamic viscosity by density defines

\[ \nu=\frac{\mu}{\rho}, \tag{1.9} \]

the kinematic viscosity, with units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\). Dynamic viscosity relates stress to deformation rate; kinematic viscosity controls the diffusion of velocity across the gap, as (1.11) shows.

How fast the plate drags the fluid: a diffusion equation L2 30:10

How long does the upper plate take to bring the fluid to its steady profile? Newton's second law and Newton's law of viscosity determine the time as well as the final stress.

Assumptions. (A1) The velocity is parallel to the plates and depends only on \(y\) and \(t\), \(\mathbf V=V_x(y,t)\,\mathbf i\), with \(\mathbf i\) the unit vector along \(x\). (A2) There is no pressure gradient along \(x\) and no body force along \(x\). (A3) \(\rho\) and \(\mu\) are constants. (A4) Newton's law (1.8) holds at every height and time. The plates are infinite, which makes (A1) plausible; a finite plate has edges where it fails. The wall condition is no slip: \(V_x(0,t)=0\) and \(V_x(H,t)=U\) for \(t\gt0\). The fluid is at rest at \(t=0\): \(V_x(y,0)=0\) for \(0\le y\lt H\).

The slab balance. Take a slab of fluid between the heights \(y\) and \(y+\mathrm dy\), with unit area in the \(x\)–\(z\) plane. Its mass is \(\rho\,\mathrm dy\), and its acceleration along \(x\) is \(\partial V_x/\partial t\), because by (A1) every part moves in a straight line. The \(x\)-force on the slab comes from the fluid across its two faces. At the upper face \(y+\mathrm dy\) the slab is on the \(-y\) side of the plane, so the fluid above exerts \(+\tau(y+\mathrm dy,t)\) on it. At the lower face \(y\) the slab is on the \(+y\) side, so the fluid below exerts \(-\tau(y,t)\) on it. The pressure forces on the two faces normal to \(x\) are equal by (A2) and cancel. Newton's second law gives \(\rho\,\mathrm dy\,\partial V_x/\partial t=\tau(y+\mathrm dy,t)-\tau(y,t)\), and dividing by \(\mathrm dy\) and letting it tend to zero,

\[ \rho\frac{\partial V_x}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial y}=\mu\frac{\partial^2V_x}{\partial y^2}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \frac{\partial V_x}{\partial t}=\nu\frac{\partial^2V_x}{\partial y^2}. \tag{1.11} \]

The second form uses (A3), (A4) and \(\nu=\mu/\rho\). Equation (1.11) has the form of a diffusion equation, with \(\nu\) in the role of the diffusivity, and it is the rate process of this chapter: momentum acquired at the moving wall spreads across the gap at a rate set by \(\nu\). In the steady state \(\partial V_x/\partial t=0\), and (1.11) gives \(\partial^2V_x/\partial y^2=0\), a linear profile; the wall conditions fix it as \(V_x=Uy/H\), which is the profile used in (1.10).

The solution. Write \(s=y/H\) and, for the dimensionless time, \(t^*=\nu t/H^2\). The solution of (1.11) with the boundary and initial conditions above is

\[ \frac{V_x}{U}=s+\frac2\pi\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{k}\,\sin(k\pi s)\,e^{-k^2\pi^2t^*}. \tag{1.12} \]

Verification. Each term of the sum satisfies (1.11): the time derivative multiplies it by \(-k^2\pi^2\nu/H^2\), and so does \(\nu\,\partial^2/\partial y^2\). Each term vanishes at \(s=0\) and \(s=1\), so (1.12) gives \(0\) and \(U\) at the walls, and the linear part satisfies (1.11) because its second derivative is zero. At \(t^*=0\) the series is the Fourier series \(\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^k\sin(k\pi s)/k=-\pi s/2\) on \(0\lt s\lt1\) (the Fourier sine series of \(s\) on \(0\lt s\lt1\), whose coefficients are \(2\int_0^1 s\sin(k\pi s)\,\mathrm ds=2(-1)^{k+1}/(k\pi)\) by integration by parts; convergence is taken as given), so the sum equals \(-s\) and \(V_x=0\). The program checks (1.12) against a finite-difference solution of (1.11).

The time scale. Every term of (1.12) decays as \(e^{-k^2\pi^2t^*}\); the slowest term, \(k=1\), has the time constant \(H^2/(\pi^2\nu)\), which is 0.118 s for water at 300 K and 6.5 ms for air at 300 K in the 1 mm gap. The velocity at the mid-plane, \(s=\tfrac12\), reaches 99 % of its steady value \(U/2\) at \(t^*=0.4911\), found by bisection in the program. With \(H=1\ \mathrm{mm}\) that is 0.573 s for water and 0.0313 s for air. The velocity disturbance therefore spreads across the gap over a finite settling time. It also shows that the time scale is a property of the fluid and the gap together, \(H^2/\nu\), and that it does not depend on \(U\).

Dimensional check. The only length that \(\nu\) and \(t\) form is \(\sqrt{\nu t}\), so momentum spreads a distance of that order. The distance reaches the gap \(H\) at \(t\sim H^2/\nu\), which is 1.17 s for water and 0.064 s for air. The exact time to settle at the mid-plane, \(0.49\,H^2/\nu\), has the same form and a different coefficient. A dimensional argument fixes the dependence on \(\nu\) and \(H\) and cannot supply the coefficient, which depends on the criterion (99 % here) and on the position.

Boundary. Equation (1.12) is a consequence of (A1)–(A4) and of no slip, so it holds only for a Newtonian fluid with constant properties, in a gap wide enough for the continuum description, and in a flow that stays parallel to the plates. It ignores the heat that viscous work releases, which changes \(\mu\) in a real viscometer at large \(U\). It does not say that the flow stays laminar: whether a parallel flow of this kind stays steady at larger speeds is a question of stability that the lecture raises for the cylinder and does not answer.

Fluids that are not Newtonian L2 39:02

Newton's law (1.8) holds for a class of fluids that the lecture calls Newtonian: those in which the shear stress is directly proportional to the velocity gradient. The speaker says that this is "a very large class" and that water, glycerin, oil and air "could be idealized as Newtonian". The lecture supplies no measurements for these fluids; their Newtonian behaviour is the speaker's idealisation and is an untested modelling assumption here. A direct test of the model with the plates is to measure the steady force per unit area at several speeds \(U\), as in (1.10): a Newtonian fluid gives the same \(\mu=\tau H/U\) at every speed.

To show other behaviours, the board plots the shear stress \(\tau\) on the vertical axis against the strain rate \(\mathrm dV_x/\mathrm dy\) on the horizontal axis, and draws a family of curves. The Newtonian fluid is a straight line through the origin with slope \(\mu\). An ideal fluid lies on the horizontal axis: whatever the strain rate, it produces no shear stress. An ideal solid lies on the vertical axis: it carries a stress with no strain rate, because it has a strain and no strain rate. An ideal plastic starts at a positive stress on the vertical axis and rises little from there: it needs a threshold stress before it flows. Curves of other shapes that start at the origin and bend above or below the straight line are, in the speaker's words, collectively non-Newtonian; the board names none of them, and the speaker states that analysing them is very difficult in this course. A real fluid lies somewhere between the ideal fluid and the ideal solid.

The apparent viscosity \(\mu_{\mathrm{app}}=\tau/(\mathrm dV_x/\mathrm dy)\), the slope of the line from the origin to a point of the curve, makes the difference measurable: it is the same at every strain rate for a Newtonian fluid, and it varies with the strain rate for the curved lines. The name and definition are this chapter's. Exercise 4 computes it for a material with a threshold.

Forces on a fluid element and the stress tensor L2 43:52

The lecture turns to analysis with Newton's second law, \(\mathbf F=m\mathbf a\), for a fluid element of mass \(m\) acted on by the force \(\mathbf F\) with acceleration \(\mathbf a\). The board splits the force into two kinds. A body force acts throughout the volume of the element; the board lists gravity and an electromagnetic potential as examples and writes that it "acts per unit volume" (a force per unit volume, as the board uses it). A surface force acts on the surface of the element; the board lists pressure and viscous forces. The speaker says that body forces are familiar and that the surface force needs a framework. The framework is the stress.

The stress tensor. The board recalls that a surface element has an area vector \(\mathrm d\mathbf A=\hat{\mathbf n}\,|\mathrm d\mathbf A|\), with \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) the unit normal. A force per unit area is a stress, but in a fluid the force on a surface depends on the orientation of the surface, so one number cannot describe it. In the notation translated below, the surface-force relation is

\[ \mathrm d\mathbf F=\mathrm d\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf T, \qquad \mathrm dF_j=\sum_iT_{ij}\,\mathrm dA_i, \tag{1.13} \]

where \(\mathbf T\) is a tensor with two indices, written as a three-by-three array \(T_{xx},T_{xy},T_{xz};\ T_{yx},T_{yy},T_{yz};\ T_{zx},T_{zy},T_{zz}\). The diagonal entries are normal stresses, forces per unit area normal to their face; the off-diagonal entries are shear stresses, forces per unit area tangent to their face. The board writes \(\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A\) without naming the face index. This chapter translates its total stress to \(\mathbf T\) and uses the book's convention: the first index names the surface normal and the second names the force component. Thus \(T_{ij}\) is the \(j\)-component of force per unit area on the positive \(i\) face, exerted by material on the side to which the normal points. Equation (1.13) sums over the face index and does not assume symmetry. For off-diagonal entries write \(\tau_{ij}=T_{ij}\); the scalar \(\tau\) in (1.8) is \(T_{yx}=\tau_{yx}\), the \(x\)-force on the positive \(y\) face. The program uses this convention too. The lecture states that the tensor has nine components and, being symmetric, only six independent ones.

The symmetry, derived. In the book's notation, the board's statement "is symmetric for equilibrium" reads \(T_{ij}=T_{ji}\), and the speaker says that he is not showing the proof but that it can be proved. The following derivation is this chapter's. Take a cube of fluid of side \(\delta\), with its centre at a point and its edges along the axes. Consider the moments about the axis through the centre parallel to \(z\). The faces normal to \(x\), at \(x=\pm\delta/2\), carry the forces \(\pm T_{xy}\delta^2\) in the \(y\)-direction (the sign comes from the outward normal \(\pm\hat{\mathbf x}\) in (1.13)), and their lever arms are \(\pm\delta/2\); the two contribute a moment of \(T_{xy}\delta^3\) about the axis in the \(+z\) sense. The faces normal to \(y\) carry \(\pm T_{yx}\delta^2\) in the \(x\)-direction at the arms \(\pm\delta/2\), and contribute \(-T_{yx}\delta^3\). The normal stresses act along lines through the centre. The forces on the two faces normal to \(z\) act at points on the axis, and the \(z\)-forces on the four other faces are parallel to it. None contributes a moment about the \(z\)-axis. The net moment of the uniform part of the stress is

\[ (T_{xy}-T_{yx})\,\delta^3 . \tag{1.14} \]

The stress varies from face to face, and the variation changes each face force by an amount of order \(\delta^3\) acting at an arm of order \(\delta\), so the correction to (1.14) is of order \(\delta^4\); a body force acting at the centre of mass gives no moment, and a body force that does not act exactly there gives a moment of order \(\delta^4\). The moment of inertia of the cube about the axis is \(\rho\delta^3\cdot\delta^2/6=\rho\delta^5/6\). If \(\alpha_z\) is the angular acceleration, the balance of angular momentum is

\[ (T_{xy}-T_{yx})\,\delta^3+O(\delta^4)=\frac{\rho\,\delta^5}{6}\,\alpha_z . \tag{1.15} \]

Divide by \(\delta^3\): \(T_{xy}-T_{yx}=O(\delta)\) if \(\alpha_z\) stays bounded, so the difference tends to zero as the cube shrinks to a point. The other two pairs follow from the other two axes. The hinge is the scaling: the moment of the stress difference is proportional to the volume \(\delta^3\), and the inertia is proportional to \(\delta^5\), so inertia cannot balance a nonzero stress difference as \(\delta\to0\). The result needs two hypotheses: the angular acceleration is bounded, and the fluid carries no distributed couples (for example from small internal magnets). A fluid with such couples can have an unsymmetric stress. The program checks the algebra of (1.14) for a uniform unsymmetric stress by summing the forces on the six faces; it does not test the scaling argument.

What the lecture does not reach. The lecture states that the surface force on an element requires the stress at a point dotted with the area vector, and it stops there. It does not give the pressure as a stress, nor does it assemble the force balance into an equation of motion; this chapter does neither. The lecture lists pressure among the surface forces and does not define it further, and this chapter does not define it either.

    """Air at temperature T (K) and pressure p (Pa): returns n (1/m^3),

            return name
    return "free molecular"

def series(s, t_nd, terms=200):
    """V/U at s = y/H and t_nd = nu t/H^2 > 0, V_t = nu V_yy,
    V(0) = 0, V(H) = U, V = 0 at t = 0 (separation of variables)."""
    k = np.arange(1, terms + 1)
    return s + 2/np.pi*np.sum((-1.0)**k/k*np.sin(k*np.pi*s)
                              * np.exp(-(k*np.pi)**2*t_nd))

        mid = (lo + hi)/2
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if series(0.5, mid) < frac/2 else (lo, mid)
    return (lo + hi)/2

What the chapter establishes and what it does not

The definition of a fluid, the continuum hypothesis and the Knudsen number are definitions and a hypothesis. The Knudsen-number thresholds are empirical, taken from one preprint that attributes them to earlier work, and a different source may place them differently. The mean free path (1.4) is a result for a dilute gas of hard spheres; the value at 130 km rests on the Standard Atmosphere's collision diameter above the height where the standard says that diameter begins to change. The error analysis of the density estimate assumes independent molecule positions and a smooth variation of the density, so it models a dilute gas and gives a model number for a liquid. Newton's law of viscosity (1.8) is a model; the chapter has no measurement of it. The diffusion equation (1.11) and its solution (1.12) are derived from (A1)–(A4) and hold only under them. The symmetry of the stress tensor holds under the two hypotheses of its derivation. The statements about the cylinder wake, the boundary layer and the streamlined bodies are the lecture's and the slide captions', apart from the critical Reynolds number, which comes from a preprint's table and varies by about 4 % between its sources.

The chapter does not treat the pressure, the equations of motion, the Reynolds number as a ratio of forces, compressibility, heat transfer or the non-Newtonian laws; it names them where the lecture does.

The reader can now test a gas-flow continuum approximation with \(\mathrm{Kn}\), estimate a density-averaging window from (1.6)–(1.7), compute plate shear stress from (1.10), and calculate settling times from (1.11)–(1.12). The stress components specify forces on oriented faces, and the shrinking-cube balance explains why the tensor is symmetric when the acceleration is bounded and distributed couples are absent.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive). (a) Derive (1.3), the mean free path for stationary targets, from the sweep argument of section "Mean free path and the Knudsen number in numbers". (b) Evaluate it for \(\sigma=3.65\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm m\) in air at 300 K and 101325 Pa, compare with (1.4), and compute how many collisions a molecule has while it crosses the 1 mm gap in a straight line. (c) Name one assumption of the derivation that fails in a dense gas.

Solution

(a) A molecule moves a distance \(\ell\) in a straight line. Another molecule collides with it if its centre lies within \(\sigma\) of the line, that is inside a cylinder of cross-section \(\pi\sigma^2\) and length \(\ell\). The expected number of centres in that volume is \(n\pi\sigma^2\ell\) if the centres are placed independently with density \(n\). The mean free path is the length for which the expected number of collisions is one: \(n\pi\sigma^2\lambda_0=1\), so \(\lambda_0=1/(n\pi\sigma^2)\).

(b) With \(n=2.446\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), \(n\pi\sigma^2=1.024\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\) and \(\lambda_0=9.77\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\). Equation (1.4) divides this by \(\sqrt2\): \(6.91\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\), the value in the text. A molecule crosses 1 mm in \(10^{-3}/6.906\times10^{-8}=1.4\times10^4\) free paths, so it has about 14,000 collisions. This is the number behind the statement that \(\mathrm{Kn}=6.9\times10^{-5}\) is small.

(c) The argument assumes that the target centres are independent and that the swept cylinders do not overlap. In a dense gas or a liquid the volume of the molecules is a noticeable fraction of the volume, the centres cannot be closer than \(\sigma\), and the expected number \(n\pi\sigma^2\ell\) over-counts. Here \(n\sigma^3=1.2\times10^{-3}\) for air at 300 K and 101325 Pa, so the assumption holds well.

(a) The number density of water is \(n_w=996.556/0.018015\times6.022\times10^{23}=3.33\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), the spacing is \(n_w^{-1/3}=0.311\ \mathrm{nm}\). One per cent needs \(\bar N=10^4\), so the side is \((10^4/n_w)^{1/3}=6.7\ \mathrm{nm}\), which is 21.5 spacings. (b) For air the side is 74 nm and the spacing is 3.44 nm, again 21.5 spacings. (c) The ratio is the same because \(\bar N^{1/3}=21.5\) in both: in units of the spacing, the lower bound depends only on the tolerance. The numbers 6.7 nm and 74 nm differ because the spacings differ. For air, 74 nm is also about one mean free path (1.07). For water it is not a measured limit: (1.6) assumes independent positions, and the positions in a liquid are strongly correlated, so the true fluctuation of the count differs from the Poisson value in a way this chapter does not compute.

Exercise 4 (counterexample). A student writes: "A fluid at rest carries no shear stress, so any material that flows has no shear stress at rest." A paste obeys the following model, which is not from the lecture: it does not deform for \(\tau\le\tau_y\), and for \(\tau\gt\tau_y\) the stress is \(\tau=\tau_y+\mu_p\,\mathrm dV_x/\mathrm dy\), with \(\tau_y=10\ \mathrm{Pa}\) and \(\mu_p=0.1\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\). (a) Show that the paste contradicts the student's claim. (b) The paste fills the running gap \(H=1\ \mathrm{mm}\) between plates and the upper plate moves at 0.1 m/s. Find the steady shear stress and the apparent viscosity. (c) Repeat for \(U=0.001\ \mathrm{m/s}\). (d) What do the two apparent viscosities say about Newton's law for this paste?

Solution

(a) Below \(\tau_y=10\ \mathrm{Pa}\) the paste does not deform, so it sits at rest with a shear stress of up to 10 Pa. It also flows when \(\tau\gt\tau_y\). So it is a material that flows and that carries a shear stress at rest, and the student's claim is false for it. The claim follows from the definition of a fluid, and the paste is a fluid only above its threshold.

(b) The strain rate is \(U/H=100\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The stress is the same at every height in the steady plate flow, so every layer flows: \(\tau=10+0.1\times100=20\ \mathrm{Pa}\), and \(\mu_{\mathrm{app}}=20/100=0.2\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\), twice \(\mu_p\). (c) The strain rate is \(1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(\tau=10+0.1\times1=10.1\ \mathrm{Pa}\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{app}}=10.1\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\). (d) The apparent viscosity differs by a factor of 50 between the two speeds, so \(\tau\) is not proportional to the strain rate and Newton's law does not describe the paste. A single \(\mu\) would predict the same \(\mu_{\mathrm{app}}\) at both speeds. The paste is the board's ideal plastic with a linear rise after the threshold.

Exercise 5 (design). A sensor estimates the density of air at 300 K and 101325 Pa from the molecules counted in a cube of side \(\ell\). The density varies along one direction as in (1.7) with \(\varepsilon=0.1\) over a length \(L=2\ \mathrm{mm}\). The design requires a noise below 0.5 % and a bias below 0.5 %. (a) Find the smallest side. (b) Find the largest side. (c) Is there a window, and what side minimises the total error?

Solution

(a) Noise below 0.5 % needs \(\bar N\ge1/0.005^2=4\times10^4\), so \(\ell\ge(4\times10^4/2.446\times10^{25})^{1/3}=1.18\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm m\). (b) The bias (1.7) is below 0.5 % when \(\varepsilon\,[1-\operatorname{sinc}(\ell/L)]/(1+\varepsilon)\lt0.005\). Solving numerically gives \(\ell/L\lt0.184\), so \(\ell\lt0.369\ \mathrm{mm}\). (c) The window runs from 0.118 µm to 369 µm, a factor of 3100, so it exists. The total error is smallest where the derivative of its square vanishes. Using the small-\(\varepsilon\) approximation to the noise and the small-\(\ell/L\) form of the bias, the noise variance \(1/(n\ell^3)\) is \(4/3\) of the squared bias at the approximate minimum. With the small-\(\ell\) form of (1.7), \(b=c\ell^2\) with \(c=\varepsilon\pi^2/[6(1+\varepsilon)L^2]=3.74\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}\), the derivative of \(1/(n\ell^3)+c^2\ell^4\) vanishes at \(\ell^7=3/(4nc^2)\), which gives \(\ell\approx11\ \mu\mathrm m\) and a total error of about \(7\times10^{-6}\). The design is limited by neither bound. The requirement that the cube be larger than 0.118 µm is about \(1.7\lambda\), the same size as the mean free path, and at the optimum \(\ell\ll L\) holds with a margin of 180.

Exercise 6 (compute and judge). A technician measures the viscosity of water at 300 K with the running gap (\(H=1\ \mathrm{mm}\)), starting the upper plate at 0.1 m/s and reading the shear stress on the fixed plate 0.2 s later, then using \(\mu=\tau H/U\) as in (1.10). (a) Differentiate (1.12) to find the stress on the fixed plate as a function of time. (b) What fraction of the steady stress does it reach at 0.2 s, and what viscosity does the technician infer? (c) How long must the technician wait for the stress to be within 1 % of the steady value? How long for air?

Solution

(a) The stress on the fixed plate is \(\tau(0,t)=\mu\,\partial V_x/\partial y|_{y=0}\). Differentiating (1.12) with respect to \(s\) at \(s=0\), the derivative of \(\sin(k\pi s)\) is \(k\pi\), and the factor \((2/\pi)(1/k)\) leaves 2: \(\tau(0,t)=(\mu U/H)\bigl[1+2\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^ke^{-k^2\pi^2t^*}\bigr]\). The expression is valid for \(t\gt0\); the bracket rises toward 1 as \(t\) grows.

(b) At 0.2 s, \(t^*=\nu t/H^2=8.567\times10^{-7}\times0.2/10^{-6}=0.1713\). The \(k=1\) term is \(e^{-\pi^2t^*}=0.1843\), the \(k=2\) term is \(1.2\times10^{-3}\), and the others are smaller. The bracket is \(1-2(0.1843)+2(0.0012)=0.634\). The reading is 0.634 of the steady value, \(0.634\times0.0854=0.0541\ \mathrm{Pa}\), and the inferred viscosity is \(5.4\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\), 37 % too low.

(c) The bracket reaches 0.99 when \(2e^{-\pi^2t^*}\approx0.01\), \(t^*=\ln200/\pi^2=0.5368\), so \(t=0.5368\,H^2/\nu=0.627\ \mathrm s\) for water; the higher terms change this by less than \(10^{-6}\). The wall stress needs 0.627 s, a little longer than the 0.573 s of the mid-plane velocity. For air, \(t=0.5368\times10^{-6}/1.5689\times10^{-5}=0.034\ \mathrm s\). A viscometer with a gap of 1 mm therefore needs a wait of the order of a second for water, and the time grows as \(H^2\): a 10 mm gap needs 100 times as long.
