The boundary layer

A 0.50 m long, 0.40 m wide plate is placed parallel to a 10 m/s stream. Suppose the fluid has density 1.20 kg/m³ and kinematic viscosity 1.50 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s, and the flow remains laminar. The layer slowed by one face is only about 4.25 mm thick at the trailing edge, yet that face experiences 0.0276 N of frictional drag. Setting viscosity to zero everywhere would predict zero tangential force and would fail the wall condition. Keeping viscosity everywhere requires a much harder flow calculation. The boundary-layer approximation resolves this conflict by retaining the terms that become large close to the wall. Its success depends on identifying which small quantities multiply large gradients.

Grounded in Lecture 32A and Lecture 32B of Prof. Sanjay Mittal's NPTEL Fluid Mechanics course, IIT Kanpur. Lecture material: the flat-plate and airfoil examples; inner and outer flow; thin-layer scaling, pressure matching, equations and boundary conditions; flat-plate similarity, local stress and integrated drag; transition, roughness and turbulent profiles; displacement and momentum thickness; the stated momentum-integral equation; adverse pressure gradients and separation. Research extensions: Prandtl's original thin-layer argument in the 1928 English translation of his 1904 paper; Blasius's flat-plate reduction and separation discussion in the 1950 English translation of his 1908 paper. Original teaching constructions: the numerical plate, the complete similarity and momentum-integral derivations, numerical solution and convergence checks, computed figures, error examples and six exercises. The fluid properties are prescribed model inputs, not a measured state or a standard-atmosphere claim. An inner viscous flow matched to an outer flow L32A 0:29

Place the origin at the leading edge of a flat plate. The coordinate \(x\) (m) runs downstream along the wall and \(y\) (m) points into the fluid, with the wall at \(y=0\). The velocity components are \(V_x(x,y)\) and \(V_y(x,y)\), in m/s; pressure is \(p(x,y)\), in Pa. Density \(\rho\) is constant, dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) has units Pa s, and kinematic viscosity is \(\nu=\mu/\rho\), in m²/s. The running plate has length \(L=0.50\) m and span \(b=0.40\) m. Its prescribed properties give \(\mu=1.80\times10^{-5}\) Pa s.

The formal model treats the fluid as a continuum: velocity, pressure and stress, a force per unit area, are fields averaged over molecular motion. All resolved flow lengths are assumed large compared with the molecular transport scale. The flow is steady, so its fields do not change with time at a fixed position. It is two-dimensional: the third velocity component is zero and no field depends on the spanwise coordinate. Density and viscosity are constant. The fluid is Newtonian, meaning that its viscous stress depends linearly on the local rate of change of shape; in simple shear, the shear stress is dynamic viscosity times the transverse velocity gradient. The corresponding continuum momentum equations are stated below as the governing model.

The wall is stationary and impermeable, so its normal fluid velocity is zero. The empirical no-slip boundary condition sets the tangential fluid velocity equal to the wall velocity, also zero here. There are no body forces. For the principal calculation the flow is laminar, meaning that an instantaneous smooth velocity field describes the transport without turbulent fluctuations. The plate is aligned with a uniform outer stream of speed \(U=10\) m/s. Finite-span side effects and leading-edge geometry are excluded. The calculation concerns one wetted face and positions sufficiently far from the leading edge for a thin layer to exist.

A boundary layer is the near-wall region in which viscosity makes a leading contribution to the momentum balance, even when viscous forces are small over the body's full length. Outside it the leading momentum equation is Euler's inviscid equation, which neglects viscous forces. Inviscid does not mean irrotational. Only if that outer velocity also has zero vorticity, the curl of velocity, may it be represented locally by a velocity potential \(\phi\), with \(\mathbf V=\nabla\phi\); constant-density continuity then gives \(\nabla^2\phi=0\). No potential-flow result is needed for the running plate: its prescribed outer velocity is simply uniform.

For a gently curved body, let \(U_e(x)\) denote the tangential outer speed at the layer's edge and \(p_e(x)\) its pressure. The subscript \(e\) means edge. The wall-normal distance must be small compared with the radius of curvature, and the along-wall flow must change slowly on that distance. These are additional conditions for using the locally flat equations below. On a wing the outer speed is generally not constant, so \(U_e\) replaces \(U\). A flat wall can also have a pressure gradient if the external flow accelerates or decelerates; flatness alone does not set the gradient to zero.

The inner and outer descriptions must agree in their common region of validity. This matching supplies the inner flow's pressure and edge speed. The outer solution uses impermeability at leading order but cannot enforce no slip. The inner solution supplies the adjustment from zero wall speed to the outer speed. Prandtl, NACA TM 452, pp. 4–5 (PDF pp. 6–7), identifies precisely this thin transition and the pressure imposed by the free fluid. In his notation the viscosity constant is \(k\); here it is \(\mu\).

Thin-layer ordering: a small normal velocity still transports momentum L32A 9:18

The steady equations under the stated hypotheses are continuity and the two Cartesian momentum equations. A subscript after a comma in the following equations denotes differentiation, so \(V_{x,y}=\partial V_x/\partial y\) and \(V_{x,yy}=\partial^2V_x/\partial y^2\):

\[ V_{x,x}+V_{y,y}=0,\qquad V_xV_{x,x}+V_yV_{x,y}=-\frac{p_{,x}}{\rho} +\nu(V_{x,xx}+V_{x,yy}),\qquad V_xV_{y,x}+V_yV_{y,y}=-\frac{p_{,y}}{\rho} +\nu(V_{y,xx}+V_{y,yy}). \tag{16.1} \]

Choose an along-wall length \(L\), a representative outer speed \(U\), and a scaling thickness \(\delta_s\). The latter is an order-of-magnitude scale, not a particular experimental edge criterion. Write \(\epsilon=\delta_s/L\), assume \(\epsilon\ll1\), and use the dimensionless coordinates \(X=x/L\), \(Y=y/\delta_s\). Suppose the dimensionless streamwise velocity and its derivatives have ordinary finite size on these scales. Continuity compares \(U/L\) with the normal-velocity scale divided by \(\delta_s\), so the normal scale is \(\epsilon U\). Define \(u=V_x/U\), \(v=V_y/(\epsilon U)\) and \(P=(p-p_0)/(\rho U^2)\), where \(p_0\) is an arbitrary constant reference pressure.

This choice makes continuity \(u_{,X}+v_{,Y}=0\). It does not require each derivative to be nonzero at every point; it describes the general developing layer. Crucially, the small velocity \(V_y\) multiplies a large normal gradient. The scales of both convective terms are \(V_xV_{x,x}\sim U^2/L\) and \(V_yV_{x,y}\sim(\epsilon U)(U/\delta_s)=U^2/L\). Dropping the second term solely because \(V_y\ll U\) destroys the leading momentum balance.

Define the length-based Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_L=UL/\nu\). Divide the streamwise equation by \(U^2/L\) and the normal equation by \(\epsilon U^2/L\):

\[ u u_{,X}+v u_{,Y}=-P_{,X} +\frac1{\mathrm{Re}_L}u_{,XX} +\frac1{\mathrm{Re}_L\epsilon^2}u_{,YY}, \tag{16.2} \] \[ u v_{,X}+v v_{,Y}=-\frac1{\epsilon^2}P_{,Y} +\frac1{\mathrm{Re}_L}v_{,XX} +\frac1{\mathrm{Re}_L\epsilon^2}v_{,YY}. \tag{16.3} \]

Both momentum equations have the same transverse-viscous coefficient after these divisions. The force scale chosen for the normal equation is smaller, but its velocity scale is smaller by the same factor. This is a useful dimensional check on (16.3).

Inside a laminar layer, transverse viscous diffusion must balance convection. Setting \(1/(\mathrm{Re}_L\epsilon^2)\) to order unity gives

\[ \frac{\delta_s}{L}\sim\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/2},\qquad \frac{V_y}{U}\sim\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/2}. \tag{16.4} \]

Streamwise viscous diffusion is smaller than transverse diffusion by \(\epsilon^2\). For the running plate \(\mathrm{Re}_L=3.3333\times10^5\), \(\delta_s=\sqrt{\nu L/U}=0.8660\) mm and the normal-speed scale is 0.01732 m/s. The discarded diffusion coefficient is \(3.0\times10^{-6}\). These are scale estimates; neither 0.8660 mm nor that coefficient is an error bound on a finite plate. The actual 99% thickness has a numerical prefactor determined by the velocity profile.

In (16.3) the convection and transverse-viscous terms are of ordinary size, whereas \(1/\epsilon^2\) is large. The notation \(O(\epsilon^2)\) denotes a quantity bounded in magnitude by a constant times \(\epsilon^2\) as \(\epsilon\to0\). Thus \(P_{,Y}=O(\epsilon^2)\): over a layer whose scaled thickness is ordinary, \(\Delta p/(\rho U^2)=O(\epsilon^2)\), where \(\Delta p\) is the pressure difference across that layer. Pressure is uniform across the layer to leading order. It need not be uniform downstream. In the running scaling, \(\rho U^2\epsilon^2=3.6\times10^{-4}\) Pa characterizes the small normal pressure correction, not a computed measured pressure difference. Blasius, NACA TM 1256, pp. 3–4 (PDF pp. 4–5), gives the velocity-gradient ordering and the imposed outer pressure. Curved coordinates introduce additional normal-acceleration terms, so this particular \(O(\epsilon^2)\) pressure estimate is for the locally flat ordering used here.

The limit is singular: \(\nu\) becomes small while \(V_{x,yy}\) becomes large, leaving their product finite. Setting \(\nu=0\) before resolving the normal coordinate loses that product. Equation (16.4) is not a universal thickness law for turbulent or separated flows, nor does it establish that a laminar solution is stable.

The boundary-layer equations and their boundary data L32A 32:33

Retaining the leading terms gives the Prandtl boundary-layer equations:

\[ V_{x,x}+V_{y,y}=0,\qquad V_xV_{x,x}+V_yV_{x,y}=-\frac1\rho\frac{dp_e}{dx} +\nu V_{x,yy},\qquad p(x,y)=p_e(x). \tag{16.5} \]

Along the outer streamline next to the layer, steady inviscid momentum without body force gives \(U_e\,dU_e/dx=-(1/\rho)dp_e/dx\). This is the tangential component of acceleration equals pressure force per unit mass; it requires no assumption that the entire outer flow is irrotational. Substitution makes the driving term of (16.5) \(U_e\,dU_e/dx\). For the running case \(U_e=U\) is constant and \(dp_e/dx=0\).

The stationary impermeable wall requires \(V_x(x,0)=V_y(x,0)=0\). Matching requires \(V_x(x,y)\to U_e(x)\) in the outer limit of the inner solution. The conventional notation \(y\to\infty\) for this condition means far compared with the layer thickness, within an overlap where the outer field varies little in the normal direction. It does not require the locally flat inner equations to describe arbitrarily distant space.

The normal velocity is determined by continuity and the wall condition; it must be matched to the outer flow rather than set to zero at the layer edge. For a developing layer it is generally nonzero there. An upstream profile is also needed for a downstream calculation. The ideal flat-plate similarity problem takes uniform incident fluid at the sharp leading edge; a general calculation started at \(x=x_0\gt0\) instead needs a specified compatible profile \(V_x(x_0,y)\). Without such data the equations do not determine a unique developing layer.

The 99% thickness \(\delta_{99}(x)\) is defined for a monotone attached profile by \(V_x(x,\delta_{99})=0.99U_e(x)\). It is a reporting convention extracted from a solution. Replacing matching at infinity by an exact boundary at an unknown \(\delta_{99}\) is not equivalent. In particular, imposing both velocity components equal to 99% of a uniform outer velocity would incorrectly force \(V_y=0\). The chapter uses the scalar tangential-speed criterion. The infinite-limit boundary condition is also the one written in Blasius, TM 1256, p. 4 (PDF p. 5).

A flat plate: derive the similarity problem L32A 36:58

The same profile shape at different downstream positions would reduce two independent variables to one. For uniform \(U\), the local balance suggests the normal scale \(\sqrt{\nu x/U}\). Define the dimensionless similarity coordinate \(\eta=y\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\), for \(x\gt0\). Introduce a stream function \(\psi(x,y)\), in m²/s, by \(V_x=\partial\psi/\partial y\), \(V_y=-\partial\psi/\partial x\). Equality of mixed derivatives enforces continuity exactly. Seek

\[ \psi=\sqrt{\nu Ux}\,F(\eta),\qquad V_x=UF'(\eta),\qquad V_y=\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\nu U}{x}} \left(\eta F'-F\right). \tag{16.6} \]

Here \(F\) is a dimensionless function and a prime means differentiation with respect to \(\eta\). The identities use \(\partial\eta/\partial x=-\eta/(2x)\) and \(\partial\eta/\partial y=\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\). No slip gives \(F'(0)=0\); impermeability gives \(F(0)=0\). Matching gives \(F'(\infty)=1\). The board's similarity coordinate is \(y/\delta_{99}\) and its plotted function is \(V_x/U\); here those are \(\eta/\eta_{99}\) and \(F'\). The separate capital \(F\) prevents confusing a velocity profile with its stream-function integral.

The consequential cancellation can be checked before solving anything. From (16.6),

\[ V_xV_{x,x}=-\frac{U^2}{2x}\eta F'F'',\qquad V_yV_{x,y}=\frac{U^2}{2x}(\eta F'-F)F'',\qquad \nu V_{x,yy}=\frac{U^2}{x}F''' . \]

The two terms involving \(\eta F'F''\) cancel, leaving the ordinary differential equation

\[ F'''+\frac12FF''=0,\qquad F(0)=F'(0)=0,\qquad F'(\infty)=1. \tag{16.7} \]

Equation (16.7) and its boundary conditions define the Blasius similarity solution. They are a derived reduction of the approximate equations, not an exact solution of the full Navier–Stokes equations. Blasius's original calculation, pp. 6–8 (PDF pp. 7–9), introduces the stream function and reduces the flat-plate equations. His variables differ: his coordinate \(\xi=\eta/2\) and function \(\zeta(\xi)=F(2\xi)\) give \(\zeta\zeta''=-\zeta'''\) and \(\zeta'(\infty)=2\). This change of normalization explains the different coefficients; neither equation contradicts the other.

Three boundary conditions belong to a third-order equation, but the last is at infinity. A numerical shooting calculation guesses the missing initial slope \(a=F''(0)\), integrates outward, and changes \(a\) until \(F'\) approaches 1. The computation below finds \(a=0.332057336\) and \(\eta_{99}=4.909997\), hence \(\delta_{99}=4.910\sqrt{\nu x/U}\). The leading-edge limit deserves care: thickness tends to zero as \(x\to0\), while wall gradients diverge. There the thin-layer assumption relative to \(x\) fails. The similarity solution describes the downstream high-local-Reynolds-number region, with an idealized leading-edge limit.

Figure 16.1 plots the computed physical profiles at two positions on the running plate. When \(x\) increases fourfold, every fixed fraction of the outer speed occurs at twice the height. The same scale factor applies to the whole profile, which is what self-similarity asserts.

Local stress and the drag integrated over the plate L32A 42:08

Let \(\tau_w(x)=\mu V_{x,y}(x,0)\) be the signed tangential force per unit area exerted by fluid on the lower wall, positive downstream. For attached forward flow it is positive. The wall exerts \(-\tau_w\) on the fluid. In the book's face-first stress convention the interior shear is \(T_{yx}=\mu(V_{x,y}+V_{y,x})\); at the stationary straight wall \(V_y(x,0)=0\) for every \(x\), so \(V_{y,x}=0\) there. This makes the definition exact at the wall for this Newtonian model and resolves the force sign.

Differentiate (16.6), and define the local Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_x=Ux/\nu\) and the local skin-friction coefficient \(C_f(x)=\tau_w(x)/(\rho U^2/2)\). Then

\[ \tau_w=\rho U^2\frac{a}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}},\qquad C_f(x)=\frac{2a}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}} \simeq\frac{0.664115}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}}. \tag{16.8} \]

The stress falls downstream because the velocity adjustment spreads over a thicker layer. At \(x=L\), the running plate has \(C_f(L)=0.0011503\) and \(\tau_w(L)=0.06902\) Pa. Dynamic pressure is \(\rho U^2/2=60\) Pa. These values concern one point on the plate; multiplying the trailing-edge stress by the whole area underestimates the drag.

The one-face force \(F_D\) is \(b\int_0^L\tau_w(x)\,dx\). Define the area-averaged coefficient \(\overline C_f=F_D/(\rho U^2bL/2)\), which the board calls the plate drag coefficient \(C_D\). Since \(\int_0^Lx^{-1/2}dx=2\sqrt L\),

\[ \overline C_f=\frac{4a}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_L}} =2C_f(L),\qquad F_D=2a\rho bU^{3/2}\sqrt{\nu L}. \tag{16.9} \]

The integrable leading-edge singularity gives a finite force. For the running plate, \(\overline C_f=0.0023006\) and \(F_D=0.027607\) N. Using \(C_f(L)\) as the mean would give half that force. Two identical exposed faces double the force; the coefficient also doubles if the reference area remains the single planform area \(bL\), but stays the same if the reference area becomes the total wetted area \(2bL\). An area convention must accompany a drag coefficient.

The region in which the near-edge approximation fails is excluded from a pointwise accuracy claim, even though the ideal integral starts at zero. The formal fraction of drag from \(0\lt x\lt x_0\) is \(\sqrt{x_0/L}\). Thus a small fractional length can contribute a less small fraction of drag: a 1% leading segment supplies 10% in this model. A finite leading-edge shape or nonuniform inlet requires a separate estimate there.

Transition and the limits of turbulent power laws L32A 46:36

A large Reynolds number permits a thin laminar layer but does not guarantee that the actual layer stays laminar. Transition is the development of irregular fluctuations that produce a turbulent flow. Surface roughness, free-stream disturbances and vibrations can move its location. There is no transition Reynolds number derived by the steady similarity calculation. Consequently the running plate's laminar state is a stated hypothesis, not a conclusion from \(\mathrm{Re}_L\) alone.

For a turbulent layer the averaged velocity is \(\overline V_x\), where the overbar means a time average over the fluctuations. Momentum transport by those fluctuations changes the mean equation; (16.7) cannot describe it. The mean profile is typically fuller, with higher speed through much of its thickness, and its wall gradient can be larger despite its greater thickness. Molecular viscosity still determines the wall shear, \(\tau_w=\mu\,\partial\overline V_x/\partial y\) at a smooth stationary wall: the fluctuations vanish at that wall. It is the altered gradient, rather than a changed molecular viscosity, that raises skin friction.

The approximate lecture expressions for a smooth plate, with a turbulent layer idealized as starting at its leading edge, are

\[ \frac{\overline V_x}{U}\approx\left(\frac y{\delta_t}\right)^{1/7},\qquad \frac{\delta_t}{x}\approx0.35\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{-1/5},\qquad \overline C_{f,t}\approx0.072\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/5}. \tag{16.10} \]

The symbol \(\delta_t\) denotes the nominal turbulent-layer thickness used by this fit. Interpret the power law as an approximate interior profile for \(0\lt y\le\delta_t\), with a viscous region near the wall requiring a different description. For the idealized deficit calculation in Exercise 4, formally extrapolate the power law to \(y=0\) and set \(\overline V_x/U=1\) for \(y\gt\delta_t\). This piecewise continuation specifies the whole profile needed by the infinite-limit integrals; it is a teaching construction, not a wall-resolved empirical mean profile. The expressions in (16.10) are empirical approximations supplied by the recording, not consequences of the laminar equations. The recording supplies no universal fitted range, and the numerical constants are not independently validated here. They illustrate different trends but do not certify an aircraft drag estimate. A real transition at finite \(x\), pressure gradients, or rough walls changes the problem.

The power law cannot be differentiated at the wall to predict shear: its derivative is proportional to \(y^{-6/7}\), which diverges as \(y\to0\). A viscous region near the wall must replace it. Thus using the first formula to derive a finite \(\tau_w\) is internally inconsistent. The separate drag formula supplies additional empirical information. For illustration at \(\mathrm{Re}_L=10^6\), the lecture formulas give \(\overline C_{f,t}=0.00454\) versus the laminar value 0.001328, a ratio 3.42. This comparison is conditional on two different states, not a prediction that both occur on the same plate.

Keeping a surface clean can delay roughness-triggered transition and reduce skin-friction drag in an attached-flow application. Total drag also includes pressure forces. If transition delays separation, pressure drag may fall enough to outweigh increased friction. The preferred state therefore depends on whether attached skin friction or separated pressure drag controls the design.

Displacement and momentum thickness measure different deficits L32B 0:20

A 99% edge is difficult to locate accurately when the profile is nearly flat. A velocity uncertainty comparable to 1% of \(U_e\) can move the inferred edge substantially. Integral thicknesses answer different, physically defined questions. At a fixed \(x\), compare the measured or calculated profile with uniform speed \(U_e\) through the same plane. Density must be constant across that plane for the following formulas.

The missing mass flux per unit span, relative to the uniform profile, is \(\rho\int_0^\infty(U_e-V_x)\,dy\), in kg/(m s). Removing a strip of uniform flow of thickness \(\delta^*\) removes mass flux \(\rho U_e\delta^*\). Equating them defines the displacement thickness:

\[ \delta^*=\int_0^\infty\left(1-\frac{V_x}{U_e}\right)dy. \tag{16.11} \]

The effective inviscid wall can be displaced outward by \(\delta^*\) to reproduce this mass deficit. This does not mean fluid mass is destroyed: streamlines are displaced and a normal flux accommodates the growing deficit. At constant \(U\), integrate continuity to obtain \(V_y(x,y)=-\partial_x\int_0^yV_x\,dy\). In the overlap, \(\int_0^yV_x\,dy\approx U(y-\delta^*)\), so \(V_y\to U\,d\delta^*/dx\). The growing layer therefore produces a small outward velocity, consistent with the scaling and inconsistent with imposing zero edge normal velocity.

The momentum thickness \(\theta_m\), in metres, compares the actual streamwise momentum with what the same local mass flux would carry at \(U_e\). A strip transports mass \(\rho V_xdy\) per unit time and span; its velocity shortfall is \(U_e-V_x\). Thus the appropriate momentum deficit per unit span is \(\rho\int_0^\infty V_x(U_e-V_x)dy=\rho U_e^2\theta_m\), giving

\[ \theta_m=\int_0^\infty\frac{V_x}{U_e} \left(1-\frac{V_x}{U_e}\right)dy. \tag{16.12} \]

The board uses \(\theta\); the subscript here distinguishes momentum thickness from a coordinate angle. It also avoids confusion with the book's absolute temperature \(\Theta\), which is not used in this model. The simple fixed-plane difference \(\rho\int(U_e^2-V_x^2)dy\) is a different quantity. Factoring its integrand gives \(\rho U_e^2(\delta^*+\theta_m)\). Confusing it with \(\rho U_e^2\theta_m\) ignores the mass-flux change and gives the wrong drag relation.

For the similarity solution, change variable to \(\eta\), so \(dy=\sqrt{\nu x/U}\,d\eta\). Define the dimensionless integrals \(A=\int_0^\infty(1-F')d\eta\) and \(B=\int_0^\infty F'(1-F')d\eta\). The calculation gives \(A\simeq1.72079\), \(B\simeq0.664115\), hence

\[ \delta^*=A\sqrt{\frac{\nu x}{U}},\qquad \theta_m=B\sqrt{\frac{\nu x}{U}},\qquad \delta_{99}=\eta_{99}\sqrt{\frac{\nu x}{U}}. \tag{16.13} \]

At the trailing edge the three thicknesses are 1.4902 mm, 0.5751 mm and 4.2522 mm. The ratio \(\delta^*/\delta_{99}\simeq0.3505\) explains the lecture's rough one-third description for this flat-plate laminar profile. It is not a universal ratio for all boundary layers. Define the shape factor \(H=\delta^*/\theta_m\); here \(H\simeq2.5911\). Different profiles can have the same \(\delta_{99}\) and different deficits.

Figure 16.2 displays the two dimensionless deficit integrands. The displacement integrand is largest at the wall. The momentum integrand vanishes there because stationary fluid transports no mass through the section, and peaks where the speed fraction is one-half. For an attached profile with \(0\le V_x/U_e\le1\), the pointwise inequality between these integrands implies \(0\le\theta_m\le\delta^*\). Extending the integrals to infinity is essential for an asymptotically recovering profile; cutting them exactly at \(\delta_{99}\) loses a small nonzero tail.

Derive the momentum-integral equation L32B 12:02

The integral equation connects wall stress to quantities measurable from an entire profile. Derive it before choosing a guessed profile. Take a fixed height \(h_o\) in the overlap, where \(V_x\approx U_e\) and \(V_{x,y}\approx0\). Define \(I_1(x)=\int_0^{h_o}V_xdy\) and \(I_2(x)=\int_0^{h_o}V_x^2dy\). The first has units m²/s and the second m³/s². Continuity gives \(V_y(x,h_o)=-dI_1/dx\).

Use continuity again to put convection in conservative form: \(V_xV_{x,x}+V_yV_{x,y}=\partial_x(V_x^2)+\partial_y(V_xV_y)\). Integrating (16.5) over the height and using the wall conditions yields

\[ \frac{dI_2}{dx}+U_e V_y(x,h_o) =h_oU_e\frac{dU_e}{dx}-\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}. \]

The viscous integral is \(\nu[V_{x,y}]_0^{h_o}=-\tau_w/\rho\), which fixes the drag sign. Replace the top normal velocity and rearrange:

\[ \frac{\tau_w}{\rho} =h_oU_eU_e'-I_2'+U_eI_1'. \]

The deficit definitions give \(I_1\approx U_e(h_o-\delta^*)\) and \(I_2\approx U_e^2(h_o-\delta^*-\theta_m)\). Substituting and differentiating cancels every term proportional to the arbitrary \(h_o\), as it must. Taking the matched limit gives

\[ \boxed{\frac{\tau_w}{\rho} =\frac{d}{dx}(U_e^2\theta_m)+U_eU_e'\delta^*},\qquad \frac{C_f}{2}=\frac{d\theta_m}{dx} +(2+H)\frac{\theta_m}{U_e}\frac{dU_e}{dx}. \tag{16.14} \]

In the second form \(C_f=\tau_w/(\rho U_e^2/2)\) uses the local edge speed. Expanding the derivative in the first form supplies the factor 2: the change in outer speed alters the momentum deficit as well as the momentum carried by each unit of mass. Both forms follow from the same continuity and momentum balances.

For constant \(U\), (16.14) reduces to \(\tau_w=\rho U^2\theta_m'\). With \(\theta_m=B\sqrt{\nu x/U}\), it predicts \(\tau_w=\rho U^2(B/2)/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}\). Agreement with (16.8) requires \(B=2a\). The numerical quadrature checks this relation to \(1.08\times10^{-6}\) in the dimensionless coefficient. Integrating once more gives \(F_D/b=\rho U^2\theta_m(L)\), since the ideal leading-edge thickness is zero. The running value is \(120\times0.00057514=0.06902\) N/m; multiplying by \(b=0.40\) m reproduces 0.02761 N. This checks a velocity integral against an independently evaluated wall gradient.

Equation (16.14) is not closed: wall stress and profile shape are still unknown. A chosen profile plus a stress relation supplies an approximation, or a solved velocity field supplies all three quantities. The integral equation alone cannot locate separation merely from a specified outer pressure gradient.

Adverse pressure gradients and the onset of reverse flow L32B 13:49

An adverse pressure gradient means \(dp_e/dx\gt0\) when the outer flow is downstream with \(U_e\gt0\). Outer momentum then gives \(U_e'\lt0\): the pressure rise decelerates the outer stream. Fluid close to a no-slip wall has little streamwise momentum, so it can reverse while the outer flow remains forward. An adverse gradient encourages this process but does not guarantee separation at every location.

The local wall expansion explains the signature. Let \(s_w=V_{x,y}(x,0)=\tau_w/\mu\), in s⁻¹. At a stationary wall both convective terms in (16.5) vanish, leaving \(\mu V_{x,yy}(x,0)=dp_e/dx\). Smoothness in \(y\) then gives

\[ V_x(x,y)=s_w y+\frac1{2\mu}\frac{dp_e}{dx}y^2+O(y^3). \tag{16.15} \]

For \(s_w\gt0\) the near-wall flow is forward. At a conventional steady two-dimensional separation point, the signed wall shear passes through zero. With an adverse gradient, the first nonzero term can be positive and quadratic; zero slope at the wall does not mean that the velocity is constant throughout the profile. Downstream, \(s_w\lt0\) gives reversed flow in an immediate neighbourhood of the wall even though the profile may recover to positive outer speed farther away. Blasius, TM 1256, pp. 4–5 (PDF pp. 5–6), discusses adverse-gradient reversal and the zero-wall-gradient signature.

Zero shear is a diagnostic under this steady two-dimensional attached-flow-to-reversal setting, not a universal separation definition. For example, symmetry can give zero shear at an isolated point without a separating recirculation region. Three-dimensional and unsteady separation need a more careful characterization. Near substantial separation, rapid thickening, reverse flow and outer-flow interaction invalidate the simple one-way downstream calculation with an independently prescribed inviscid pressure. The boundary-layer idea can be extended, but the attached equations used here no longer supply the complete solution.

Return to the running case at a station where, in a modified outer flow, \(U_e=10\) m/s and \(U_e'=-5\) s⁻¹. The pressure gradient is \(-\rho U_eU_e'=60\) Pa/m, an adverse gradient. If an independently obtained profile at that station has \(\theta_m=0.575\) mm and \(H=2.59\), its pressure-gradient term in (16.14) is \((2+H)(\theta_m/U_e)U_e'=-0.001320\). To retain positive stress its thickness growth must exceed 0.001320 m per metre. This is a local conditional test, not an assertion that the zero-gradient Blasius profile remains a solution in the modified flow. A zero wall stress would require equality in this integral balance, together with a compatible velocity field.

Turbulent transport can replenish near-wall momentum and permit attachment through a larger adverse gradient than a laminar layer under comparable conditions. This is why deliberately triggering transition can sometimes reduce a bluff body's total drag despite increasing skin friction. Dimpled golf balls illustrate the mechanism in the recording; their quantitative drag, transition locations and separation angles require experiments for the actual geometry and conditions. No universal angle or speed follows from the present plate model.

An executable solution with boundary and convergence checks

The following standard-library program solves (16.7) with fourth-order Runge–Kutta integration and bisection on \(a\). Its inputs are dimensionless integration height and step count; the plate conversion uses positive finite SI inputs, one wetted face, span 0.40 m and the laminar model already defined. It prints the initial slope, integrated deficits, 99% coordinate, edge residual and plate quantities. It writes no files. The finite integration height replaces infinity and is tested by increasing it; the quadrature and threshold interpolation are tested by halving the step. A small shooting residual alone would not test either error.

import math


def integrate(a, end, n):
    step = end / n
    state = (0.0, 0.0, a)
    rows = [(0.0, *state)]

    def rhs(z):
        return (z[1], z[2], -0.5 * z[0] * z[2])

    def advance(z, k, factor):
        return tuple(x + factor * step * v for x, v in zip(z, k))

    for i in range(n):
        k1 = rhs(state)
        k2 = rhs(advance(state, k1, 0.5))
        k3 = rhs(advance(state, k2, 0.5))
        k4 = rhs(advance(state, k3, 1.0))
        state = tuple(x + step * (a1 + 2*a2 + 2*a3 + a4) / 6
                      for x, a1, a2, a3, a4
                      in zip(state, k1, k2, k3, k4))
        rows.append(((i + 1) * step, *state))
    return rows


def solve(end, n):
    lo, hi = 0.2, 0.5
    for _ in range(45):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if integrate(mid, end, n)[-1][2] > 1:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo = mid
    a = (lo + hi) / 2
    rows = integrate(a, end, n)
    step = end / n
    dstar = theta = 0.0
    eta99 = None
    for left, right in zip(rows, rows[1:]):
        u0, u1 = left[2], right[2]
        dstar += step * (2 - u0 - u1) / 2
        theta += step * (u0*(1-u0) + u1*(1-u1)) / 2
        if u0 < 0.99 <= u1:
            eta99 = left[0] + step * (0.99-u0) / (u1-u0)
    return a, dstar, theta, eta99, rows


def plate(speed, length, density, nu, a, eta99):
    if min(speed, length, density, nu) <= 0:
        raise ValueError('positive finite physical inputs required')
    if not all(map(math.isfinite, (speed, length, density, nu))):
        raise ValueError('positive finite physical inputs required')
    re = speed * length / nu
    cf_local = 2 * a / math.sqrt(re)
    cf_mean = 2 * cf_local
    drag = 0.5 * density * speed**2 * length * 0.4 * cf_mean
    return re, 1000 * eta99 * length / math.sqrt(re), drag

The displacement and momentum integrals change by about a factor of four less on each halving of the step, consistent with the trapezoidal rule's second-order error. At height 10, their change between 800 and 1600 steps is about \(3.2\times10^{-6}\); the initial slope is stable to the printed nine decimal places. Raising the height from 10 to 12 changes that slope by less than \(10^{-9}\), whereas using height 8 changes it by about \(1.9\times10^{-6}\). The reported 99% coordinate includes linear-interpolation error, so its last digits need not converge monotonically. These diagnostics support the displayed engineering precision, rather than proving exact real-arithmetic accuracy.

The momentum identity \(B=2a\) checks the integrated profile against its wall derivative. Two deliberately incorrect calculations are also executed. Omitting cross-stream convection at \(\eta=2\) leaves a dimensionless momentum residual of −0.081293 and fails the tolerance of \(10^{-6}\). Holding the shooting slope at 0.34 gives an edge-speed residual 0.015883 and fails the tolerance of \(10^{-11}\). Both assertion failures are caught and reported; the program also fails if either mutation unexpectedly passes. A negative kinematic viscosity triggers ValueError before the physical conversion. These are model-specific failures: none tests whether a real experimental plate is laminar.

What the plate calculation establishes

The running plate has a thin developing viscous layer, with distinct scaling, edge and integral thicknesses. Its computed one-face force follows both the wall-gradient integral and the momentum-deficit relation. The small normal velocity remains essential to convection. The leading pressure is transmitted from the outer flow across the layer, which is why an externally imposed adverse pressure gradient can change the near-wall velocity so strongly.

The result remains conditional on continuum no slip, constant properties, two-dimensional steady laminar attachment, gentle geometry and sufficiently high local Reynolds number. Transition requires a different transport model. Separation can require interaction with the outer flow. Compressibility and heat transfer require additional equations and property information; no temperature profile is predicted here. The theoretical plate force is therefore a controlled baseline for an experiment, not a substitute for checking its state and boundary conditions.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive and diagnose, thin-layer ordering). A proposed approximation keeps \(V_xV_{x,x}\) and \(\nu V_{x,yy}\), but deletes \(V_yV_{x,y}\) because \(V_y/U\ll1\). Starting from continuity and the scales \(L,\delta_s,U\), determine the ratio of the deleted term to the retained convection. Explain whether a successful thickness estimate would rescue the proposed differential equation.

Solution

Continuity balances \(U/L\) against \(V_y/\delta_s\), giving \(V_y\sim U\delta_s/L\). Thus \(V_yV_{x,y}\sim(U\delta_s/L)(U/\delta_s)=U^2/L\), the same scale as \(V_xV_{x,x}\). Their ratio is order unity, although particular terms can vanish at special points. Balancing the retained diffusion against convection still produces \(\delta_s\sim\sqrt{\nu L/U}\), so a correct exponent does not establish a correct equation. In the similarity calculation the deleted term supplies the cancellation leading to (16.7); its omission produces the nonzero residual measured by the program. Scaling is a necessary consistency test, not a proof of the full profile.

Exercise 2 (compute an inverse design, friction). Within the laminar uniform-stream model, increase the running plate's length while keeping speed, width and properties fixed. What length would double the one-face drag? What are the new length-based Reynolds number and 99% trailing-edge thickness? Explain the unverified condition that may invalidate this inverse design.

Solution

Equation (16.9) gives \(F_D\propto\sqrt L\), so twice the force requires four times the length, \(L=2.00\) m. The Reynolds number is four times its original value, \(1.3333\times10^6\). Equation (16.13) gives twice the trailing-edge thickness, 8.5044 mm. The mean coefficient halves, while area quadruples, so force doubles to 0.055214 N, an independent check. The new layer must remain laminar over the entire plate. The larger Reynolds number does not prove this, and disturbance-triggered transition can invalidate the drag law before 2 m. There is no justified universal transition length in the supplied data.

Exercise 3 (derive and compare, deficit definitions). Consider the constructed profile \(V_x/U=2r-r^2\) for \(0\le r=y/d\le1\), and \(V_x/U=1\) above \(d\), where \(d\) is a nominal thickness. Compute \(\delta^*/d\), \(\theta_m/d\), \(H\), and \(\delta_{99}/d\). If this profile is used as an approximation in the zero-gradient momentum-integral equation, derive \(d(x)\) with \(d(0)=0\) and compare its local friction coefficient with Blasius.

Solution

The displacement integrand is \((1-r)^2\), so \(\delta^*/d=1/3\). For momentum, integrate \((2r-r^2)(1-2r+r^2)=2r-5r^2+4r^3-r^4\) from zero to one: \(1-5/3+1-1/5=2/15\). Thus \(H=(1/3)/(2/15)=2.5\). Solving \(2r-r^2=0.99\) gives \(r=0.9\), so the nominal edge is not the 99% edge.

The wall slope is \(2U/d\), giving \(\tau_w=2\mu U/d\). Equation (16.14) requires \((2/15)d'=2\nu/(Ud)\), hence \(d d'=15\nu/U\), and integration gives \(d=\sqrt{30\nu x/U}\). Consequently \(C_f=4/\sqrt{30\mathrm{Re}_x}=0.73030/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}\), about 9.97% above the Blasius coefficient 0.664115. The approximate \(\delta_{99}=0.9\sqrt{30}\sqrt{\nu x/U}=4.9295\sqrt{\nu x/U}\) is close to 4.910 even though the wall stress differs by about 10%. Agreement of one thickness is insufficient evidence of profile accuracy.

Exercise 4 (counterexample, turbulent profile). A student differentiates the one-seventh power law at the wall and inserts the result into \(\tau_w=\mu\,\partial\overline V_x/\partial y\). Show the problem. Compute the integral ratios \(\delta^*/\delta_t\) and \(\theta_m/\delta_t\) of the same power law, and explain why those integrals can be finite when its wall shear is not.

Solution

With \(r=y/\delta_t\), the derivative is \(U r^{-6/7}/(7\delta_t)\), which diverges at zero. It cannot be the wall-resolved mean profile for finite stress. However, \(\int_0^1(1-r^{1/7})dr=1-7/8=1/8\) and \(\int_0^1(r^{1/7}-r^{2/7})dr=7/8-7/9=7/72\), so both deficit integrals exist and \(H=9/7\). Integrals tolerate a localized derivative singularity that a pointwise wall quantity cannot. These are integrals of an approximate profile; a real near-wall correction changes them slightly and is indispensable for stress. No finite friction coefficient follows from these ratios alone.

Exercise 5 (judge a separation inference, momentum integral). At a station with \(U_e=10\) m/s, \(U_e'=-5\) s⁻¹, \(\theta_m=0.575\) mm and \(H=2.59\), two proposed measured growth rates are \(\theta_m'=0.0020\) and 0.0010 m/m. Find the signed wall stress for each using \(\rho=1.20\) kg/m³. Does the pressure gradient alone establish separation? Can the second case be a monotone attached forward profile at a no-slip wall?

Solution

The common pressure-gradient term is \(-0.001319625\). Equation (16.14) gives \(\tau_w=\rho U_e^2[\theta_m'-0.001319625]\). The first value is \(120(0.000680375)=0.081645\) Pa, positive. The second is \(120(-0.000319625)=-0.038355\) Pa. Both have the same adverse pressure gradient, so its sign alone cannot determine wall-stress sign. The second result implies a negative wall slope for positive viscosity, and therefore reversed flow immediately above the stationary wall. It is incompatible with a smooth monotone forward attached profile. It may indicate reverse flow, incompatible measurement/model inputs, or failure of the approximation; the scalar integral balance does not select among those possibilities or determine a global separation streamline.

Exercise 6 (numerical verification, similarity). Why can an edge residual below \(10^{-11}\) coexist with a wrong wall-slope coefficient? Use the program's height-8 and height-12 solutions to quantify the discrepancy. Then explain how step refinement, domain extension and the momentum identity test different parts of the calculation.

Solution

Shooting enforces \(F'(\eta_{\max})=1\), a finite-height boundary condition. At height 8 it gives \(a=0.332059192\), while height 12 gives 0.332057336. The difference is \(1.856\times10^{-6}\), or about \(5.59\times10^{-6}\) relative. Bisection can solve the wrong truncated boundary problem extremely accurately, so a tiny edge residual does not remove truncation error. Increasing the height tests the infinity replacement; halving the step tests integration, quadrature and threshold resolution; checking \(B=2a\) compares an integrated deficit with a wall derivative through momentum conservation. The latter is especially sensitive to quadrature: the 400-to-800-to-1600-step integral changes shrink by about four. None of these numerical tests validates laminar attachment in an experiment. They establish convergence and consistency of this mathematical model.
