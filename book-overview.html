A route through the book

A calculation becomes useful when its assumptions and its checks are as clear as its answer. The chapters build that habit from forces on a stationary fluid to conservation laws, exact flows, approximations and aerodynamic applications. A first reading can follow the chapter order. A return visit can follow the dependencies below, with the needed definitions restated in each chapter.

Prerequisites and a way to read

The book assumes single-variable calculus, partial derivatives, vectors, elementary mechanics and basic thermodynamics. Vector calculus enters through gradients, divergence, curl and the divergence theorem; Chapter 2 establishes the tensor and traction conventions, and later derivations state where the calculus results apply. Before using an equation, identify the fluid region, the coordinate directions, the units and the conditions under which terms were removed.

Read a chapter's opening problem and model first. Work through the derivation before using the executable check. Then attempt an exercise with its solution closed, and compare the reasoning as well as the answer. The code and solutions are checks on an argument already developed in the text. They are most useful when an altered assumption makes a familiar answer fail.

Chapters 1–3: continuum, forces and motion

Chapter 1 introduces the continuum model, density at a point, viscosity, no slip and rate processes. Its cylinder and moving-plate cases establish why the size and time scales of a question matter. Use it to decide what a fluid field represents before differentiating one.

Chapter 2 develops gradients, tensors, traction and the stress convention, then applies them to hydrostatic pressure, manometers, surface forces and buoyancy. Read it before a momentum balance: pressure and shear must be assigned to oriented surfaces before their forces can be added.

Chapter 3 distinguishes particle and field descriptions, the material derivative, pathlines, streamlines and streaklines. It separates deformation from rotation and connects strain rate to the Newtonian constitutive model. Chapters 1 and 2 supply the material and stress interpretation; this chapter supplies the description of motion needed by the conservation laws.

Chapters 4–7: conservation laws and exact solutions

Chapter 4 passes from a fixed set of particles to fixed and moving control volumes. Its tank balances and continuity derivations connect storage to boundary flux, with outward normals and relative velocity stated explicitly. Chapter 3's material derivative helps distinguish a changing field from a changing particle property.

Chapter 5 combines that transport framework with Newton's law and Chapter 2's surface forces. The turned jet, moving boundaries, local stress divergence and constitutive closure show why a force calculation needs more than a flow-rate calculation.

Chapter 6 assembles the governing equations with initial, wall, interface and far-field data. Its rotating vessel and coordinate calculations use Chapters 2–5 to make a complete model. Use its distinctions among steady, uniform, fully developed and periodic flow before simplifying the equations.

Chapter 7 reduces those equations for parallel plates and a circular pipe. It derives velocity, volume flow rate and wall stress, and compares the laminar model with its regime and entrance limits. The numerical laboratory can be read after Chapters 4 and 7: it checks a mass balance and the integration and force budget of the exact pipe profile.

Chapters 8–11: energy, measurement and pipe systems

Chapter 8 extends Chapter 4's transport method to the first law. Pressure work, enthalpy, shaft power, nonuniform profiles and unsteady storage enter a common energy ledger. Basic thermodynamics and the earlier mass and force balances are the prerequisites for deciding what belongs in that ledger.

Chapter 9 derives the streamline relation from momentum and relates it to Chapter 8's energy accounting. The Venturi case distinguishes an ideal pressure-to-flow inference from calibration and loss. Read the assumptions beside the invariant before choosing measurement points or sharing a constant across streamlines.

Chapter 10 uses dimensions, the governing equations and boundary conditions to construct similarity parameters. Its vessel, airship, pump and aircraft cases explain what must be matched before model data can be extrapolated. The physical balances in Chapters 4–9 give those parameters meaning beyond dimensional homogeneity.

Chapter 11 brings the exact reference of Chapter 7 together with the energy accounting of Chapters 8–9 and the scaling of Chapter 10. Entrance conditions, roughness, transition, resistance correlations and localized losses enter reservoir and pump calculations. Keep the friction-factor definition and regime beside every resistance formula.

Chapters 12–17: dominant balances and external flows

Chapter 12 returns to the governing equations to compare low-Reynolds-number and thin-layer balances. The settling sphere and the need for a viscous wall layer use Chapters 5–6 and 10 to connect a neglected term with the conditions that make neglect plausible.

Chapter 13 develops inviscid, irrotational flow, circulation, velocity potential, stream function and boundary data. It uses Chapter 3's vorticity, Chapter 6's equations and Chapter 9's pressure relation. Its local and global distinctions matter before a potential is assigned in a domain containing a hole.

Chapter 14 constructs fields from uniform flow, sources, sinks, vortices and doublets. Chapter 13 supplies the equations and conventions that superposition preserves. The Rankine half body and finite-separation checks turn elementary fields into boundary constructions with explicit errors and singularities.

Chapter 15 applies those fields to a cylinder, surface pressure and integrated force, then compares the ideal construction with viscous separation and rotation. Read Chapters 12–14 first, and retain Chapter 9's conditions when recovering pressure. The distinction between prescribed circulation and its physical origin is part of the model.

Chapter 16 develops the thin-layer approximation introduced in Chapter 12. Similarity, wall stress, displacement and momentum thickness, and the momentum-integral equation connect the outer flow to a viscous layer. Chapters 4–6 supply the balances; the pressure gradients encountered in Chapter 15 give the separation question a concrete setting.

Chapter 17 uses transition, separation asymmetry and force histories to examine cricket-ball swing. Chapters 12, 15 and 16 supply the aerodynamic interpretation, while mechanics connects a specified force history to deflection. Distinguish that trajectory calculation from a prediction of the force coefficient itself.

Use assumptions, sources and checks together

A timestamp beside a section heading links to the corresponding course recording and starting time. It locates the topic; it does not certify every added derivation or numerical input. Each chapter's source note distinguishes lecture material, research extensions and original teaching constructions. Follow the nearby source links and the source map when tracing a claim, and retain any stated uncertainty or access limitation.

For a numerical result, record the model, units, parameter values and applicable regime before running the code. Compare the printed output, check an independent balance or limit, and change one assumption at a time. The catalog of all seventeen executable examples identifies their chapter sections and imports. Successful execution establishes the stated implementation checks; measurements and model selection remain separate evidence.
