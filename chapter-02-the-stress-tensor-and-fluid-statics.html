The stress tensor and fluid statics

Water at 300 K stands 20 m deep behind a vertical wall 100 m wide. The pressure at the foot of the wall exceeds the atmosphere by 195.5 kPa, and the horizontal force of the water on the wall is 1.955×108 N, about 196 MN. That force acts at 13.33 m below the free surface, that is 6.67 m above the foot, so its moment about the foot is 1.30×109 N m. This chapter derives these numbers from the definition of stress. It shows why the force on a surface in a fluid at rest is pressure times area whatever the orientation of the surface, and why that statement needs a derivation: in a moving fluid, the force on a surface depends on its orientation through nine stress components, and the nine reduce to one number for a fluid at rest or moving as a rigid body. The chapter also shows where constant density, the assumption behind the wall calculation, fails. In the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, air at a geopotential altitude of 10,981 m′ (geometric altitude 11,000 m; the prime marks geopotential units, defined in the last section) has a pressure of 22,699 Pa; a calculation with the sea-level density of air, the standard's constant \(g_0'=9.80665\ \mathrm{m^2/(s^2\,m')}\) and the geopotential altitude gives −30,591 Pa.

Grounded in two recordings of the NPTEL course Fluid Mechanics (Prof. Sanjay Mittal, IIT Kanpur): Lecture 3 (tensor notation, body and surface forces, the stress tensor) and Lecture 4 (fluid statics). Lecture material: scalars, vectors and second-order tensors; the gradient of a scalar and of a vector; symmetric and skew-symmetric tensors and the splitting of a matrix into the two; Newton's second law for a fluid element with body and surface forces; the force on a surface element as the stress tensor applied to the area; the index convention of the stress components and the two-dimensional state of stress; the moment balance that makes the stress tensor symmetric; the argument that pressure in a fluid at rest is the same in all directions; the net pressure force on a fluid element, \(-\nabla p\) per unit volume; absolute and gauge pressure; the equation of fluid statics and the hydrostatic pressure distribution in layers; the manometer; the force on a submerged surface and the point of action of the resultant; buoyancy and Archimedes' principle. Research extensions: the density and speed of sound of water at 300 K at three pressures (IAPWS R6-95(2018), Table 7); the hydrostatic equation with the ideal-gas law and a linear temperature profile, and the tabulated air properties at 10,981 m (U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976). Original teaching constructions: the running case (the wall and its numbers); the derivation of the traction on an arbitrary plane by the tetrahedron argument; the moment balance with every neglected term sized; the second derivation of isotropic pressure from the stress tensor; the local form of Newton's second law with the stress tensor; the compatibility condition that makes the density depend on height alone; the derivation of the force and centre of pressure on a plane surface; the divergence-theorem form of Archimedes' principle; Figures 2.1 to 2.3; the code and the six exercises.

The running case and the objects of the chapter

The fluid is liquid water at 300 K (26.85 °C) and a pressure of about 0.1 MPa. The IAPWS-95 formulation lists this state with density \(\rho=996.556\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), pressure 0.0992418 MPa and speed of sound \(w=1501.5\ \mathrm{m/s}\) (IAPWS R6-95(2018), Table 7, p. 15). The gravitational acceleration is \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\), directed along \(-z\), with \(z\) measured upward from the free surface of the water. (The free surface is the surface between the water and the air above it; the air presses on it with the atmospheric pressure \(p_{\text{atm}}\), taken as uniform.) A vertical plane wall at \(x=0\) has width \(W=100\ \mathrm m\) in the \(y\) direction. Water of depth \(H=20\ \mathrm m\) lies on the side \(x\lt0\). The air presses on the dry side of the wall and on the free surface with the same \(p_{\text{atm}}\). The wall is much wider than the water is deep, so the ends are ignored and the pressure depends on \(z\) alone.

Three questions follow, and each section below answers one. First, what is the force that the water exerts on a small piece of the wall, and how does it depend on how the piece is oriented? That is the question of the stress tensor. Second, what is the stress in water at rest? That is Pascal's result and the equation of fluid statics. Third, how are the forces on the pieces added up, and where does the sum act? That is the hydrostatic force and the centre of pressure.

The formal model has three parts. The fluid is a continuum: at each point it has a density \(\rho\) and a stress, a force per unit area on any surface through the point. A fluid element is a small region whose size \(\ell\) is small against the lengths of the problem and large against the spacing of the molecules; the limit \(\ell\to0\) below means that, within this range (the continuum hypothesis, taken as given). The forces are body forces, which act on every part of the volume, and surface forces, which act across the boundary of a region. The law is Newton's second law applied to an element.

Symbols are defined where they first appear. Two choices of notation differ from the board. The board writes a vector or a tensor with a tilde under the letter; this book writes them in bold type. The board writes the volume of an element as \(\forall\), and so does this chapter, because \(V\) is the velocity. The temperature does not appear as a symbol except in the atmosphere section, where it is written \(\Theta\), because \(T\) is the stress tensor.

Scalars, vectors and second-order tensors L3 0:13

A scalar, for example the density \(\rho\) or the pressure \(p\), is described at each point by one number. A vector, for example the velocity \(\mathbf V\) or the unit normal \(\mathbf n\) to a surface, has a magnitude and one direction. In Cartesian coordinates a vector is \(\mathbf A=A_x\mathbf i+A_y\mathbf j+A_z\mathbf k\), where \(\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\) are unit vectors along the axes and the components \(A_x,A_y,A_z\) are scalars.

A second-order tensor has two directions attached to it. Its components carry two indices, \(T_{ij}\), and each index takes one of the values \(x,y,z\). The components are written as a matrix whose rows are labelled by the first index and whose columns by the second:

\[ \mathbf T=[T_{ij}]=\begin{bmatrix}T_{xx}&T_{xy}&T_{xz}\\T_{yx}&T_{yy}&T_{yz}\\T_{zx}&T_{zy}&T_{zz}\end{bmatrix}. \]

A tensor has nine components in three dimensions and four in two dimensions; a vector has three and two. A third-order tensor has three indices and 27 components. It does not occur in this chapter. The definition by components is enough here. What distinguishes a tensor from a table of nine numbers is that its components change in a definite way when the axes are rotated, so that the vector that the tensor produces from a given vector (a product defined in the section on the traction) is the same physical vector in every set of axes. The rotation law is taken as given and is not used below.

The gradient of a scalar and of a vector L3 4:11

The gradient of a scalar field \(a(x,y,z)\) records how \(a\) changes in each direction. It is a vector:

\[ \nabla a=\frac{\partial a}{\partial x}\mathbf i+\frac{\partial a}{\partial y}\mathbf j+\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}\mathbf k. \tag{2.1} \]

The gradient of a vector field \(\mathbf V=V_x\mathbf i+V_y\mathbf j+V_z\mathbf k\) has two directions attached: each of the three components can change in each of the three coordinate directions. That gives nine numbers, and the gradient of a vector is a second-order tensor. The board arranges them with the component of the vector along the row and the direction of differentiation along the column:

\[ \nabla\mathbf V=\begin{bmatrix}\partial V_x/\partial x&\partial V_x/\partial y&\partial V_x/\partial z\\\partial V_y/\partial x&\partial V_y/\partial y&\partial V_y/\partial z\\\partial V_z/\partial x&\partial V_z/\partial y&\partial V_z/\partial z\end{bmatrix}. \tag{2.2} \]

In a velocity field the entries have the units of a rate, \(\mathrm s^{-1}\). For the simple shear field \(\mathbf V=(\dot\gamma\,y,\,0,\,0)\), with a constant \(\dot\gamma\) in \(\mathrm s^{-1}\), only one entry of (2.2) is not zero: \(\partial V_x/\partial y=\dot\gamma\) in the first row and second column. The arrangement is a convention, and some texts transpose it, with the direction of differentiation along the row. In (2.2) the first index is the component of the vector; in the stress tensor of the next sections the first index is the face. The entries of the two tensors must therefore not be compared position by position.

Symmetric and skew-symmetric parts L3 8:45

The transpose \(\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}\) of a matrix exchanges rows and columns: \((\mathbf T^{\mathsf T})_{ij}=T_{ji}\). A matrix is symmetric if \(\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}=\mathbf T\), that is \(T_{xy}=T_{yx}\), \(T_{xz}=T_{zx}\), \(T_{yz}=T_{zy}\). The three diagonal entries and the three entries above the diagonal fix all nine, so a symmetric matrix has six independent components. A matrix is skew-symmetric if \(\mathbf Q^{\mathsf T}=-\mathbf Q\). Then \(Q_{ii}=-Q_{ii}\), so every diagonal entry is zero, and the three entries above the diagonal fix the matrix:

\[ \mathbf Q=\begin{bmatrix}0&Q_{xy}&Q_{xz}\\-Q_{xy}&0&Q_{yz}\\-Q_{xz}&-Q_{yz}&0\end{bmatrix}. \]

A skew-symmetric matrix is a vector. Three independent components suggest that \(\mathbf Q\) can be traded for a vector, and it can. Define \(\boldsymbol\xi=(\xi_x,\xi_y,\xi_z)=(-Q_{yz},\,Q_{xz},\,-Q_{xy})\). Multiplying out, \((\mathbf Q\mathbf b)_x=Q_{xy}b_y+Q_{xz}b_z=\xi_yb_z-\xi_zb_y=(\boldsymbol\xi\times\mathbf b)_x\), and the other two components check in the same way, so

\[ \mathbf Q\,\mathbf b=\boldsymbol\xi\times\mathbf b\quad\text{for every vector }\mathbf b. \tag{2.3} \]

Conversely, any vector \(\boldsymbol\xi\) defines a skew-symmetric matrix by reading the three relations backward. The correspondence holds in three dimensions. In two dimensions a skew-symmetric matrix has one independent component.

Every matrix is a sum of the two. For any matrix \(\mathbf G\),

\[ \mathbf G=\mathbf S+\mathbf Q,\qquad \mathbf S=\tfrac12(\mathbf G+\mathbf G^{\mathsf T}),\quad \mathbf Q=\tfrac12(\mathbf G-\mathbf G^{\mathsf T}). \tag{2.4} \]

The identity holds because the two halves of \(\mathbf G\) add and the two halves of \(\mathbf G^{\mathsf T}\) cancel. \(\mathbf S\) is symmetric, because transposing \(\mathbf G+\mathbf G^{\mathsf T}\) reproduces it, and \(\mathbf Q\) is skew-symmetric, because transposing \(\mathbf G-\mathbf G^{\mathsf T}\) reverses its sign. The split is unique: if \(\mathbf S+\mathbf Q=\mathbf S'+\mathbf Q'\), then \(\mathbf S-\mathbf S'=\mathbf Q'-\mathbf Q\) is both symmetric and skew-symmetric, and a matrix \(\mathbf X\) with \(\mathbf X=\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}=-\mathbf X\) is zero.

As a check, take \(\mathbf G\) with rows \((1,4,-2)\), \((0,3,5)\), \((2,-1,6)\). Then \(Q_{xy}=(4-0)/2=2\), \(Q_{xz}=(-2-2)/2=-2\), \(Q_{yz}=(5+1)/2=3\), and \(\boldsymbol\xi=(-3,-2,-2)\); the program in the implementation section confirms (2.3) for this matrix. The count is 6 + 3 = 9: the split moves information between two parts and creates none. The symmetric part is the one that matters for the stress tensor, because the next sections show that the stress tensor has no skew-symmetric part.

Forces on a fluid element: body force, surface force and the traction L3 18:31

Stress is the reason for introducing tensors: it is a second-order tensor with nine components. The diagonal components are called normal stresses and the off-diagonal components shear stresses, and the stress tensor will be shown to be symmetric. The sections below define the stress and prove that statement.

Newton's second law for a fluid element of volume \(\mathrm d\forall\), density \(\rho\), mass \(\mathrm dm=\rho\,\mathrm d\forall\) and acceleration \(\mathbf a\) reads

\[ \mathrm d\mathbf F=\mathrm dm\,\mathbf a=\rho\,\mathbf a\,\mathrm d\forall. \tag{2.5} \]

The force \(\mathrm d\mathbf F\) is the sum of two kinds. A body force acts on every part of the volume and is proportional to the mass or the volume of the element. Gravity is the example of the lecture, and a force on electrically charged particles distributed through the fluid is another. Gravity gives \(\mathrm d\mathbf F_{\text{body}}=\rho\,\mathbf g\,\mathrm d\forall\), with \(\mathbf g\) the acceleration of gravity as a vector. A surface force acts across the boundary of the element and is proportional to the area, not the volume. Friction is the example of the lecture; pressure is another.

The surface force on a small patch of area \(\mathrm dA\) with unit normal \(\mathbf n\) is written as a force per unit area times the area. The force per unit area is the traction \(\mathbf t\), so \(\mathrm d\mathbf F_{\text{surface}}=\mathbf t\,\mathrm dA\). The convention for \(\mathbf n\) and the meaning of \(\mathbf t\) are these: \(\mathbf n\) points out of the element, and \(\mathbf t\) is the force per unit area exerted on the element by the fluid outside the patch, on the side to which \(\mathbf n\) points. The unit of the traction is the pascal, \(1\ \mathrm{Pa}=1\ \mathrm{N/m^2}\).

The difficulty is that the traction can depend on \(\mathbf n\): two patches at the same point with different orientations can carry different forces per unit area. There are infinitely many orientations. The lecture resolves this with the stress tensor, and writes the surface force as \(\mathrm d\mathbf F_{\text{surface}}=\mathbf T\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A=(\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf n)\,|\mathrm dA|\), where the area vector is \(\mathrm d\mathbf A=\mathbf n\,|\mathrm dA|\) and \(\mathbf T\) is the stress tensor (board notation). The next section defines the nine components of \(\mathbf T\); the section after it derives the relation between \(\mathbf T\) and \(\mathbf t\) that the board asserts.

The stress tensor: faces, directions and signs L3 27:13

Consider a small box with faces normal to the coordinate axes (Figure 2.1 shows the two-dimensional version). The face whose outward normal points along \(+x\) is the positive \(x\) face, and the face whose outward normal points along \(-x\) is the negative \(x\) face; similarly for \(y\) and \(z\). On each face the surrounding fluid exerts a force per unit area, the traction defined above. The nine components of the stress are defined by projecting those tractions on the axes. The board's rule: in \(T_{ij}\), the first index \(i\) names the face, by the direction of its normal, and the second index \(j\) is the direction of the force. So \(T_{ij}\) is the \(j\)-component of the traction on the positive \(i\) face. The board does not write the units; they are pascals.

The diagonal components \(T_{xx},T_{yy},T_{zz}\) act normal to their face and are the normal stresses. The off-diagonal components act in the plane of their face and are the shear stresses; this book writes \(\tau_{ij}\) for \(T_{ij}\) with \(i\ne j\). The sign convention of the board is that a positive \(T_{ij}\) on a positive face points along the positive \(j\) axis, and on the negative face the force is reversed, as Figure 2.1 shows. A positive normal stress pulls the element outward (tension), and a negative normal stress pushes it inward (compression). The pressure \(p\) of a fluid at rest is a compressive normal stress, so a pressure \(p\) corresponds to a normal stress \(-p\). The reversal of the arrows on the negative face is a statement about the traction on opposite faces, \(\mathbf t(-\mathbf n)=-\mathbf t(\mathbf n)\); the next section proves it.

The nine numbers \(T_{ij}\) are the state of stress at a point. The state varies from point to point. If the surface forces on opposite faces of an element differ, they do not cancel, and the element accelerates; this is how a difference of stress between neighbouring points moves a fluid.

The traction on an arbitrary plane

The claim. The traction on a plane with outward unit normal \(\mathbf n=(n_x,n_y,n_z)\) is

\[ t_j=\sum_{i}n_iT_{ij}\quad (j=x,y,z),\qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathbf t=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf T. \tag{2.6} \]

The board writes \(\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf n\) and calls it the force per unit area. With the board's rule that the first index is the face, the product that follows from the derivation is \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf T\), the sum over the first index. The two products are equal once \(\mathbf T\) is symmetric, which the next section proves. The derivation of (2.6) uses no symmetry.

Hypotheses. The traction at a point on a surface depends on the surface only through its unit normal at that point (the Cauchy stress principle, the model of surface forces used here). The traction, the density, the body force and the acceleration are bounded, and the traction is continuous in position, so that its value on a small face differs from its value at a nearby point by an amount that goes to zero with the size of the face.

Step 1: the tractions on opposite sides of a surface are opposite. Take a thin slab of fluid of thickness \(\lambda\) and face area \(A\), with the unit normal \(\mathbf n\) on one face and \(-\mathbf n\) on the other. The momentum equation (2.5) for the slab reads \(\rho\,\mathbf a\,A\lambda=\rho\,\mathbf g\,A\lambda+[\mathbf t(\mathbf n)+\mathbf t(-\mathbf n)]A+(\text{forces on the rim})\), and the rim force is proportional to \(\lambda\). Divide by \(A\) and let \(\lambda\to0\): the terms proportional to the volume and to the rim area go to zero, and \(\mathbf t(\mathbf n)+\mathbf t(-\mathbf n)=\mathbf 0\).

Step 2: the tetrahedron. Choose a tetrahedron with one vertex at the point of interest and three edges along the positive coordinate axes, so that the three faces at the vertex have outward normals \(-\mathbf e_x,-\mathbf e_y,-\mathbf e_z\) (unit vectors along the axes) and the fourth face has outward normal \(\mathbf n\), taken with all three components positive; the other octants follow by changing signs. Let that face have area \(A\). The projection of a plane figure of area \(A\) onto the plane perpendicular to \(\mathbf e_i\) has area \(A\,n_i\), so the faces at the vertex have areas \(A\,n_x\), \(A\,n_y\), \(A\,n_z\). The traction on the face with normal \(-\mathbf e_i\) is \(-\mathbf t(\mathbf e_i)\) by Step 1, and by the definition of the components, \(t_j(\mathbf e_i)=T_{ij}\). The volume is \(\forall=\tfrac13A\zeta\), with \(\zeta\) the distance from the vertex to the fourth face. Newton's law (2.5) for the tetrahedron is

\[ \rho\,\mathbf a\,\forall=\rho\,\mathbf g\,\forall+\mathbf t(\mathbf n)\,A-\sum_i\mathbf t(\mathbf e_i)\,A\,n_i . \]

Divide by \(A\) and let the tetrahedron shrink to the vertex with its shape fixed, so that \(\zeta\to0\). The two volume terms are proportional to \(\zeta\) and go to zero. The tractions on the four faces tend to their values at the vertex, by continuity. What remains is \(\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=\sum_i n_i\,\mathbf t(\mathbf e_i)\), which is (2.6). ∎

Interpretation. Nine numbers give the traction on every plane through the point. The part of the traction along the normal is the normal stress on that plane, \(t_n=\mathbf t\cdot\mathbf n=\sum_{ij}n_iT_{ij}n_j\), and the part in the plane is the shear traction, of magnitude \(\sqrt{|\mathbf t|^2-t_n^2}\).

Check. Let \(\mathbf T\) have rows \((-120,\,30,\,0)\), \((30,\,-80,\,10)\), \((0,\,10,\,-100)\), in Pa (values chosen for arithmetic, not measured), and let \(\mathbf n=(1,2,2)/3\). Then (2.6) gives \(\mathbf t=(-20,\,-36.67,\,-60)\) Pa. The normal stress is \(\mathbf t\cdot\mathbf n=-71.11\) Pa and the shear traction is \(16.96\) Pa. For \(\mathbf n=\mathbf e_x\), (2.6) returns the first row of \(\mathbf T\), as the definition of the components requires.

Boundary. Step 2 applies to any continuum with bounded acceleration. It does not use the fluid property that a fluid at rest sustains no shear, and it does not use symmetry.

The stress tensor is symmetric L3 37:20

The claim.

\[ T_{ij}=T_{ji}\quad\text{for all }i,j. \tag{2.7} \]

Then the stress tensor has six independent components, not nine, and \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf T=\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf n\).

The idea. A shear stress \(T_{xy}\) on the \(x\) faces and \(T_{yx}\) on the \(y\) faces produce torques of opposite sense on an element, and both torques are proportional to the area of the element. The angular momentum of the element, on the other hand, is proportional to its moment of inertia, which shrinks with the fourth power of the size. If the torques did not cancel, the angular acceleration of a small element would be arbitrarily large.

Derivation. Take a rectangle of sides \(\mathrm dx\) and \(\mathrm dy\) with unit depth, its centre at the origin, as in Figure 2.1, and let \(\ell\) be the larger side. Count the torque about the centre, counter-clockwise positive. The positive \(x\) face carries the force per unit area \((T_{xx},T_{xy})\) and lies at \(x=\mathrm dx/2\); the force \(T_{xy}\,\mathrm dy\) along \(+y\) has the arm \(\mathrm dx/2\) and gives a counter-clockwise torque \(\tfrac12T_{xy}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy\). The negative \(x\) face carries the reversed force at \(x=-\mathrm dx/2\), and gives the same torque. The two \(T_{xy}\) contributions add to \(T_{xy}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy\), up to corrections of order \(\ell^4\), because the stress at the face differs from its value at the centre by terms of order \(\ell\) that cancel between opposite faces and by terms of order \(\ell^2\) that do not. The normal stress \(T_{xx}\) on the two faces acts along the \(x\) axis at the height \(y\) of the point of the face: its torque is \(-y\,T_{xx}\) per unit area on the positive face and \(+y\,T_{xx}\) on the negative face, and the net is the integral of \(-y\,[T_{xx}(\tfrac{\mathrm dx}2,y)-T_{xx}(-\tfrac{\mathrm dx}2,y)]\) over \(y\), which is of order \(\ell^4\) because the bracket is of order \(\ell\) and the integral of \(y\) over the face is zero at that order. The \(y\) faces give \(-T_{yx}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy\) by the same argument. The torque of the body force about the centre is of order \(\ell^4\) (zero for a uniform body force on a rectangle). So

\[ \mathcal N=(T_{xy}-T_{yx})\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy+O(\ell^4),\quad\text{a remainder smaller than the first term by a factor of order }\ell^2. \]

Equate \(\mathcal N\) to \(J\,\dot\omega\), where \(\dot\omega\) is the angular acceleration and \(J=\rho\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy\,(\mathrm dx^2+\mathrm dy^2)/12\) is the moment of inertia of the rectangle about its centre, per unit depth. The board gives the same \(J\) and writes the torque with the same difference \(T_{xy}-T_{yx}\), about the corner \(O\); the other terms of the corner version are also of higher order than \(\ell^2\), so the conclusion is the same. The angular momentum of a deforming element about its centre is of order \(\ell^4\) when the velocity gradient and its rate of change are bounded, so \(J\,\dot\omega=O(\ell^4)\). Divide by \(\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy\), which is of order \(\ell^2\), and let \(\ell\to0\): \(T_{xy}-T_{yx}\to0\), which is (2.7) for the pair \(xy\). The same argument about the \(x\) and \(y\) axes gives the other two pairs. ∎

The numbers. Suppose \(T_{xy}-T_{yx}=\tau_\Delta\ne0\). The angular acceleration of a square of side \(s\) is then \(\dot\omega=\mathcal N/J=6\tau_\Delta/(\rho s^2)\). For water with \(\tau_\Delta=10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa}\), this is 6.0 rad/s² at \(s=1\) mm, 602 rad/s² at 0.1 mm, and \(6.0\times10^4\) rad/s² at 10 µm: it grows without bound as the element shrinks. A fluid element whose angular acceleration is bounded has \(\tau_\Delta=0\).

Boundary. The orders \(O(\ell^4)\) above assume that \(\mathbf T\) is twice continuously differentiable. If \(\mathbf T\) is only continuous, the remainders are \(o(\ell^2)\) and the conclusion is the same. The argument assumes that there is no body couple per unit volume and no internal angular momentum stored in the element, and that the velocity gradient and the acceleration are bounded. A continuum that has body couples or internal angular momentum can have a non-symmetric stress. This chapter does not treat such media. The result does not assume that the fluid is at rest.

Pressure in a fluid at rest is the same in all directions L4 0:14

The question. A fluid at rest has some stress at each point. What is it?

The hypothesis. A fluid is a substance that deforms continuously under any shear stress, however small. A fluid at rest does not deform, so it carries no shear traction: the traction on every surface is normal to the surface. The lecture gives the same argument (the fluid starts moving because of shear force) and calls the normal stress pressure. In symbols, \(\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=-p(\mathbf n)\,\mathbf n\), with \(p\) positive when the fluid is compressed; the notation \(p(\mathbf n)\) allows the pressure to depend on the orientation of the surface until the argument below shows that it does not.

Derivation 1: the shrinking wedge (lecture). Figure 2.2 shows a prism of fluid with a right triangle as cross-section, horizontal edge \(\delta x\), vertical edge \(\delta z\), slanted edge \(\delta l\), angle \(\theta\) at the lower left, and depth \(L\) perpendicular to the page. Write \(p_x\) for the pressure on the vertical face, \(p_z\) on the bottom face and \(p_n\) on the slanted face. The triangular end faces have normals along the depth direction, and the pressure force on them has no component in the plane of the page. The wedge is at rest, and the pressure force on each face points into the wedge.

The outward normal of the slanted face is \((-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\) in the \((x,z)\) plane, and \(\sin\theta=\delta z/\delta l\), \(\cos\theta=\delta x/\delta l\). The weight is \(\rho g\,(\delta x\,\delta z/2)\,L\), because the area of the triangle is \(\delta x\,\delta z/2\). Balance of the forces on the wedge in the \(x\) direction and in the \(z\) direction gives

\[ -p_x\,\delta z\,L+p_n\,\delta l\,L\sin\theta=0,\qquad p_z\,\delta x\,L-\rho g\,\frac{\delta x\,\delta z}{2}L-p_n\,\delta l\,L\cos\theta=0 . \]

The first equation, with \(\delta l\sin\theta=\delta z\), gives \(p_n=p_x\) exactly. The second, with \(\delta l\cos\theta=\delta x\), gives \(p_z-p_n=\rho g\,\delta z/2\). Let the wedge shrink to a point with \(\theta\) fixed. The right-hand side goes to zero, so \(p_z\to p_n\), and the three pressures are equal. These are pressures averaged over a face, and they tend to the pressure at the point if the pressure is continuous. The angle \(\theta\) is arbitrary, and the axes can be rotated about any direction so that a given normal lies in the plane of the wedge; therefore \(p(\mathbf n)\) does not depend on \(\mathbf n\). This is Pascal's result: in a fluid at rest the pressure at a point is the same in all directions; the word for this is isotropic.

Derivation 2: the stress tensor. By (2.6), \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf T=-p(\mathbf n)\,\mathbf n\) for every unit vector \(\mathbf n\). Take \(\mathbf n=\mathbf e_x\): the first row of \(\mathbf T\) is \((-p(\mathbf e_x),0,0)\), so \(T_{xy}=T_{xz}=0\). The other two axes give the other rows, so \(\mathbf T\) is diagonal with entries \(-p(\mathbf e_x),-p(\mathbf e_y),-p(\mathbf e_z)\). Take \(\mathbf n=(\mathbf e_x+\mathbf e_y)/\sqrt2\): then \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf T=-(p(\mathbf e_x)\mathbf e_x+p(\mathbf e_y)\mathbf e_y)/\sqrt2\) is parallel to \(\mathbf n\) only if \(p(\mathbf e_x)=p(\mathbf e_y)\). The same argument for the pair \(y,z\) gives

\[ \mathbf T=-p\,\mathbf I\quad\text{(fluid at rest)},\qquad T_{ij}=-p\,\delta_{ij}, \tag{2.8} \]

with \(\mathbf I\) the identity matrix and \(\delta_{ij}\) equal to 1 for \(i=j\) and 0 otherwise. The traction on any surface is \(\mathbf t=-p\,\mathbf n\). The two derivations agree: the first uses the force balance on a wedge and no tensor, and the second uses the tensor and the absence of shear.

The boundary. Both derivations use the absence of shear traction. Derivation 2 holds for a fluid at rest and for a fluid in rigid-body motion (Exercise 2), where nothing deforms. Derivation 1 holds for rigid-body motion too: with the acceleration \(\mathbf a\) the right-hand sides of the two balances become \(\rho a_x\,\delta x\,\delta z\,L/2\) and \(\rho a_z\,\delta x\,\delta z\,L/2\), so \(p_n-p_x=\rho a_x\,\delta x/2\) and \(p_z-p_n=\rho\,(g+a_z)\,\delta z/2\), and both differences go to zero with the size of the wedge. The lecture adds that the result is known to hold in other situations where the fluid moves. That is true only when the shear stresses vanish. A Newtonian fluid is one whose viscous stress is proportional to the rate of deformation. The question. If the pressure varies from point to point, what is the net force on an element?

Derivation. Take a box with edges \(\mathrm dx,\mathrm dy,\mathrm dz\) along the axes, in a pressure field \(p(x,y,z)\). The pressure acts normal to each face and into the box. In the \(x\) direction only the two faces normal to \(x\) contribute. On the face at \(x\) the pressure is \(p\); on the face at \(x+\mathrm dx\) it is \(p+(\partial p/\partial x)\,\mathrm dx\), to first order in \(\mathrm dx\) (the board's Taylor expansion; \(\mathrm dx\) is small compared with the length over which \(p\) changes). The net force in the \(+x\) direction is

\[ \bigl[p-\bigl(p+\tfrac{\partial p}{\partial x}\mathrm dx\bigr)\bigr]\,\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dz=-\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\,\mathrm d\forall . \]

The pressure itself cancels between the two faces. The \(y\) and \(z\) directions are alike. The vector form is

\[ \mathrm d\mathbf F_p=-\nabla p\,\mathrm d\forall . \tag{2.9} \]

The error of the first-order face values is a relative error of order \(\ell/\)(length over which \(p\) changes), which goes to zero with the element.

The same result from the stress tensor. For a stress field \(T_{ij}(x,y,z)\), the traction on the face at \(x+\mathrm dx\) with outward normal \(\mathbf e_x\) is \(T_{xj}\), on the face at \(x\) it is \(-T_{xj}\), and the net \(j\)-component on the two faces is \([T_{xj}(x+\mathrm dx)-T_{xj}(x)]\,\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dz=(\partial T_{xj}/\partial x)\,\mathrm d\forall\). \[ \mathrm dF_{\text{surface},j}=\sum_i\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_i}\,\mathrm d\forall,\qquad \rho\,a_j=\rho\,g_j+\sum_i\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_i}, \tag{2.10} \]

Observation: the net force depends on the gradient of pressure. A uniform pressure, whatever its size, exerts no net force on an element. The net force on an element depends on \(\nabla p\) and not on \(p\) itself. The gauge pressure is \(p_g=p-p_{\text{atm}}\), where \(p_{\text{atm}}\) is the atmospheric pressure. Because \(p_{\text{atm}}\) is uniform, \(\nabla p_g=\nabla p\), and the force on an element is the same whichever pressure is used. A negative gauge pressure means that the pressure is below the atmosphere, a partial vacuum.

The equation of fluid statics. A fluid at rest has \(\mathbf a=\mathbf 0\), so the pressure force balances the body force. With gravity, (2.10) gives

\[ \nabla p=\rho\,\mathbf g. \tag{2.11} \]

With the axes of the lecture, \(z\) up and \(\mathbf g=-g\,\mathbf k\),

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial x}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial p}{\partial z}=-\rho g. \tag{2.12} \]

The pressure does not vary in a horizontal plane. It falls with height at the rate \(\rho g\): the units of \(\rho g\) are \(\mathrm{kg/m^3\times m/s^2=Pa/m}\). For water at 300 K, \(\rho g=9776\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\), so the pressure rises by about 9.8 kPa per metre of depth.

A condition on the density. Equation (2.12) does more than give the pressure; it restricts \(\rho\). If \(\rho\) depended on \(x\) at fixed \(z\), then \(\partial p/\partial z=-\rho g\) would vary with \(x\), so \(\partial^2p/\partial x\,\partial z\ne0\), while \(\partial p/\partial x=0\) says that this derivative is zero. The two cannot hold together. Hence \(\rho=\rho(z)\): a fluid at rest in uniform gravity has horizontal surfaces of constant density, and in a connected region of the same fluid the pressure is the same at the same height. Two separate bodies of fluid at the same height can have different pressures.

Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle L4 50:57

The question. What is the net force of a fluid at rest on a body that is completely immersed in it?

The lecture's derivation. Split the surface of the body, of volume \(\forall\), into an upper and a lower part. By the vertical-prism interpretation of (2.19), the water exerts on the upper surface a downward force \(\rho g\forall_u\), the weight of the prism of water between the upper surface and the free surface, volume \(\forall_u\). On the lower surface it exerts an upward force \(\rho g\forall_b\), where \(\forall_b\) is the volume of the prism between the lower surface and the free surface. The prism above the lower surface contains the body, so \(\forall_b-\forall_u=\forall\), and the net upward force is

\[ B_f=\rho g\,(\forall_b-\forall_u)=\rho g\,\forall. \tag{2.23} \]

This is Archimedes' principle: the buoyancy force on a body equals the weight of the fluid it displaces. The derivation assumes a constant density, a free surface above the body, and a body for which each vertical line meets its surface twice. A balloon is in equilibrium when its weight equals \(B_f\), and rises when \(B_f\) exceeds its weight; for orientation, a rigid body of volume 1 m³ in sea-level air (1.225 kg/m³) experiences \(1.225\times9.81\times1=12.02\) N, so it rises only if it weighs less than 12.02 N.

A derivation without those restrictions. Let \(\mathbf F_{\text{buoy}}=-\oint p\,\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA\) be the pressure force on the closed surface of the body, with \(\mathbf n\) the outward normal. Suppose the fluid at rest would fill the volume of the body with the same pressure field, \(\nabla p=\rho\,\mathbf g\) extended through the body (which requires that the fluid touch the whole surface). The divergence theorem, \(\oint p\,\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA=\int\nabla p\,\mathrm d\forall\), then gives

\[ \mathbf F_{\text{buoy}}=-\int_{\text{body}}\nabla p\,\mathrm d\forall=-\int_{\text{body}}\rho\,\mathbf g\,\mathrm d\forall . \tag{2.24} \]

The force is minus the weight of the fluid that would occupy the body's volume. It points opposite to \(\mathbf g\) and has the size \(g\int\rho\,\mathrm d\forall\) for a variable density \(\rho(z)\) too. The atmospheric part of the pressure contributes zero, \(\oint p_{\text{atm}}\,\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA=\mathbf 0\), so the gauge pressure can be used. The same transformation applied to the moment shows that the moment of the pressure forces about any point equals the moment of the weight of the displaced fluid about it (Exercise 6), so the line of action of the buoyancy passes through the centre of mass of the displaced fluid.

Boundaries. The principle needs fluid on the whole surface of the body. A body pressed onto the floor with no fluid beneath it feels no buoyancy and is pushed down by the fluid above (Exercise 4). A floating body has its surface partly in air; with the density of air neglected, the displaced volume is the submerged part. What the chapter establishes and what it does not

The derivations in this chapter are exact within their assumptions. Their assumptions are of three kinds. The model assumptions are the continuum hypothesis, the Cauchy stress principle (the traction depends on the surface only through its normal), the absence of body couples, and the statement that a fluid at rest carries no shear traction. The idealizations are uniform gravity, a free surface at uniform atmospheric pressure, a plane wall of infinite width, connected fluid of the same kind in each layer, horizontal interfaces without surface tension, and constant density within a layer. The approximations are those of the examples: constant density for water at the wall, justified by the density change of \(8.7\times10^{-5}\), and the neglect of the light fluids in the manometer, justified in the example by 0.12 %.

They fail in identifiable places. The stress is symmetric under the assumptions above, but a medium with internal angular momentum is outside them. The pressure is isotropic only without shear, and the Newtonian stress of a moving fluid has unequal normal stresses. The constant-density formula fails for gases over heights of kilometres, as the atmosphere calculation shows, and for water at depths where the pressure reaches tens of megapascals. Archimedes' principle fails when fluid is absent from part of the surface. The formula for the centre of pressure is for plane surfaces only. A fluid in rigid-body acceleration, for instance in a tank that is accelerated, does not satisfy \(\nabla p=\rho\,\mathbf g\) (Exercise 2): it satisfies (2.10) with \(\mathbf a\ne\mathbf 0\).

The reader can now (1) compute the traction on any plane through a point from the nine stress components by (2.6), and say why the tensor is symmetric; (2) state why a fluid at rest has \(\mathbf T=-p\,\mathbf I\) and what fails when the fluid deforms; (3) find \(p(z)\) for layers of constant density and for a gas with a given temperature profile, and test whether constant density is allowed; (4) compute the force and the point of action of the water on a plane wall or gate (the running case: \(1.955\times10^{8}\) N at the depth \(2H/3=13.33\) m), and the horizontal and vertical components on an inclined wall; (5) read a manometer, and state the buoyancy force on a body together with the conditions of Archimedes' principle.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (compute and check a convention). The stress at a point has the matrix \(\mathbf T\) of the section on the traction (rows \((-120,30,0)\), \((30,-80,10)\), \((0,10,-100)\) Pa). (a) Find the force on a patch of area 2 mm² whose outward normal is \((1,2,2)/3\). (b) Change \(T_{yx}\) to 10 Pa, leaving the other entries, and compute \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf T'\) and \(\mathbf T'\cdot\mathbf n\) for the same \(\mathbf n\). Which one is the traction under the convention that the first index is the face? Why is the distinction irrelevant for the actual stress tensor?

Solution

(a) By (2.6), \(t_j=\sum_in_iT_{ij}\): \(t_x=(-120+2\cdot30+2\cdot0)/3=-20\) Pa, \(t_y=(30+2(-80)+2\cdot10)/3=-36.67\) Pa, \(t_z=(0+2\cdot10+2(-100))/3=-60\) Pa. The force is \(\mathbf t\,\mathrm dA=(-20,-36.67,-60)\ \mathrm{Pa}\times2\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}=(-4.0,-7.33,-12.0)\times10^{-5}\) N.

(b) With the modified matrix, rows \((-120,30,0)\), \((10,-80,10)\), \((0,10,-100)\): \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf T'=(-100,-110,-180)/3=(-33.33,-36.67,-60)\) Pa, and \(\mathbf T'\cdot\mathbf n=(-120+60,\,10-160+20,\,0+20-200)/3=(-20,-43.33,-60)\) Pa. They differ. Under the convention that the first index is the face, the traction on the face with normal \(\mathbf e_x\) is the first row of \(\mathbf T'\), and (2.6) reproduces it; so the traction is \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf T'\), the first product. For the actual stress tensor (2.7) says \(\mathbf T=\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}\), so the two products are equal and the order does not matter. The modified matrix has \(T_{xy}-T_{yx}=20\) Pa and cannot be a stress tensor.

Exercise 2 (check an assumption). A rectangular tank of water at 300 K, of length \(L=1.0\ \mathrm m\), is accelerated horizontally with the constant acceleration \(a=2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) in the \(+x\) direction. After transients, the water is at rest relative to the tank and moves as a rigid body. (a) Why is \(\mathbf T=-p\,\mathbf I\) still valid? (b) Use (2.10) to find \(p(x,z)\) and the slope of the free surface. (c) By how much do the pressures at the two ends of the bottom differ? (d) What does \(\nabla p=\rho\,\mathbf g\), equation (2.11), predict?

Solution

(a) The water does not deform (rigid-body motion), and a Newtonian fluid carries a viscous stress only in a deforming flow (this constitutive law is a model, taken as given), so no shear stress acts and (2.8) holds. The assumption of rest is replaced by the assumption of rigid-body motion.

(b) With \(T_{ij}=-p\,\delta_{ij}\), (2.10) reads \(\rho\,\mathbf a=\rho\,\mathbf g-\nabla p\). The components are \(\rho a=-\partial p/\partial x\) and \(0=-\rho g-\partial p/\partial z\). Hence \(p=p_0-\rho a\,x-\rho g\,z\), and the lines of constant pressure are the planes \(z=-(a/g)x+\text{const}\). The free surface is the surface of constant pressure \(p_{\text{atm}}\), so its slope is \(\mathrm dz/\mathrm dx=-a/g=-0.204\), an angle of 11.5°; the surface is lower at the front, the end that moves ahead, and higher at the back. Over the 1.0 m length it falls by 0.204 m.

(c) On the bottom, \(\Delta p=\rho a L=996.556\times2.0\times1.0=1993\) Pa, higher at the back end.

(d) The equation of statics gives \(\partial p/\partial x=0\) and therefore a horizontal free surface and equal bottom pressures. It does not describe this flow, because it omits the term \(\rho\,\mathbf a\). The tank behaves as if gravity were \(\mathbf g-\mathbf a\), of magnitude \(\sqrt{g^2+a^2}=10.01\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\), tilted by 11.5° from the vertical.

Exercise 3 (compute and design). A vertical rectangular gate is 3 m wide and 2 m high, with its top edge 1.00 m below the free surface of water at 300 K. The atmosphere acts on both sides. The gate is hinged along its top edge. (a) Find the force of the water on the gate and its centre of pressure. (b) Find the force normal to the gate, applied at the bottom edge, that keeps the gate closed. (c) What changes if the hinge is at the bottom edge instead?

Solution

(a) The centroid is at \(h_c=2\) m and the area is \(A=6\ \mathrm{m^2}\). By (2.21), \(F=\rho g\,h_cA=996.556\times9.81\times2\times6=1.173\times10^5\) N. By (2.22), \(h_{cp}=h_c+I_c/(h_cA)\) with \(I_c=3\times2^3/12=2\ \mathrm{m^4}\), so \(h_{cp}=2+2/12=2.1667\) m below the surface, 1.1667 m below the hinge.

(b) The moment about the hinge must balance: \(P\times2\ \mathrm m=F\times1.1667\ \mathrm m\), so \(P=1.173\times10^5\times1.1667/2=6.84\times10^4\) N.

(c) With the hinge at the bottom edge, the distance from the line of action to the hinge is \(3-2.1667=0.8333\) m, and the force at the top edge is \(F\times0.8333/2=4.89\times10^4\) N. The centre of pressure is low on the gate, so a hinge at the bottom carries less moment and needs less holding force, but it carries a larger share of \(F\) in reaction.

Exercise 4 (counterexample). A student argues: "any body in a fluid at rest feels the buoyancy \(\rho g\forall\)." A cylinder of radius 0.10 m and height 0.30 m stands upright on the flat floor of a tank that holds water at 300 K to a depth of 1.00 m. A gasket seals its base to the floor, so there is no water under it, and the space under the base is at atmospheric pressure. Find the force of the water on the cylinder, and compare it with the student's value.

Solution

The student's value is \(\rho g\forall=996.556\times9.81\times\pi(0.10)^2(0.30)=92.1\) N upward. The actual forces: the side faces carry horizontal forces that cancel by symmetry. The top is at the depth \(1.00-0.30=0.70\) m, where the gauge pressure is \(\rho g\times0.70=6843\) Pa, and the area is \(\pi(0.10)^2=0.03142\ \mathrm{m^2}\), so the water pushes the cylinder down with \(215\) N. The base carries zero gauge pressure. The net force is 215 N downward, not 92 N upward. The derivation of (2.24) needs \(\nabla p=\rho\,\mathbf g\) to hold in the water on the whole surface, and the water does not touch the base; the principle does not apply when fluid is absent from part of the surface.

Exercise 5 (check an assumption and design). (a) In the manometer example of the chapter, what is the smallest level difference \(h_3-h_2\) for which the neglected term of (2.16) is below 1 % of \(\rho_2g(h_3-h_2)\)? (b) Keep the heights of the example, but let the bulb hold a liquid of density \(\rho_1=996.556\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), and let the manometer liquid have density \(\rho_2=1500\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) (a value chosen for arithmetic). Compare (2.16) and (2.17). (c) State the condition under which (2.17) is justified.

Solution

(a) The neglected terms of (2.16) for an air bulb are \(\rho_{\text{air}}g\,(h_4-h_1)=1.225\times9.81\times0.20=2.40\) Pa. For this to be below 1 % of \(\rho_2g(h_3-h_2)\), the level difference must be at least \(100\times1.225\times0.20/996.556=0.0246\) m, about 25 mm of water.

(b) Equation (2.16): \(p_{A,g}=\rho_{\text{air}}gh_4+\rho_2g(h_3-h_2)-\rho_1gh_1=6.01+2943.0-2932.9=16.1\) Pa. Equation (2.17): \(2943\) Pa. The simplified formula is 182 times too large. The term \(\rho_1gh_1\) is almost as large as the main term and cannot be dropped.

(c) Equation (2.17) is justified when \(|\rho_{\text{air}}h_4-\rho_1h_1|\ll\rho_2(h_3-h_2)\). The lecture's assumptions, \(\rho_2\gg\rho_1,\rho_{\text{air}}\) and \(\rho_1\approx\rho_{\text{air}}\), imply it only if \(h_4\) and \(h_1\) are not much larger than \(h_3-h_2\): a bulb liquid of the same density as the manometer liquid, or a very tall air column, violates it.

Exercise 6 (derive). (a) Show that for a smooth scalar \(p\), \(\oint p\,\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA=\int\nabla p\,\mathrm d\forall\) for a closed surface of outward normal \(\mathbf n\) around the body, and that the moment of the pressure forces about the origin satisfies \(-\oint\mathbf r\times p\,\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA=-\int\mathbf r\times\nabla p\,\mathrm d\forall\). (b) Use \(\nabla p=\rho\,\mathbf g\) to conclude that the moment of the pressure forces equals the moment of the weight of the displaced fluid. (c) A prism of unit depth has the cross-section with vertices \((0,-0.5)\), \((1,-0.5)\) and \((0,0.5)\) in the \((x,z)\) plane (metres). It is immersed in two layers: density 1000 kg/m³ for \(z\gt0\) and 1100 kg/m³ for \(z\lt0\). Find the horizontal coordinate of the line of action of the buoyancy force and compare it with the geometric centroid.

Solution

(a) Apply the divergence theorem to each component of the vector field \(p\,\mathbf e_k\): \(\oint p\,n_k\,\mathrm dA=\int\partial p/\partial x_k\,\mathrm d\forall\), which is the first statement. For the moment, the \(i\)-th component of \(\mathbf r\times p\,\mathbf n\) is \(\epsilon_{ijk}x_jp\,n_k\), where \(\epsilon_{ijk}\) is the Levi-Civita symbol: +1 when \(ijk\) is \(xyz\), \(yzx\) or \(zxy\), \(-1\) for the three odd permutations, and 0 when two indices are equal, so that \((\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)_i=\epsilon_{ijk}a_jb_k\). The divergence theorem gives \(\oint\epsilon_{ijk}x_jp\,n_k\,\mathrm dA=\int\epsilon_{ijk}\,\partial(x_jp)/\partial x_k\,\mathrm d\forall=\int\epsilon_{ijk}(\delta_{jk}p+x_j\,\partial p/\partial x_k)\,\mathrm d\forall\). The term with \(\delta_{jk}\) vanishes because \(\epsilon_{ijk}\delta_{jk}=0\), leaving \(\int(\mathbf r\times\nabla p)_i\,\mathrm d\forall\).

(b) Substituting \(\nabla p=\rho\,\mathbf g\): the moment of the pressure forces is \(-\int\mathbf r\times\rho\,\mathbf g\,\mathrm d\forall\), which is the moment of a force \(-\int\rho\,\mathbf g\,\mathrm d\forall\) (the buoyancy of (2.24)) applied at the centre of mass of the displaced fluid, \(\int\rho\,\mathbf r\,\mathrm d\forall/\int\rho\,\mathrm d\forall\). Because \(\mathbf g\) is vertical, the horizontal coordinate of the line of action of the buoyancy is the horizontal coordinate of that centre of mass.

(c) At height \(z\) the width of the prism is \(0.5-z\), from \(x=0\) to \(x=0.5-z\). The mass of the displaced fluid per unit depth is \(1100\int_{-0.5}^{0}(0.5-z)\,\mathrm dz+1000\int_0^{0.5}(0.5-z)\,\mathrm dz=1100(0.375)+1000(0.125)=537.5\) kg. The first moment is \(1100\int_{-0.5}^{0}\tfrac12(0.5-z)^2\mathrm dz+1000\int_0^{0.5}\tfrac12(0.5-z)^2\mathrm dz=1100(0.14583)+1000(0.020833)=181.25\) kg m. The centre of mass is at \(x=181.25/537.5=0.3372\) m. The geometric centroid is at \(x=1/3=0.3333\) m. The buoyancy is \(9.81\times537.5=5273\) N and its line of action is 3.9 mm to the right of the centroid, because the denser lower layer holds the wider part of the prism. A direct numerical integration of the pressure over the three faces gives a net horizontal force of zero, a vertical force of 5272.9 N, and a moment about the origin of 1778.06 N m, which equals \(5272.9\times0.33721\), the weight of the displaced fluid times its horizontal centre of mass.
