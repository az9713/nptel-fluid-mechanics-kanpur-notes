The Bernoulli equation

A cooling line must deliver at least 10.0 L/s of liquid. A horizontal Venturi meter has a 100 mm inlet diameter, a 50 mm throat diameter and a measured inlet-to-throat pressure difference of 15.0 kPa. Does the reading establish that the line meets its duty? With a specified density of 1000 kg/m³, an inviscid calculation gives 11.107 L/s; a separately calibrated discharge coefficient of 0.980 gives 10.885 L/s. The difference matters, but neither number is a pressure loss through the whole meter. The duty decision requires a relation between pressure difference and flow, a justification for the pressures compared, and a separate account of calibration and permanent loss.

Grounded in Lecture 18A and Lecture 18B of Prof. Sanjay Mittal's NPTEL Fluid Mechanics course, IIT Kanpur. Lecture material: reduction of a steady-flow energy balance along a thin stream tube; Bernoulli's equation and pressure-flow work; Venturi geometry, continuity, differential pressure, discharge coefficient and Reynolds-number calibration; adverse pressure gradients, separation and recirculation; orifice meters, the vena contracta and flow nozzles; Pitot and Pitot-static tubes, static, dynamic and stagnation pressure, and alignment limitations. Research extensions: the National Bureau of Standards' distinction between metering differential pressure and permanent pressure loss, and its account of calibration and installation effects; NASA's account of separate Pitot-static passages and measurement limitations. Original teaching constructions: the cooling-line case and all its specified data; the Euler-equation derivation and the vorticity criterion; the rotational counterexample; the reservoir and head-budget checks; the complete two-fluid manometer calculation; the uncertainty analysis, computed figures, executable model and six exercises. The measurement question and its formal model

The running case uses an idealized liquid with density \(\rho=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and dynamic viscosity \(\mu=1.00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\). These are specified model inputs, not property measurements at an unstated temperature. The inlet and throat diameters are \(D_1=0.100\ \mathrm m\) and \(D_2=0.050\ \mathrm m\), their areas are \(A_i=\pi D_i^2/4\), and their centre-line elevations are equal. The static pressures are \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), with \(\Delta p=p_1-p_2=15000\ \mathrm{Pa}\). The unknown volume flow rate is \(Q\), in \(\mathrm{m^3/s}\); \(1\ \mathrm{L/s}=10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\). The board writes a dot over \(Q\); here \(Q\) already means a rate.

The formal model is a continuum occupying a connected fluid region, described by pressure \(p(\mathbf x,t)\) and velocity \(\mathbf V(\mathbf x,t)\), with position \(\mathbf x=(x,y,z)\), time \(t\), and speed \(V=|\mathbf V|\). Elevation \(z\) increases upward and gravity is \(-g\mathbf k\), where \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) and \(\mathbf k\) is the upward unit vector. We first assume steady flow, constant density and negligible viscous force in the region used for the calculation. Steady means that fields at fixed positions do not change with time; it does not mean that a moving particle has constant velocity. The fluid may accelerate as it enters the throat.

A streamline is a curve tangent to the velocity at each point at one instant. A stream tube is a bundle of streamlines whose side boundary has no normal velocity. For steady flow it can serve as a fixed control volume, a region through whose inlet and outlet fluid passes. With constant density and no leakage, conservation of mass makes the volume flow the same at both ends. If each end has nearly uniform axial speed, \(Q=A_1V_1=A_2V_2\). More generally \(Q=\int_A\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA\), with \(\mathbf n\) directed downstream, and the bulk speed is \(V_{\text{avg}}=Q/A\). The uniform-port assumption permits a bulk speed to stand in for a point speed; it is an additional approximation, not part of Bernoulli's theorem.

The model will be enlarged in a controlled way. First a streamline acquires a conserved mechanical-energy quantity. Next a meter relates that quantity to a pressure difference. A calibration then corrects the meter relation, and a head-loss balance accounts for irreversible mechanical-energy conversion. Keeping these steps separate prevents a measured coefficient from being mistaken for a theorem.

From a stream tube to mechanical energy L18A 0:41

Consider a thin steady stream tube with inlet and outlet sufficiently small that each section has effectively uniform properties. In time \(\mathrm dt\), the same volume \(\mathrm d\forall\) enters and leaves because density is constant. Pressure at the inlet does work \(p_1\mathrm d\forall\) on the fluid; the fluid does work \(p_2\mathrm d\forall\) on its downstream surroundings. The net pressure work per unit transported mass \(\rho\mathrm d\forall\) is \((p_1-p_2)/\rho\). This is why a fluid's mechanical-energy expression contains pressure as well as the kinetic and gravitational terms familiar from particle mechanics.

If viscous work and dissipation are negligible and no shaft transfers work to or from the fluid, that net pressure work changes the kinetic and gravitational energy per unit mass. Thus

\[ \frac{p_1-p_2}{\rho}=\frac{V_2^2-V_1^2}{2}+g(z_2-z_1),\qquad \frac{p_1}{\rho}+\frac{V_1^2}{2}+gz_1= \frac{p_2}{\rho}+\frac{V_2^2}{2}+gz_2. \tag{9.1} \]

Every term has units \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}=\mathrm{J/kg}\). The term \(p/\rho\) is pressure-flow work per unit mass in this balance. It is not the internal energy of the liquid. A real flow can conserve total energy while converting useful mechanical energy into internal energy. It then need not obey (9.1).

The thin-tube construction removes a difficulty of a large control volume: speed may vary substantially over a large inlet. Taking a successively thinner tube isolates one streamline rather than averaging dissimilar streamlines. It does not remove shear forces by geometry alone. Fluid outside a real stream tube can exert viscous traction along its sides, where fluid is moving, and that traction can do work. Neglecting it needs the inviscid approximation. Likewise, no shaft work is a separate physical restriction; making viscosity zero does not by itself prohibit an actuator from supplying energy.

At equal height, (9.1) says that a speed increase must accompany a static-pressure decrease along that streamline. It does not say that every fast region anywhere in a flow has lower pressure than every slow region. The constant shared by two locations must first be justified. The momentum derivation makes that restriction precise.

Deriving the streamline invariant from momentum L18A 12:18

For a small inviscid fluid element, pressure is the only surface stress. Opposite pressure forces sum to \(-\nabla p\) per unit volume. This follows by subtracting the face values \(p\) and \(p+(\partial p/\partial x)\mathrm dx\), and similarly in the other two directions. Newton's law per unit mass therefore gives Euler's equation,

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf V}{\partial t}+(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V =-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p+\mathbf f. \tag{9.2} \]

Here \(\mathbf f\) is the body force per unit mass, in \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\). The left side is particle acceleration: the chain rule along a particle path gives a local time derivative plus the change caused by travelling through a nonuniform velocity field. We require a conservative body force, meaning \(\mathbf f=-\nabla\Phi\) for a potential \(\Phi\) in \(\mathrm{J/kg}\). Uniform gravity has \(\Phi=gz\). A force with no such potential cannot be absorbed into the scalar below.

Let \(s\) be arc length in the flow direction on a regular streamline, and \(\mathbf e_s=\mathbf V/V\) its unit tangent where \(V\ne0\). In steady flow, \((\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V=V\,\mathrm d(V\mathbf e_s)/\mathrm ds\). Dotting with \(\mathbf e_s\) gives \(V\,\mathrm dV/\mathrm ds\). The curvature term disappears because differentiating \(\mathbf e_s\cdot\mathbf e_s=1\) gives \(\mathbf e_s\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf e_s/\mathrm ds=0\). Curvature can require a transverse pressure gradient, but it does not change this tangential equation:

\[ V\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm ds}+ \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm ds}+ \frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm ds}=0. \tag{9.3} \]

For constant \(\rho\), each term is an exact derivative. Integrating between two points on the same streamline gives the Bernoulli invariant \(B\):

\[ \boxed{B=\frac{V^2}{2}+\frac p\rho+\Phi=C_{\mathcal S}, \qquad\text{along streamline }\mathcal S.} \tag{9.4} \]

The subscript on \(C_{\mathcal S}\) matters: different streamlines can carry different constants. The hypotheses are steady, inviscid, constant-density flow with a conservative body force, and a smooth path inside the fluid. The formula extends by continuity to a regular stagnation point approached along the streamline even though the unit tangent is undefined exactly where \(V=0\). A shock, a pump, or a region where viscous forces matter breaks the stated derivation.

For the cooling-line geometry, \(A_1/A_2=4\). If \(V_1=\sqrt2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), continuity gives \(V_2=4\sqrt2\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The kinetic-energy increase is \((32-2)/2=15\ \mathrm{J/kg}\), exactly the pressure-work decrease \(15000/1000\). The check tests both the pressure sign and the factor of two. In the zero-speed limit (9.3) with gravity gives \(\mathrm dp/\mathrm dz=-\rho g\), the hydrostatic equation. In the equal-area, equal-height, uniform-speed limit, (9.1) predicts no pressure difference; a long real pipe with a pressure drop is then direct evidence that the inviscid assumption is unsuitable for its whole length.

Constant density is stronger than saying that each particle keeps its own density: it supplies a single \(\rho\) to the integration. If density is a known function of pressure alone, one may instead integrate \(\int \mathrm dp/\rho(p)\); that is a different formula requiring that constitutive relation. No compressible-flow correction is implicit in (9.4).

When the constant can be shared across streamlines

The question of comparing different streamlines has a local answer. Define the vorticity \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf V\), a measure of local fluid rotation, in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). A flow is irrotational when this curl is zero. Inviscid describes a neglected force; irrotational describes the velocity field. Neither definition implies the other.

The vector identity \((\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V=\nabla(V^2/2)-\mathbf V\times\boldsymbol\omega\) can be checked in its \(x\)-component: the difference between the gradient term and the cross product is \(V_x\partial_xV_x+V_y\partial_yV_x+V_z\partial_zV_x\). The other components follow by cyclic permutation. Substitution in the steady Euler equation with constant density and conservative force gives

\[ \nabla B=\mathbf V\times\boldsymbol\omega,\qquad \mathbf V\cdot\nabla B=0. \tag{9.5} \]

The second relation follows because a vector is perpendicular to its cross product with any other vector. It recovers constancy along streamlines. If \(\boldsymbol\omega=0\), the first relation gives \(\nabla B=0\). In a connected region, integrating that zero gradient along any path shows that \(B\) has one common value throughout the region. This conclusion does not require a single global velocity potential: it follows directly for the already defined, single-valued scalar \(B\). Irrotationality is sufficient; (9.5) also allows special rotational fields with \(\mathbf V\times\boldsymbol\omega=0\).

A rotational counterexample. Take a steady constant-density inviscid fluid in uniform gravity, with horizontal circular velocity \(\mathbf V=\Omega r\mathbf e_\theta\). Here \(r\) is cylindrical radius, \(\mathbf e_r\) and \(\mathbf e_\theta\) are the radial and azimuthal unit vectors, and the constant angular rate is \(\Omega=2.00\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The Cartesian components \((-\Omega y,\Omega x,0)\) give vorticity \(2\Omega\mathbf k\). A particle moving in a circle has acceleration \(-\Omega^2r\mathbf e_r\). Radial and vertical Euler balance therefore require \(\partial p/\partial r=\rho\Omega^2r\) and \(\partial p/\partial z=-\rho g\). Choose \(z=0\) at an axis reference point and let its pressure be \(p_c\). Integrating both balances gives

\[ p(r,z)=p_c+\frac{\rho\Omega^2r^2}{2}-\rho gz,\qquad B=\frac{p_c}{\rho}+\Omega^2r^2. \tag{9.6} \]

Each circle at fixed \(r,z\) is a streamline and has a constant \(B\). Across circles at equal height, both pressure and speed increase outward. Between \(r=0.10\ \mathrm m\) and \(r=0.20\ \mathrm m\), the pressure rises by 60 Pa and the kinetic energy per unit mass rises by \(0.060\ \mathrm{J/kg}\), so \(B\) rises by \(0.120\ \mathrm{J/kg}\). Applying a common Bernoulli constant would predict the wrong pressure sign. The hydrostatic pressure variation cancels \(gz\) in \(B\), leaving \(\nabla B=2\Omega^2r\mathbf e_r=\mathbf V\times\boldsymbol\omega\), as (9.5) requires.

For the meter, a nearly uniform upstream reservoir of mechanical energy can justify a common constant for ideal core streamlines fed from it. A boundary layer or a wake need not share that value. A wall pressure tap and a centre-line speed can therefore be combined only with a model of the cross-section: pressure approximately uniform across it and speed uniform in the inviscid core, with remaining profile effects handled by calibration. One cannot justify that combination by drawing a streamline along a stationary no-slip wall and assigning the bulk speed to it.

Energy, pressure and head: choosing the two points

With \(\Phi=gz\), multiplying (9.4) by \(\rho\) gives pressure units, while dividing by \(g\) gives length units. Define total head \(H=B/g\), pressure head \(p/(\rho g)\), velocity head \(V^2/(2g)\), and elevation head \(z\):

\[ p+\frac12\rho V^2+\rho gz=\rho B, \qquad H=\frac p{\rho g}+\frac{V^2}{2g}+z. \tag{9.7} \]

These are representations of the same streamline result under the same hypotheses, not three independent conservation laws. A head is energy per unit weight, measured in metres of the flowing fluid. A manometer height of a different fluid is not automatically that pressure head.

Adding a constant \(z_0\) to every elevation changes \(H\) by \(z_0\) and leaves every difference unchanged. Similarly, if the same uniform atmospheric pressure \(p_{\text{atm}}\) is subtracted at both points, the gauge pressure \(p_g=p-p_{\text{atm}}\) changes the constant but not the prediction. Two surfaces exposed to different gas pressures do not permit atmospheric cancellation. Absolute pressure must still be recovered when assessing whether a liquid can remain liquid; a gauge pressure below zero does not mean a negative absolute pressure.

A reservoir-to-jet calculation exposes the point-selection issue. Suppose the surface of a very large reservoir is \(Z=2.00\ \mathrm m\) above a horizontal jet, and both the reservoir surface and the jet are exposed to the same atmosphere. Assume quasi-steady drainage, constant density, negligible viscous effects along a connecting streamline, negligible reservoir-surface speed and negligible surface tension and air stress. The jet section must be in a nearly parallel free jet, rather than an arbitrary point inside the contraction. There its pressure is approximately atmospheric throughout the section: the free surface has atmospheric pressure and transverse acceleration is negligible. Then (9.1) gives

\[ V_j=\sqrt{2gZ}=6.264\ \mathrm{m/s}. \tag{9.8} \]

At \(Z\to0\), the predicted jet speed goes to zero. With a finite reservoir surface area \(A_R\) and jet area \(A_j\), continuity gives the downward surface speed \(V_R=(A_j/A_R)V_j\); keeping its kinetic term gives \(V_j=\sqrt{2gZ/[1-(A_j/A_R)^2]}\). The large-reservoir approximation is therefore testable. If \(A_R/A_j=10\), it underpredicts the ideal quasi-steady speed by about 0.50%. Actual draining is unsteady; the quasi-steady approximation requires the head to vary slowly compared with passage through the nozzle. This example neither makes the pressure everywhere inside a submerged nozzle atmospheric nor supplies its real discharge coefficient.

The Venturi meter: turning pressure into flow L18A 15:13

A Venturi meter contracts smoothly to a minimum-area section, the throat, and then expands through a diffuser. A pressure tap is a small flush wall opening connected to an instrument; its intended signal is local static pressure. Figure 9.1 locates the inlet tap, throat tap and downstream recovery section. The diffuser is long relative to the contraction to keep its expansion gradual; its real purpose includes avoiding separation, discussed below.

For the ideal meter, assume steady, constant-density flow; negligible viscous effects along the core streamlines; no shaft work; equal elevations; nearly uniform section speeds and approximately uniform transverse pressure. Then continuity gives \(V_1=(A_2/A_1)V_2\). Combining it with the same-streamline Bernoulli relation yields

\[ \Delta p=\frac{\rho V_2^2}{2}\left[1-\left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\right)^2\right], \qquad Q_{\text{ideal}}=A_2\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta p}{\rho[1-(A_2/A_1)^2]}}. \tag{9.9} \]

The denominator accounts for upstream kinetic energy: the inlet fluid already moves. Define the diameter ratio \(\beta=D_2/D_1\); then \(A_2/A_1=\beta^2\) and the denominator is \(1-\beta^4\), not \(1-\beta^2\). The running case has \(\beta=0.5\), \(A_2=0.00196350\ \mathrm{m^2}\), \(V_2=\sqrt{32}=5.65685\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(V_1=1.41421\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(Q_{\text{ideal}}=0.0111072\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\). The pressure-head drop is 1.52905 m and equals the velocity-head rise.

Two limits clarify the formula. If \(A_2/A_1\to0\) at fixed throat area, the inlet approaches a large reservoir and \(V_2\to\sqrt{2\Delta p/\rho}\). If \(A_2/A_1\to1\), the differential-pressure signal at a fixed flow goes to zero. The inverse formula becomes ill-conditioned: a tiny differential-pressure error can represent a large flow error. It does not predict infinite flow through a real equal-area pipe driven by an arbitrary measured pressure drop; that would violate the assumed inviscid model.

If the taps differ in elevation, the effective differential entering (9.9) is \(p_1-p_2+\rho g(z_1-z_2)\). One must either correct measured tap pressures for elevation or use a hydrostatically corrected instrument signal. A pressure decrease caused solely by climbing should not be interpreted as additional throat acceleration.

What a differential manometer actually measures L18A 24:50

Connect the two equal-height taps to a U-tube containing a denser immiscible manometer liquid of density \(\rho_m\). The connecting legs contain the flowing liquid of density \(\rho\), but the instrument columns are at rest. Let \(z_a\) and \(z_b\) be the left and right interface elevations; the higher inlet pressure depresses the left interface, so \(\Delta h=z_b-z_a\gt0\). Neglect capillary pressure and assume both densities constant. Hydrostatics in the two legs and in the manometer liquid gives, with common tap elevation \(z_t\),

\[ p_a=p_1+\rho g(z_t-z_a),\qquad p_b=p_2+\rho g(z_t-z_b),\qquad p_a-p_b=\rho_m g(z_b-z_a). \]

Subtracting the first two expressions and using the third gives

\[ \boxed{p_1-p_2=(\rho_m-\rho)g\Delta h.} \tag{9.10} \]

The density difference occurs because unequal lengths of the flowing liquid occupy the two legs. The shorter expression \(\rho_mg\Delta h\) is appropriate only when that leg-density contribution is negligible, as it can be for gas connected to a much denser liquid. It is not the general formula for a liquid pipeline. If the taps have different heights, the same calculation gives \(p_1-p_2+\rho g(z_1-z_2)=(\rho_m-\rho)g\Delta h\), exactly the effective differential needed above.

For a constructed manometer liquid with \(\rho_m=13600\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), the running differential requires \(\Delta h=15000/(12600\times9.81)=0.121353\ \mathrm m\). Ignoring the flowing-liquid legs would infer 16190.5 Pa and overestimate ideal flow by \(\sqrt{13600/12600}-1=3.89\%\). At \(\rho_m=\rho\), interface displacement supplies no differential-pressure signal at equal-height taps, as (9.10) requires. The density 13600 is a chosen input for arithmetic, not a claim about a particular manometer fluid at an unspecified temperature.

Discharge coefficient and a calibration that depends on flow L18A 28:27

A real meter has nonuniform velocity profiles, viscous layers, and pressure-sampling effects. Define its dimensionless discharge coefficient by the ratio of independently measured actual flow to the ideal flow computed with the same geometry, fluid density and tap differential:

\[ C_d=\frac{Q_{\text{actual}}}{Q_{\text{ideal}}},\qquad Q=C_d A_2\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta p}{\rho(1-\beta^4)}}. \tag{9.11} \]

This is a definition and a calibrated instrument model. Bernoulli's equation does not determine \(C_d\). A reference method, such as collecting a measured liquid mass during a measured time, supplies an independent flow rate. Repeating that comparison across the intended range gives a calibration. For fixed geometry, an important parameter is the inlet Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_{D_1}=\rho V_{\text{avg},1}D_1/\mu\), comparing inertial and viscous scales. In the running case with the specified \(C_d=0.980\), (9.11) gives 10.8851 L/s, bulk speeds 1.38593 and 5.54372 m/s, and \(\mathrm{Re}_{D_1}=138593\). The coefficient is stipulated as a separate calibration datum; it is not inferred from that Reynolds number.

A calibration curve written \(C_d=F(\mathrm{Re}_{D_1})\) creates an implicit equation because the Reynolds number contains the unknown \(Q\):

\[ Q=Q_{\text{ideal}}F\left(\frac{4\rho Q}{\pi\mu D_1}\right). \tag{9.12} \]

One may guess \(Q\), evaluate its Reynolds number, read \(F\), update \(Q\), and repeat. Such iteration is justified locally if the update slope has magnitude below one: \(|Q_{\text{ideal}}F'(\mathrm{Re})4\rho/(\pi\mu D_1)|\lt1\). Otherwise convergence is not assured; a bracketed root solution and a final residual check are safer. A converged number outside the calibration's Reynolds-number or installation range is still unjustified.

Calibration belongs to the installed geometry and operating conditions. Baker and Hurley, NBS Building Science Series 159 (1984, pp. 4, 10–11) distinguish the ideal relation from the discharge coefficient and describe transfer-reference calibration. Their discussion supports checking installation as well as the nominal coefficient. It does not supply the cooling line's chosen value 0.980 or certify a current commercial meter.

The head interpretation also needs care. If we retain uniform bulk profiles solely as an illustrative engineering approximation, the calibrated speeds give a kinetic-energy increase of 14.406 J/kg, while the measured pressure decrease is 15.000 J/kg. The difference is 0.594 J/kg, or 0.06055 m of head. This is an effective discrepancy between inlet and throat under that profile approximation. It cannot be identified with the permanent pressure loss of the whole device, and profile corrections could account for part of it. Measuring only \(p_1-p_2\) does not determine the recovered downstream pressure.

Pressure recovery, separation and mechanical-energy loss L18B 0:14

In the ideal contraction, continuity increases speed and Bernoulli decreases static pressure. The streamwise pressure gradient is favourable: pressure falls in the direction of flow. In the diffuser, area increases, core speed falls and pressure rises. This is an adverse pressure gradient. Recovery of static pressure is possible because kinetic energy decreases; it does not create mechanical energy. If the ideal outlet area equals the inlet area and its height is unchanged, the ideal outlet pressure recovers exactly to \(p_1\).

A real stationary wall imposes the empirical no-slip condition: fluid velocity at the wall equals the wall velocity, zero here. Near-wall fluid therefore moves more slowly than the core. When it encounters a sufficiently strong adverse pressure gradient, the near-wall forward flow can be arrested and reverse, and the main stream can leave the wall. This is separation. Closed or unsteady recirculating motions, often called eddies, can occupy the region between the main stream and the wall. The geometric area of the diffuser has grown, but less of it carries the main forward flow. A gradual expansion reduces the tendency to separate; the inviscid theorem alone predicts neither the separation point nor a permissible diffuser angle.

Recirculation is not the destruction of energy. Energy is transferred from organized motion into fluctuations and ultimately converted into internal energy by viscosity. A description that merely draws vortices has not calculated that conversion rate. The steady engineering head balance for one inlet and one outlet with constant density, uniform section pressure and elevation, and axial velocity profiles can be written

\[ \frac{p_1}{\rho g}+\alpha_1\frac{V_{\text{avg},1}^2}{2g}+z_1+h_P =\frac{p_3}{\rho g}+\alpha_3\frac{V_{\text{avg},3}^2}{2g}+z_3+h_T+h_L. \tag{9.13} \]

Here \(h_P\) is shaft energy added per unit weight by a pump, \(h_T\) is shaft energy extracted per unit weight, and \(h_L\ge0\) is mechanical head converted irreversibly in the passive flow between the chosen sections. The kinetic-energy correction factor is \(\alpha=(A V_{\text{avg}}^3)^{-1}\int_A V_n^3\,\mathrm dA\), where \(V_n\) is the downstream axial speed. Indeed the kinetic-energy flux is \(\tfrac12\rho\int_A V_n^3\,\mathrm dA=\tfrac12\rho Q\alpha V_{\text{avg}}^2\), which explains the cubic weighting. A uniform profile has \(\alpha=1\). For unsteady or turbulent profiles, the appropriate flux must include the relevant time average; applying this steady section model to strongly fluctuating signals needs additional care.

Equation (9.13) restates an engineering mechanical-energy budget; the measured or modelled loss and profile factors are additional input. It reduces to the uniform-streamtube result when \(h_P=h_T=h_L=0\) and \(\alpha_1=\alpha_3=1\). It is not a proof that losses vanish. For equal inlet and recovered-outlet areas, heights and profile factors, with no shaft work, it gives \(h_L=(p_1-p_3)/(\rho g)\). A hypothetical additional measurement \(p_1-p_3=1000\ \mathrm{Pa}\) would imply \(h_L=0.10194\ \mathrm m\) and a mechanical power conversion \(\rho gQh_L=Q(p_1-p_3)=10.885\ \mathrm W\) at the calibrated flow. That extra pressure measurement is not present in the opening problem.

The NBS report (p. 9, Table 1) tabulates permanent loss as a fraction of meter differential, with different values for orifice plates, nozzles and Venturis. This distinction is the useful engineering extension: a large measurement signal need not be a large permanent loss. Its historical device-specific estimates are not used as a universal coefficient here.

Orifice meters, the vena contracta and flow nozzles L18B 7:19

An orifice meter inserts a thin plate with a sharp-edged hole into the pipe. The stream contracts through the opening and continues to converge downstream because fluid near the edge has an inward velocity component. The minimum jet section lies downstream of the plate and is called the vena contracta. Its area \(A_c\) can be smaller than the geometric opening area \(A_o\). Recirculation occurs outside the jet, followed by mixing and pressure recovery. A flow nozzle replaces the abrupt entrance with a rounded converging contour. A Venturi additionally includes a diffuser intended to recover pressure while keeping flow attached.

The ideal algebra can still be used as a reference, but the area and tap definitions must be explicit. If an ideal streamline joins a uniform upstream section to an approximately uniform vena-contracta section of known area \(A_c\) and pressure \(p_{\mathrm{vc}}\), the same assumptions as (9.9) would give \(Q=A_c\sqrt{2(p_1-p_{\mathrm{vc}})/[\rho(1-(A_c/A_1)^2)]}\). In practice \(A_c\) is not simply the drilled-hole area, and a wall tap near the jet does not automatically read the jet's \(p_{\mathrm{vc}}\). A calibrated orifice relation is usually referenced to the known geometric area \(A_o\) and a specified pair of taps. Its discharge coefficient then incorporates the contraction, profile and pressure-sampling effects appropriate to those choices.

This distinction resolves a common double correction. Defining a contraction coefficient \(C_c=A_c/A_o\) does not license multiplying an already calibrated geometric-area discharge coefficient by \(C_c\) again. The latter already relates actual flow to its chosen geometric-area ideal calculation. Conversely, substituting \(A_o\) for \(A_c\) in an uncorrected vena-contracta derivation changes its model. A coefficient has meaning only with its reference equation.

The lecture illustrates an orifice discharge coefficient near 0.6 and a Venturi value above 0.9, with a nozzle intermediate, and presents the orifice as simpler to manufacture and the Venturi as longer and more expensive. These are illustrative comparisons, not specifications for every diameter ratio, tap arrangement or Reynolds number. In particular, \(C_d\) is not a thermodynamic efficiency and \(1-C_d\) is not a fraction of power lost. Device selection for the cooling line requires a calibrated flow uncertainty and a permanent-loss measurement at its duty, together with installation space and cost. The opening differential alone establishes none of those latter quantities.

Static, dynamic and stagnation pressure L18B 15:26

A Pitot tube presents an open end toward the oncoming flow. Once the connected sensing passage has pressurized, there is no sustained through-flow through an ideal pressure instrument. The external streamline approaching the tip ends at a stagnation point, where fluid speed relative to the stationary probe is zero. Consider a point upstream on that streamline with speed \(V\) and static pressure \(p\). If the flow is steady, constant-density and nearly inviscid along the deceleration path, with the same elevation and no shaft work, (9.1) gives

\[ p_0=p+\frac12\rho V^2,\qquad V=\sqrt{\frac{2(p_0-p)}{\rho}}. \tag{9.14} \]

The pressure \(p_0\) is the stagnation pressure, also called total pressure in this incompressible setting. Static pressure \(p\) exists in moving fluid; “static” does not mean that the flow is at rest. The quantity \(\tfrac12\rho V^2\) is dynamic pressure, a kinetic-energy density. It equals a measured stagnation-minus-static pressure only under the stated deceleration model. If the stagnation point is higher than the upstream point by an elevation difference \(\Delta z_s\), the relation becomes \(p_0=p+\tfrac12\rho V^2-\rho g\Delta z_s\). At a common height in an ideal irrotational region, total pressure is common; in a rotational flow it may differ between streamlines.

A simple Pitot tube supplies \(p_0\); it still needs a separate static-pressure measurement. A Pitot-static tube has separate internal passages: a forward opening senses stagnation pressure and side openings are positioned to approximate the undisturbed static pressure. Figure 9.2 shows the separation of the signals. The side openings are not supposed to stagnate the stream. NASA's Pitot-static description explains these separate passages and the differential-pressure inversion, and identifies low signal, blocked passages and supersonic shocks as limitations. Compressibility can require a different relation even before a shock is present.

At the calibrated inlet speed of the running case, (9.14) predicts \(p_0-p=960.4\ \mathrm{Pa}\); the ideal uncalibrated inlet prediction is 1000 Pa. An ideal uniform inlet could therefore be checked with an independent Pitot-static measurement. In a nonuniform inlet, a Pitot tube gives a local speed. To recover \(Q\), a spatial traverse must determine the signed downstream velocity \(\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf n\) for the area integral. In reversing flow, local speed magnitudes alone do not establish that sign; one centre-line reading does not determine the flow rate. For example, the parabolic pipe profile \(V_n(r)=V_{\max}[1-(r/R)^2]\), with pipe radius \(R\), integrates to \(Q=2\pi\int_0^R V_n r\,\mathrm dr=\pi R^2V_{\max}/2\). Multiplying a centre-line speed by area would overestimate that flow by a factor two.

Alignment also matters. The stagnation and side-port interpretations presume the probe is pointed into the local flow and used within its calibration. Flow behind a building or in a separated region can have an unknown, fluctuating direction. A single pressure difference then does not determine an unknown velocity vector. Multiple-hole probes can supply directional information after their own calibration; no universal cosine correction is established by Bernoulli's theorem. The lecture's 0.5 mm sensing holes and 1.5 mm outside diameter describe an illustrative small probe, not required dimensions for every Pitot-static tube.

The square root amplifies relative errors when the pressure signal is small. Differentiating (9.14) at fixed density gives \(\mathrm dV/V=\tfrac12\mathrm d(p_0-p)/(p_0-p)\). For the model liquid at 0.10 m/s, dynamic pressure is only 5 Pa; a 1 Pa differential offset causes a first-order speed error of 10%. At 1.38593 m/s the same offset contributes about 0.052%. A mathematically correct inversion cannot recover precision absent from the pressure measurement.

Resolving the cooling-line decision

The calibrated nominal result is 10.8851 L/s, about 8.85% above the required 10.0 L/s. The answer is conditional on the supplied calibration and measurement conditions. To make the decision testable, suppose independently specified bounds are \(14700\le\Delta p\le15300\ \mathrm{Pa}\), \(0.975\le C_d\le0.985\), and \(0.0499\le D_2\le0.0501\ \mathrm m\); hold \(D_1\) and \(\rho\) fixed. These are constructed bounds, not confidence intervals from an experiment. The minimum predicted flow uses the smallest differential, coefficient and throat; the maximum uses the largest. The code below evaluates the nonlinear corners rather than adding rounded percentages.

The dependence on geometry follows by taking logarithms of (9.11). Here each logarithm of a dimensional input means the logarithm of its numerical value in fixed SI reference units: \(Q/(1\ \mathrm{m^3/s})\), \(D_i/(1\ \mathrm m)\), \(\Delta p/(1\ \mathrm{Pa})\), and \(\rho/(1\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})\). The logarithm arguments are therefore dimensionless; changing these fixed reference units changes only the additive constant. With this convention, treating the inputs as independently variable gives \(\log Q=\log C_d+2\log D_2+\tfrac12\log\Delta p-\tfrac12\log\rho-\tfrac12\log(1-\beta^4)+\text{constant}\). Differentiating, with \(\beta=D_2/D_1\), gives

\[ \frac{\mathrm dQ}{Q}=\frac{\mathrm dC_d}{C_d} +\frac12\frac{\mathrm d\Delta p}{\Delta p} -\frac12\frac{\mathrm d\rho}{\rho} +\frac{2}{1-\beta^4}\frac{\mathrm dD_2}{D_2} -\frac{2\beta^4}{1-\beta^4}\frac{\mathrm dD_1}{D_1}. \tag{9.15} \]

At \(\beta=0.5\), the throat-diameter sensitivity is 2.1333 and the inlet-diameter sensitivity is −0.1333. Measuring the throat well matters much more than the same relative improvement in inlet diameter. The coefficient and pressure bounds above contribute approximately 0.51% and 1.00% to flow; the throat bound contributes 0.427%. These linear estimates explain the nonlinear check but do not replace it. Correlated errors require a joint treatment; varying the geometry can also alter the calibration, whereas this constructed interval holds the stated coefficient bounds valid throughout.

The nonlinear interval is 10.6750–11.0967 L/s. Within this bounded instrument model its lower end remains above the duty. That establishes robustness to these specified input variations. It does not establish robustness to an invalid calibration, a partially obstructed tap, density changes, or an unknown velocity profile. A useful commissioning plan therefore compares the differential meter with an independent reference flow and measures the recovered outlet pressure if pumping power matters.

Executable inverse model and failure checks

The standard-library Python program implements (9.9)–(9.11), the inverse pressure relation and the specified corner bounds. Inputs are in SI units; printed flow is in L/s. It also independently checks the ideal pressure difference from the two computed velocities, verifies the manometer pressure walk, and checks the rotational counterexample. The flow inversion rejects nonpositive density, nonpositive coefficient, nonpositive differential, and geometries without a smaller positive throat. The forward pressure function is used only with those valid model inputs. These domain checks do not detect an unsuitable physical model. The program makes no network calls or file changes.

from itertools import product
from math import isclose, pi, sqrt


def flow(dp, rho, d1, d2, cd=1.0):
    if not (dp > 0 and rho > 0 and cd > 0 and 0 < d2 < d1):
        raise ValueError("positive inputs and 0 < d2 < d1 required")
    a2 = pi * d2**2 / 4
    return cd * a2 * sqrt(2 * dp / (rho * (1 - (d2 / d1)**4)))


def pressure(q, rho, d1, d2, cd=1.0):
    a2 = pi * d2**2 / 4
    return 0.5 * rho * (q / (cd * a2))**2 * (1 - (d2 / d1)**4)


rho, mu, g = 1000.0, 0.001, 9.81
d1, d2, dp, cd = 0.1, 0.05, 15000.0, 0.98
a1, a2 = pi * d1**2 / 4, pi * d2**2 / 4
qi = flow(dp, rho, d1, d2)
q = flow(dp, rho, d1, d2, cd)
v1, v2 = qi / a1, qi / a2
assert isclose(0.5 * rho * (v2**2 - v1**2), dp, rel_tol=1e-12)
assert isclose(pressure(q, rho, d1, d2, cd), dp, rel_tol=1e-12)
print(f"ideal flow = {1000 * qi:.6f} L/s")
print(f"calibrated flow = {1000 * q:.6f} L/s")
print(f"inlet Re = {rho * (q / a1) * d1 / mu:.3f}")
print(f"inlet Pitot differential = {0.5 * rho * (q / a1)**2:.3f} Pa")
corners = [flow(p, rho, d1, d, c) for p, d, c in product(
    (14700.0, 15300.0), (0.0499, 0.0501), (0.975, 0.985))]
print(f"bounded flow = {1000 * min(corners):.6f} to "
      f"{1000 * max(corners):.6f} L/s")
assert min(corners) > 0.010
rho_m = 13600.0
dh = dp / ((rho_m - rho) * g)
za, zb, zt = -0.3, -0.3 + dh, 0.0
pa = dp + rho * g * (zt - za)
pb = rho * g * (zt - zb)
assert isclose(pa - pb, rho_m * g * dh, rel_tol=1e-12)
print(f"manometer displacement = {dh:.6f} m")
omega, ra, rb = 2.0, 0.1, 0.2
delta_p = 0.5 * rho * omega**2 * (rb**2 - ra**2)
delta_b = delta_p / rho + 0.5 * omega**2 * (rb**2 - ra**2)
assert isclose(delta_b, 0.12, rel_tol=1e-12)
print(f"rotational delta B = {delta_b:.6f} J/kg")
bad_q = cd * a2 * sqrt(2 * dp / rho)
bad_residual = pressure(bad_q, rho, d1, d2, cd) - dp
try:
    assert abs(bad_residual) < 1e-8
except AssertionError:
    print(f"missing-denominator mutation rejected: {bad_residual:.3f} Pa")
else:
    raise AssertionError("mutation escaped")
try:
    flow(dp, rho, d1, d1, cd)
except ValueError:
    print("equal-diameter input rejected")
else:
    raise AssertionError("invalid geometry escaped")

The mutation deliberately omits \(1-\beta^4\) from the flow calculation. Substitution into the independently written forward pressure relation then misses the measured differential by −937.5 Pa, so the residual assertion fails and the failure is caught and printed. Merely checking that flow is positive would not reveal this error. The rotational assertion checks arithmetic under its own Euler solution; it does not make a global Bernoulli constant valid there. None of these internal checks is experimental validation of the instrument.

What the model captures and what it misses

Bernoulli's equation captures the exchange among pressure-flow work, kinetic energy and conservative-force potential energy along an ideal steady streamline. It explains why the throat pressure falls and why a Pitot tip can turn local kinetic energy into a pressure rise. The vorticity relation decides whether different streamlines may share the constant. The reservoir limit checks point selection, while the rotating-flow counterexample blocks the unrestricted claim that higher speed always means lower pressure.

The inviscid approximation omits the wall layers that permit separation and irreversible loss. The calibrated discharge coefficient addresses the meter inversion within a specified range; a separate head budget addresses the mechanical power requirement. The uniform-port approximation omits profile weighting, which can be tested by a traverse or corrected through an energy-flux factor. Constant density omits compressibility, and steady flow omits transient acceleration. One must reconsider the relevant governing equation when any of these omissions controls the measurement. There is no universal loss coefficient, discharge coefficient, diffuser angle or probe size in the Bernoulli theorem.

Exercises

1. Derive and check — an inclined meter. A Venturi with the running-case diameters carries the specified liquid. The throat is 0.50 m above the inlet, and the measured tap pressures satisfy \(p_1-p_2=15.0\ \mathrm{kPa}\). Under the steady inviscid constant-density, uniform-section model with gravity, derive the correct ideal flow. Would ignoring elevation overestimate or underestimate it? Explain what an ideal two-fluid differential manometer connected to these taps would encode.

Solution

Bernoulli along a connecting core streamline gives \(\tfrac12\rho(V_2^2-V_1^2)=p_1-p_2+\rho g(z_1-z_2)=15000-4905=10095\ \mathrm{Pa}\). Continuity supplies the same geometric denominator as (9.9), so \(Q=Q_{\text{ideal,horizontal}}\sqrt{10095/15000}=9.112\ \mathrm{L/s}\), rounded. Some of the observed pressure decrease supplies gravitational potential energy, leaving less for acceleration. Ignoring elevation would overestimate flow. The hydrostatic pressure walk gives \((\rho_m-\rho)g\Delta h=10095\ \mathrm{Pa}\). Thus an ideal differential manometer already reports the effective pressure difference when interpreted through its fluid-density difference; applying a second elevation correction to that effective signal would count gravity twice.

2. Counterexample — equal total pressure? For the solid-body rotational field in (9.6), compare static pressure and the pressure obtained by ideally stopping fluid at the same height at radii 0.10 and 0.20 m. Use the supplied \(\rho\) and \(\Omega\). Explain why each local Pitot relation can hold while the two stagnation pressures differ. What changes if \(\Omega\to0\)?

Solution

The speeds are 0.20 and 0.40 m/s. Radial momentum gives \(p_b-p_a=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2(r_b^2-r_a^2)=60\ \mathrm{Pa}\). Ideal local stopping adds dynamic pressures 20 and 80 Pa respectively, so \(p_{0b}-p_{0a}=60+(80-20)=120\ \mathrm{Pa}\). Each stopping operation concerns its own incident streamline; Bernoulli never equated the constants of the two circles. This is also \(\rho(B_b-B_a)\) at equal height. The operation is a local ideal probe model and need not preserve the original unperturbed circular field near the probe. As \(\Omega\to0\), both differences vanish quadratically, and the horizontal pressure field becomes uniform.

3. Inverse calibration and sensitivity — infer a curve. A reference collection measures 10.8851 L/s at the running-case differential. Recover \(C_d\). Then a second constructed calibration law is \(F(\mathrm{Re})=0.970+0.020\mathrm{Re}/(\mathrm{Re}+100000)\). Solve (9.12) for the same differential, justify convergence of fixed-point iteration, and distinguish this synthetic law from measured evidence.

Solution

The reference ratio gives \(C_d=10.8851/11.107207\simeq0.980003\); rounding the reference flow explains the small difference from 0.980. For the second case, put \(a=4\rho/(\pi\mu D_1)\), so \(\mathrm{Re}=aQ\), and iterate \(Q_{n+1}=Q_{\text{ideal}}[0.970+0.020aQ_n/(aQ_n+100000)]\). Starting at \(Q_{\text{ideal}}\) gives a solution approximately 10.903 L/s and \(\mathrm{Re}\simeq138823\). For every positive \(Q\), \(F'\le0.020/100000=2.0\times10^{-7}\), so the update derivative is at most \(Q_{\text{ideal}}aF'\simeq0.0283\lt1\). The update maps positive guesses into the bounded interval \([0.970Q_{\text{ideal}},0.990Q_{\text{ideal}}]\), so successive differences shrink. A final substitution checks the solution. The supplied functional law is an original construction for learning the implicit inverse problem; it has no empirical validity or certified Reynolds-number range.

4. Diagnose a boundary condition — the submerged nozzle. An open reservoir surface lies 2.00 m above a small horizontal outlet. A student assigns atmospheric pressure to the entire nozzle because its final jet is in air, and therefore concludes that speed is the same throughout its horizontal contraction. Identify the invalid step. Under what restrictions does (9.8) hold, and how would a prescribed nozzle head loss \(h_L=0.20\ \mathrm m\) change the predicted exit speed?

Solution

Atmospheric pressure is a dynamic boundary condition on a free liquid-air surface when surface tension and air stress are negligible. The inside of the nozzle is confined by solid walls and need not have that pressure. In a contraction, continuity requires acceleration and the ideal streamline equation supplies the associated pressure decrease. The atmospheric value can be assigned throughout a nearly parallel exposed jet if transverse acceleration is negligible, not throughout every upstream section. Formula (9.8) also needs a very large reservoir, quasi-steady flow, constant density, no shaft input and negligible loss on the selected path. With the prescribed loss, the engineering head balance becomes \(Z=V_j^2/(2g)+h_L\), giving \(V_j=\sqrt{2g(2.00-0.20)}=5.943\ \mathrm{m/s}\). This answer uses an added loss datum; it is not the inviscid theorem. If \(h_L\ge Z\), this assumed positive-discharge balance cannot produce a positive speed without another energy source.

5. Design judgment — signal and pumping power. At 10.0 L/s, two candidate meters have independently measured permanent pressure losses of 1.0 and 6.0 kPa. Both meet the same specified flow uncertainty, installation conditions and Reynolds-number range. The higher-loss meter is cheaper to install. Derive the hydraulic power difference and the electrical-energy difference over 4000 h if the pump's stipulated overall efficiency is 0.70. Does a larger \(C_d\) alone establish which meter is cheaper to operate?

Solution

At a fixed duty the extra irreversible hydraulic power is \(Q\Delta p_L=0.010(6000-1000)=50\ \mathrm W\). The extra electrical power under the stipulated efficiency is \(50/0.70=71.43\ \mathrm W\); over 4000 h this is \(285.7\ \mathrm{kWh}\). A monetary comparison needs the electricity tariff and installation-cost difference, neither supplied. Discharge coefficient compares actual flow with a chosen ideal metering equation. It is not the permanent-pressure-loss fraction, so ranking \(C_d\) alone cannot establish operating cost. The decision depends here on measured permanent loss, subject to the equal-duty and equal-efficiency assumptions. A changed pump operating point could change the efficiency and would need another model.

6. Compute a traverse — local speed is not bulk speed. A steady axisymmetric pipe has the constructed axial profile \(V_n(r)=V_{\max}[1-(r/R)^n]\), with \(n\gt0\). Assume each local Pitot inversion is valid with the same density and negligible height correction. Derive \(Q\) and the kinetic-energy correction factor \(\alpha\). Evaluate \(n=2\) and \(n=8\). What would a single centre-line Pitot reading miss?

Solution

Writing \(x=r/R\), the area integral gives \(Q=2\pi R^2V_{\max}\int_0^1(1-x^n)x\,\mathrm dx=\pi R^2V_{\max}n/(n+2)\). Thus \(V_{\text{avg}}/V_{\max}=n/(n+2)\). The kinetic-energy numerator is \(2\pi R^2V_{\max}^3\int_0^1(1-x^n)^3x\,\mathrm dx\). Expanding the cube and dividing by \(\pi R^2V_{\text{avg}}^3\) gives

\[ \alpha=2\left(\frac{n+2}{n}\right)^3 \left[\frac12-\frac3{n+2}+\frac3{2n+2}-\frac1{3n+2}\right]. \]

For \(n=2\), the mean/max ratio is 0.5 and \(\alpha=2\). For \(n=8\), they are 0.8 and \(\alpha=1.28205\). A centre-line reading reports \(V_{\max}\) and would overestimate volume flow by factors 2 and 1.25 respectively if multiplied by area. It also cannot determine \(\alpha\): that needs the spatial distribution and its cubic weighting. As \(n\to\infty\), both the mean/max ratio and \(\alpha\) tend to one, checking the uniform-profile limit. These profiles are constructed examples; a fully developed viscous pipe flow generally loses pressure along the pipe even if ideal local probe deceleration remains a useful approximation.
