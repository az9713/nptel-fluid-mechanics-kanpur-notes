The Reynolds transport theorem and conservation of mass

A cylindrical water tank 5 m in diameter is fed through a pipe 10 cm in diameter, in which the water moves at a uniform 3 m/s, and drained through a pipe 6 cm in diameter at a uniform 2 m/s. The water level rises at \(9.12\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s}\), that is 3.28 m/h, so a rise of 1 m takes 1096 s, about 18 min; the density of the water does not enter the result. A second tank, a sphere 1 m in diameter that holds air at \(1.013\times10^{5}\) Pa and 300 K, is filled through a pipe 2 cm in diameter in which the air moves at 2 m/s from a line at \(3\times10^{5}\) Pa. Holding the inlet speed and line state constant gives a fill time of 552 s, or 9.20 min. A pressure-driven inflow generally falls as the pressure difference decreases, so this time depends on the prescribed-inflow idealization. Both answers come from one relation. The laws of mechanics are statements about a fixed set of fluid particles, but an engineering question concerns a fixed region of space through which the particles pass. This chapter derives the relation between the two (the Reynolds transport theorem), for a fixed region and for a region whose boundary moves, applies it to conservation of mass, and obtains the differential equation \(\partial\rho/\partial t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)=0\) together with the hypotheses under which it holds. It also separates three conditions that are often confused: steady flow, incompressible flow, and uniform density.

Lecture material: the balance laws and the reason for a control volume; the jet engine as an example of a control volume; the property \(N\) and the specific property \(\eta\); the accumulation balance with influx and efflux; the theorem for one inlet port and one exit port and its generalization to a closed control surface; the table of choices of \(\eta\) for mass, momentum and energy; conservation of mass as the theorem with \(\eta=1\); the steady form; the water tank with a control volume aligned with the pipes and an alternative control volume; the air tank with uniform density; the differential continuity equation by the divergence theorem and by an infinitesimal element; the simplifications for steady and for incompressible flow, with the oil and water interface as an example. Research extensions: the specific gas constant of air from the gas constant and the molar mass of air in the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976, and its sea-level density as a check. Original teaching constructions: the running case and its numbers; the derivation of the theorem for a moving control volume by the Leibniz rule, which introduces the relative velocity \(\mathbf V-\mathbf V_{cs}\); the moving control volume that follows the free surface of the tank; the footprint area of the slanted jet; the proof of the argument for an arbitrary volume; the hypotheses of each step; the three counterexamples that separate steady, incompressible and uniform-density flow; the closure with decaying inflow that bounds the fill time of the air tank; Figures 4.1 to 4.4; the code and the six exercises.

The running case and the objects of the chapter

The running case is the water tank of the lead. The tank has a vertical wall and a constant cross-section of diameter 5 m. The inlet pipe has diameter 10 cm and the water in it moves at a uniform 3 m/s; the outlet pipe has diameter 6 cm and the water in it moves at a uniform 2 m/s. Uniform means that the speed is the same at every point of the pipe cross-section; this is the mean speed of the pipe, an idealization that the lecture states. The question is the rate at which the level \(h\) of the water in the tank changes. Both are problems in which the fluid inside a chosen region changes with time because fluid crosses the boundary of the region.

The formal model has four parts. The fluid is a continuum with a density \(\rho(\mathbf x,t)\) and a velocity \(\mathbf V(\mathbf x,t)\) defined at each point \(\mathbf x\) and each time \(t\). The velocity is written in bold type, as in chapter 2; the board writes it with a tilde under the letter, and writes its Cartesian components \(V_x,V_y,V_z\). The volume of a region is \(\forall\) and an element of volume is \(\mathrm d\forall\), as in chapter 2, because \(V\) is a velocity. The law is conservation of mass. The tool is the theorem that this chapter derives. Unless a section says otherwise, \(\rho\) and \(\mathbf V\) are assumed to be continuous and to have continuous first derivatives in space and time; the places where this assumption is used, and where it fails, are marked.

Two derivatives occur, and they differ. For a field \(f(\mathbf x,t)\), for instance the density, the material derivative is the rate of change of \(f\) seen by a fluid particle that moves with the flow. A particle that is at \(\mathbf X(t)\) at time \(t\) satisfies \(\mathrm d\mathbf X/\mathrm dt=\mathbf V(\mathbf X,t)\). The chain rule applied to \(f(\mathbf X(t),t)\) gives

\[ \frac{\mathrm Df}{\mathrm Dt}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\mathbf V\cdot\nabla f. \tag{4.1} \]

The first term is the change at a fixed point; the second is the change caused by the motion of the particle to a point where \(f\) is different. The board writes the material derivative as \(\mathrm D/\mathrm Dt\), and so does this chapter. No diameter is written with the letter \(D\) in this chapter (diameters are given in words and metres), so the letter has one meaning here. An ordinary derivative \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dt\) is the derivative of a function of time alone, for instance the level \(h(t)\) of the tank; the lecture warns against reading \(\mathrm dh/\mathrm dt\) as a material derivative. Later sections apply the symbol \(\mathrm D/\mathrm Dt\) to a second object, the property of a set of particles; the definition is given with that use.

Balance laws, control mass and control volume L7 0:13

The question. Which laws govern a flow, and why can they not be applied directly to the region in which the engineer is interested?

The lecture lists three balance laws. Conservation of mass leads to the continuity equation. The balance of momentum relates the force on a body of fluid, the sum of body forces and surface forces, to the rate of change of its momentum; it gives the momentum equations. The Navier–Stokes equations additionally use a constitutive model for a Newtonian fluid. The balance of energy involves the internal energy and the kinetic energy of the fluid; the lecture restricts the course to the mechanical energy, that is the kinetic and potential energy. This chapter derives only the first of these laws in the flow setting. The other two enter the chapter only as entries of Table 4.1, which show which specific property \(\eta\) each of them uses.

Each of these laws is a statement about a system, also called a control mass: a fixed set of fluid particles. The system always contains the same particles. Its boundary moves with the fluid, so that no particle crosses it, and its mass is constant. Newton's second law, for example, relates the force on a chosen amount of mass to the change of momentum of that mass.

The engineer's question is different. The lecture's example is a jet engine. Air enters the engine at the inlet; a compressor, a combustion chamber and a turbine on a common shaft act on it; hot gas leaves at high speed. To follow a particle of air through the engine and out of it is impractical, because it passes through complicated motions. The practical choice is a region of space, drawn around the engine, and a balance of what enters, what leaves and what accumulates inside. Such a region is a control volume (abbreviated CV) and its boundary is the control surface (CS). Fluid crosses the control surface. A control volume is chosen by the analyst and need not coincide with any set of particles.

Two conventions are fixed here for the whole chapter. The unit normal \(\mathbf n\) to the control surface points outward, away from the control volume. An element of the control surface is written as the vector \(\mathrm d\mathbf A=\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA\), where \(\mathrm dA\) is its area; the board writes the area of a port as a vector \(\mathbf A_i\) in the same way. The product \(\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A\) is then positive where fluid leaves the control volume and negative where it enters. What is needed is a rule that expresses the rate of change of a quantity of a system through quantities defined on a control volume. The rule is the Reynolds transport theorem.

A property of a body of fluid and its specific property L7 11:45

The theorem is stated for any property that a body of fluid carries. Let \(N\) be such a property (mass, momentum, energy), and let \(\eta(\mathbf x,t)\) be the specific property, the amount of \(N\) per unit mass, so that an element of fluid of mass \(\rho\,\mathrm d\forall\) carries \(\eta\rho\,\mathrm d\forall\). The amount of \(N\) in a region of volume \(\forall\) is

\[ N=\int_\forall \rho\,\eta\,\mathrm d\forall . \tag{4.2} \]

The quantity \(\eta\) may be a scalar or a vector; \(\mathrm d\forall\) is the volume element. Table 4.1 gives the three choices of the lecture.

The mass-balance applications below use \(\eta=1\); the transport theorem is first derived for a general specific property.

Two integrals of the form (4.2) are needed, and they must not be confused. \(N_{cv}(t)\) is the integral over the control volume, the region fixed by the analyst. \(N_S(t)\) is the integral over the system \(S(t)\), the set of particles that fill the control volume at one chosen instant and then move with the flow. At that instant the two regions coincide and \(N_{cv}=N_S\). Afterwards they differ, because particles leave the control volume and others enter it. The material rate of change of the system's property is the quantity that the physical law governs, and the lecture writes it \(\mathrm DN/\mathrm Dt\):

\[ \frac{\mathrm DN}{\mathrm Dt}\equiv\frac{\mathrm dN_S}{\mathrm dt}. \]

For mass, \(\mathrm DN/\mathrm Dt\) is zero, as the section on conservation of mass states. For momentum it is the net force on the system, and for energy it is set by the work and heat added; those two uses are outside this chapter.

The accumulation balance for a control volume with ports L7 15:46

The question. A control volume has one inlet port and one exit port. How does \(N_{cv}\) change in a short time \(\Delta t\), given how the system's property changes?

Setup. Take the control volume of Figure 4.1. It is fixed in space and its walls are rigid and closed to the fluid; fluid crosses the control surface only at the inlet port, of area \(A_i\) with outward normal pointing out of the control volume, and at the exit port, of area \(A_e\). The area vectors are \(\mathbf A_i=A_i\mathbf n_i\) and \(\mathbf A_e=A_e\mathbf n_e\), where \(\mathbf n_i\) and \(\mathbf n_e\) are the outward normals. The velocity of the fluid is \(\mathbf V_i\) over the whole inlet port and \(\mathbf V_e\) over the whole exit port, and \(\rho\) and \(\eta\) are uniform over each port. These uniformity assumptions are those of the lecture; they are removed in the next section.

Derivation. The hinge is to compare the system at \(t\) with the system at \(t+\Delta t\). At time \(t\) the system \(S\) is the fluid in the control volume, so \(N_S(t)=N_{cv}(t)\). At time \(t+\Delta t\) the particles of \(S\) have moved. Those that were within a distance \(|\mathbf V_e|\Delta t\) of the exit port have left the control volume. The right slab in Figure 4.1 marks their positions just inside the exit at time \(t\). At time \(t+\Delta t\) these same particles lie outside the control volume. The particles that were in the left slab at time \(t\) are not in \(S\); they are inside the control volume at time \(t+\Delta t\), mixed with the particles of \(S\), and must be subtracted when the contents of the control volume are used to count \(S\). Therefore

  1. \(N_S(t+\Delta t)=N_{cv}(t+\Delta t)-N_{\text{in}}+N_{\text{out}}\), where \(N_{\text{in}}\) is the amount of \(N\) in the particles that entered during \(\Delta t\) and \(N_{\text{out}}\) is the amount in the particles that left.
  2. The entering particles fill a slab whose base is the port and whose height along the normal to the port is \(-\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf n_i\,\Delta t\). The volume of the slab is \(-\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i\,\Delta t\), which is positive because \(\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i\lt0\). Its mass is \(\rho\,(-\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i)\,\Delta t\) and its content of \(N\) is \(N_{\text{in}}=-\rho\eta\,\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i\,\Delta t\). The same argument at the exit gives \(N_{\text{out}}=\rho\eta\,\mathbf V_e\cdot\mathbf A_e\,\Delta t\).
  3. Subtract \(N_S(t)=N_{cv}(t)\) and divide by \(\Delta t\): \(\dfrac{N_S(t+\Delta t)-N_S(t)}{\Delta t}=\dfrac{N_{cv}(t+\Delta t)-N_{cv}(t)}{\Delta t}-\dot I+\dot E\), where \(\dot I=N_{\text{in}}/\Delta t\) and \(\dot E=N_{\text{out}}/\Delta t\).
  4. Let \(\Delta t\to0\). The slabs become thin, so the assumption of uniform values over a slab holds exactly in the limit.

The board's accumulation balance, with the board's names for the terms, is

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\mathrm dN_{cv}}{\mathrm dt}}_{\text{accumulation}}=\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm DN}{\mathrm Dt}}_{\text{material growth}}+\dot I-\dot E, \tag{4.3} \]

with the influx and the efflux of the property

\[ \dot I=-\rho\,\eta\,\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i,\qquad \dot E=\rho\,\eta\,\mathbf V_e\cdot\mathbf A_e . \tag{4.4} \]

Substituting (4.4) into (4.3) gives the form that holds for any number of ports once the terms of all ports are added:

\[ \frac{\mathrm dN_{cv}}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm DN}{\mathrm Dt}-\rho\eta\,\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i-\rho\eta\,\mathbf V_e\cdot\mathbf A_e. \tag{4.5} \]

Interpretation. The accumulation is the rate at which the amount of \(N\) in the fixed region changes. The influx \(\dot I\) is the rate at which the entering fluid brings \(N\) in, and the efflux \(\dot E\) is the rate at which the leaving fluid takes it away; the minus sign in \(\dot I\) is the sign of \(\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i\), which is negative for an inlet whose outward normal opposes the flow. The term \(\mathrm DN/\mathrm Dt\) is the rate of change of the property of the particles that fill the control volume at time \(t\), followed as they move. The board calls it the generation, or the material growth, of \(N\). The word does not mean that fluid is created: for a quantity such as the mass of one chemical species it is the rate at which a reaction makes that species, and for the total mass it is zero.

Assumptions. The control volume is fixed and rigid, and only the ports let fluid across its surface. The density, the specific property and the velocity are uniform over each port and over the thin slab. Each port is flat, so that each slab is a prism; its height along the normal to the port is \(|\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf n|\Delta t\), which is why the dot product appears when the velocity is oblique to the port. The steady special case checks the signs. For \(N\) the mass of the system, \(\eta=1\) and \(\mathrm DN/\mathrm Dt=0\), and a steady flow has \(\mathrm dN_{cv}/\mathrm dt=0\), so (4.3) gives \(\dot I=\dot E\), that is \(\rho_iV_iA_i=\rho_eV_eA_e\) for velocities normal to the ports. The mass that enters per second equals the mass that leaves per second, as it must.

The theorem for a general control surface L7 25:52

The lecture generalizes (4.5) to a closed control surface of any shape. The control surface is divided into small elements \(\mathrm d\mathbf A=\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA\). Through each element the particles that cross in a time \(\Delta t\) occupy a slanted prism with the element as base and height \((\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf n)\Delta t\), so the volume that crosses is \((\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A)\,\Delta t\) with the sign of \(\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf n\): positive when the fluid leaves, negative when it enters. The element carries the amount \(\rho\eta\,\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A\) of \(N\) per unit time out of the control volume. Wherever the fluid does not cross, \(\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf n=0\) and the element contributes nothing; this is why a rigid wall of the control surface does not appear in (4.5). Summing over all elements replaces the two port terms of (4.5) by an integral over the closed control surface:

\[ \frac{\mathrm DN}{\mathrm Dt}=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{cv}\rho\eta\,\mathrm d\forall+\oint_{cs}\rho\eta\,\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A . \tag{4.6} \]

This is the Reynolds transport theorem for a fixed control volume. The first term on the right is the accumulation in the control volume, and the second is the net rate at which \(N\) leaves through the control surface. The board writes it as \(\partial/\partial t\int\rho\eta\,\mathrm d\forall=\mathrm D/\mathrm Dt\int\rho\eta\,\mathrm d\forall-\oint\rho\,\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A\), with the partial derivative because the integral is over a fixed region; the first time the board writes the boxed form it leaves \(\eta\) out of the surface integral, and the form with \(\eta\) is the one that follows from (4.5) and the one written in the next lecture. The integral over a fixed region depends on \(t\) only, so \(\partial/\partial t\) and \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dt\) mean the same here; (4.6) uses \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dt\) so that the symbol does not suggest a derivative at a point.

What the argument has and has not shown. The prism argument of this section is a first-order argument for small \(\Delta t\) and small elements. It assumes that the fields do not change appreciably within one prism and that the control surface is smooth enough to be divided into flat elements. It also assumes a control volume that does not move. The next section removes the last assumption and also gives the derivation of the first-order claim.

The theorem for a control volume whose boundary moves

The question. Equation (4.6) holds for a control volume that is fixed in space. A piston, a free surface that rises, or an engine that moves through the air bounds a region that moves or deforms. What replaces (4.6) there, and what is the velocity that multiplies the area element?

Definitions. Let \(\Omega(t)\) be a region whose boundary is piecewise smooth and whose boundary points move with a given velocity \(\mathbf w(\mathbf x,t)\), called the boundary velocity. For a control volume the boundary velocity is written \(\mathbf V_{cs}\); it is the velocity of the points of the control surface and need not equal the fluid velocity \(\mathbf V\). For the system \(S(t)\) the boundary points are made of the same particles at every time, because no particle crosses the surface of a system, so the boundary velocity of \(S\) is the fluid velocity \(\mathbf V\).

Lemma (Leibniz rule for a moving region). Let \(f(\mathbf x,t)\) be a scalar field with continuous \(f\) and \(\partial f/\partial t\) in a neighbourhood of the region during the interval considered, and let the boundary of \(\Omega(t)\) move continuously with the continuous velocity \(\mathbf w\). Then

\[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{\Omega(t)}f\,\mathrm d\forall=\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\,\mathrm d\forall+\oint_{\partial\Omega(t)}f\,\mathbf w\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A . \tag{4.7} \]

Derivation. Write the change over \(\Delta t\) as the sum of two differences:

\[ \int_{\Omega(t+\Delta t)}f(\mathbf x,t+\Delta t)\,\mathrm d\forall-\int_{\Omega(t)}f(\mathbf x,t)\,\mathrm d\forall =\int_{\Omega(t)}\bigl[f(\mathbf x,t+\Delta t)-f(\mathbf x,t)\bigr]\mathrm d\forall+\Bigl[\int_{\Omega(t+\Delta t)}-\int_{\Omega(t)}\Bigr]f(\mathbf x,t+\Delta t)\,\mathrm d\forall . \]

The first term on the right, divided by \(\Delta t\), tends to \(\int\partial f/\partial t\,\mathrm d\forall\) as \(\Delta t\to0\), because \(\partial f/\partial t\) is continuous. The second term is the integral of \(f\) over the layer between the two boundaries. The hinge is the volume of that layer. A surface element \(\mathrm dA\) with outward normal \(\mathbf n\) moves along \(\mathbf n\) by \((\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf n)\Delta t\) to first order, so it sweeps a prism of volume \((\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf n)\Delta t\,\mathrm dA\) (Figure 4.2); the sign is positive where the boundary moves outward and the region gains volume, negative where it moves inward. In the limit the value of \(f\) in the layer is its value on the surface, so the second term divided by \(\Delta t\) tends to \(\oint f\,\mathbf w\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A\). Corners and edges of the surface contribute terms of higher order in \(\Delta t\). This gives (4.7). \(\blacksquare\)

The first term of (4.7) is the change of the integral caused by the change of \(f\) in place, and the second is the change caused by the movement of the boundary. If \(\mathbf w=\mathbf 0\), only the first term is left, and the derivative moves inside the integral.

The theorem for a moving control volume. Apply (4.7) with \(f=\rho\eta\) twice. For the system, \(\mathbf w=\mathbf V\), and \(\mathrm DN/\mathrm Dt=\mathrm dN_S/\mathrm dt\) is

\[ \frac{\mathrm DN}{\mathrm Dt}=\int_{S(t)}\frac{\partial(\rho\eta)}{\partial t}\,\mathrm d\forall+\oint_{\partial S(t)}\rho\eta\,\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A . \]

For the control volume, \(\mathbf w=\mathbf V_{cs}\), and

\[ \frac{\mathrm dN_{cv}}{\mathrm dt}=\int_{cv(t)}\frac{\partial(\rho\eta)}{\partial t}\,\mathrm d\forall+\oint_{cs(t)}\rho\eta\,\mathbf V_{cs}\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A . \]

At the chosen instant \(S\) and the control volume are the same region, so the two volume integrals have the same integrand over the same region and cancel when the second line is subtracted from the first. What is left is

\[ \frac{\mathrm DN}{\mathrm Dt}=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{cv(t)}\rho\eta\,\mathrm d\forall+\oint_{cs(t)}\rho\eta\,(\mathbf V-\mathbf V_{cs})\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A . \tag{4.8} \]

Interpretation. The vector \(\mathbf V-\mathbf V_{cs}\) is the velocity of the fluid relative to the surface, and only its component along the normal carries \(N\) across the surface. Fluid that slides along the surface, and a surface that moves with the fluid, carry nothing across it. Two checks follow. If \(\mathbf V_{cs}=\mathbf 0\) the equation is (4.6). If \(\mathbf V_{cs}=\mathbf V\) everywhere on the surface, the surface integral is zero and \(\mathrm DN/\mathrm Dt=\mathrm dN_{cv}/\mathrm dt\), as it must be, because a control volume that moves with the fluid is a system. A control volume that moves at a constant velocity \(\mathbf U\) has \(\mathbf V_{cs}=\mathbf U\) on its whole surface, and the surface integral involves \(\mathbf V-\mathbf U\), the fluid velocity as seen from the control volume.

Assumptions and boundary. The field \(\rho\eta\) is continuously differentiable in space and time in a neighbourhood of the region, the fluid velocity is continuous so that the system deforms continuously, the boundary of the control volume is piecewise smooth, and its velocity \(\mathbf V_{cs}\) is prescribed. The derivation is exact in the limit \(\Delta t\to0\) and does not assume uniform values over a port. A common error is to use the fluid velocity \(\mathbf V\) in the surface integral of a moving control volume. Exercise 3 shows that this error doubles the outlet speed of a piston.

Conservation of mass in integral form L8A 0:14

The question. What does the theorem say when the property is the mass?

Take \(\eta=1\), so that \(N\) is the mass \(M\) of the fluid. The law of conservation of mass is the statement that a system neither gains nor loses mass: \(\mathrm DM/\mathrm Dt=0\) for every system. The lecture states it as the basic law; it is a hypothesis of the model, valid when matter is not created or destroyed, which excludes nuclear reactions and conversion of mass to energy; a chemical reaction rearranges the species but does not change the total mass. Setting \(\mathrm DM/\mathrm Dt=0\) in (4.8) gives, for any control volume,

\[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{cv(t)}\rho\,\mathrm d\forall+\oint_{cs(t)}\rho\,(\mathbf V-\mathbf V_{cs})\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A=0 . \tag{4.9} \]

For a fixed control volume, \(\mathbf V_{cs}=\mathbf 0\), and (4.9) is the board's equation \(\partial/\partial t\int\rho\,\mathrm d\forall=-\oint\rho\,\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A\). The mass in the control volume increases at the rate at which mass enters through the surface minus the rate at which it leaves.

For a density with a jump, the smooth transport-theorem proof above is insufficient. Conservation can instead be stated over a time interval: the final mass minus the initial mass equals minus the time integral of the outward relative mass flux. This statement counts conserved particles without differentiating the density at the jump. It assumes that there is no mass source and no separate mass sheet on the interface; bounded fluxes and one-sided traces then permit the thin-box limit used in Exercise 5.

Two derivatives with two meanings. The lecture insists on a distinction that (4.9) makes explicit. The term \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dt\int_{cv}\rho\,\mathrm d\forall\) is the rate of change of the mass in a fixed place; the particles in it are not the same at two times. The term \(\mathrm DM/\mathrm Dt\) follows the particles that were inside at time \(t\), wherever they go, and conservation of mass says its value is zero. The first term can be positive while the second is zero, as in a tank that fills, because the mass in the control volume grows by the arrival of new particles and not by the creation of mass. The air tank below shows this.

Steady flow. A flow is steady if \(\rho\), \(\mathbf V\) and every other field at a fixed point do not change with time. For a fixed control volume the first term of (4.9) is then zero, and

\[ \oint_{cs}\rho\,\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A=0 . \tag{4.10} \]

The mass flow rate through a port is \(\dot m=\int\rho\,\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A\), with the outward normal, so that it is positive for outflow; for a flat port of area \(A\), density \(\rho\) and speed \(V\) normal to the port it is \(\pm\rho VA\). The volume flow rate is \(Q=VA\), and the mean speed over a port is \(V_{\text{avg}}=Q/A\). Equation (4.10) says that the total of the mass flow rates is zero: for one inlet and one exit, \(\rho_iV_iA_i=\rho_eV_eA_e\). The volume flow rates \(V_iA_i\) and \(V_eA_e\) are equal only if the densities are equal.

The water tank and the choice of control volume L8A 6:28

The question. At what rate does the level in the running-case tank change?

Model. The tank has a vertical wall and a constant cross-section of area \(A_t=\pi(5\ \mathrm m)^2/4=19.63\ \mathrm{m^2}\) over the range of \(h\) considered. The inlet pipe has area \(A_{\text{in}}=\pi(0.10\ \mathrm m)^2/4=7.854\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2}\) and the water in it moves at the uniform speed \(V_i=3\ \mathrm{m/s}\); the outlet pipe has area \(A_{\text{out}}=\pi(0.06\ \mathrm m)^2/4=2.827\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2}\) and the water moves at \(V_e=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Both pipes run full. The water surface in the tank is horizontal, and the density of the water, \(\rho_w\), is uniform and constant; the lecture takes \(\rho_w=10^3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\). The relative density change of water at rest in a column 20 m deep is \(8.7\times10^{-5}\) (chapter 2, section When the density varies: the atmosphere and the compressibility of water), which supports a constant density at pressures of this size.

The lecture chooses the fixed control volume drawn in dashed lines around the tank and the two pipe ends. It cuts the pipes at sections where the velocity is normal to the cut. The point of that choice is that \(\mathbf V\) and \(\mathrm d\mathbf A\) are parallel or antiparallel on every surface where fluid crosses, so that \(\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A=\pm V\,\mathrm dA\) and the integral is a product. The control volume contains water and, above the water, air. The question concerns the mass of water. Let \(\rho\) in (4.9) be the mass of water per unit volume: equal to the constant \(\rho_w\) where there is water and zero where there is air. A system made of the water particles in the control volume at time \(t\) keeps its mass, so (4.9) holds with this density. The lecture makes the same point: air is replaced by water in the tank but is not converted to water, and the balance for the air is a separate balance.

The water mass density jumps at the free surface, so the smooth-field derivation of the transport theorem does not apply there pointwise. The integral mass balance still follows by counting conserved water particles: the change of water mass inside the region equals the water mass that crosses its boundary. We assume no evaporation and no mass stored as a separate sheet on the interface. Thus (4.9) is used here as integral conservation of water mass; no derivative of the discontinuous density at the free surface is taken.

Derivation. The mass of water in the control volume is \(\rho_w(A_th+\forall_0)\), where \(\forall_0\) is the volume of water in the pipes inside the control volume, which does not change. Its rate of change is \(\rho_wA_t\,\mathrm dh/\mathrm dt\); the derivative is an ordinary derivative because \(h\) depends on \(t\) alone. Water crosses the control surface only at the two pipe sections. The continuity equation from the integral form L8B 0:14

The question. Equation (4.9) is a statement about a region. Is there a statement of conservation of mass at a point, and under what conditions does it follow from (4.9)?

Derivation. Take a control volume that is fixed in space and lies inside the flow. Equation (4.9) with \(\mathbf V_{cs}=\mathbf 0\) is \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dt\int\rho\,\mathrm d\forall+\oint\rho\,\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A=0\). Three steps turn it into a pointwise equation.

  1. Move the time derivative inside the integral. For a fixed region this is (4.7) with \(\mathbf w=\mathbf 0\) and \(f=\rho\); it needs \(\partial\rho/\partial t\) to be continuous.
  2. Convert the surface integral to a volume integral. The divergence theorem, taken from vector calculus, states that for a vector field \(\mathbf F\) whose components have continuous first derivatives in a closed region with a piecewise smooth boundary, \[ \oint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A=\int_\Omega\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,\mathrm d\forall,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}. \tag{4.15} \] The hypothesis is that \(\mathbf F=\rho\mathbf V\) is continuously differentiable throughout the control volume. With \(\mathbf F=\rho\mathbf V\), (4.15) turns the surface integral into the volume integral of \(\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)\), and the two volume integrals combine: \[ \int_{cv}\Bigl[\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)\Bigr]\mathrm d\forall=0 . \] This holds for every fixed region in the flow, small or large.
  3. Use that the region is arbitrary. Let \(g=\partial\rho/\partial t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)\), which is continuous under the hypotheses of the steps above. Suppose \(g(\mathbf x_0)\ne0\) at some point, say \(g(\mathbf x_0)\gt0\). By continuity there is a small ball \(B\) around \(\mathbf x_0\) in which \(g\gt g(\mathbf x_0)/2\), so \(\int_Bg\,\mathrm d\forall\gt\tfrac12g(\mathbf x_0)\,\forall_B\gt0\), which contradicts the equation for the control volume \(B\). The case \(g(\mathbf x_0)\lt0\) is the same with the inequalities reversed. Hence \(g=0\) at every point.

The result is the continuity equation in differential form:

\[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)=0 . \tag{4.16} \]

Interpretation. The quantity \(\rho\mathbf V\) is the mass flux, the mass that crosses a unit area per unit time (units kg/(m\(^2\) s)), and \(\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)\) is the net outflow of mass per unit volume and unit time (kg/(m\(^3\) s)). The density at a point rises where the mass flux converges, and it falls where the flux diverges.

Boundary of the result. The lecture calls the integral form and the pointwise form equivalent. The derivation shows that (4.16) follows from (4.9) where \(\rho\) and \(\mathbf V\) are continuously differentiable. It does not follow where they are not. At a surface across which \(\rho\) or \(\mathbf V\) jumps, for example the front between oil and water in a pipe, the derivatives in (4.16) do not exist, and only the integral form holds. The code below shows both facts for a front that moves with the flow. A point that the front crosses in a time \(\Delta t\) sees the density change by \(-148\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) in \(\Delta t\), so the difference quotient \((\rho(t+\Delta t)-\rho(t))/\Delta t\) is \(-1.48\times10^5\ \mathrm{kg/(m^3\,s)}\) for \(\Delta t=10^{-3}\) s and \(-1.48\times10^7\) for \(10^{-5}\) s: it has no limit. The mass in a fixed length of pipe still changes at the rate \(-296\ \mathrm{kg/(m^2\,s)}\) per unit cross-section, and this equals the mass flux in minus the mass flux out. The integral form is the more general statement, and (4.16) is its form for smooth fields. Exercise 5 derives the condition that replaces (4.16) at the front.

Steady, incompressible and divergence-free flow L8B 14:50

The question. The continuity equation (4.16) is often used in simpler forms. What conditions license each form, and how are the conditions different from each other?

The product rule gives \(\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)=\mathbf V\cdot\nabla\rho+\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf V\). Combined with (4.1), \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=\partial\rho/\partial t+\mathbf V\cdot\nabla\rho\), the equation (4.16) is

\[ \frac{\mathrm D\rho}{\mathrm Dt}+\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0 . \tag{4.17} \]

This holds for every flow in which (4.16) holds, compressible or not. It says that the divergence of the velocity is the fractional rate at which the density of a particle falls, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=-(1/\rho)\,\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt\). Equivalently, because the mass \(\rho\,\delta\forall\) of a particle is constant, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V\) is the fractional rate at which its volume \(\delta\forall\) grows.

Three conditions are in use, and they are different.

  1. Steady flow. Every field at a fixed point is independent of time, \(\partial\rho/\partial t=0\). Then (4.16) becomes \(\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)=0\). The density may still vary from point to point and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V\) need not vanish.
  2. Incompressible flow. The density of each fluid particle does not change as the particle moves, \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\) at every point. (This is the definition on the board. It describes a flow and not only a fluid: the same liquid or gas can be in a flow that satisfies it and in one that does not.) Then (4.17) gives \(\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0\), and since \(\rho\gt0\), \[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0 . \tag{4.18} \] Equation (4.18) is a result of conservation of mass for a flow that has \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\), and it holds for unsteady as well as steady flow. It is not a law of fluids. The velocity is then called divergence-free, and some authors call (4.18) the continuity equation of an incompressible flow.
  3. Spatially uniform density. At each time the density is the same at every position, so \(\rho=\rho(t)\) and \(\nabla\rho=\mathbf0\). It may still change with time. Equation (4.17) then reads \(\mathrm d\rho/\mathrm dt+\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0\); the air tank has this form and is compressible. The stronger condition density constant in space and time, \(\rho=\rho_0\), gives \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\) and hence incompressible flow. Its converse is false: an incompressible flow can carry a non-uniform density profile.

Steady and not incompressible. On the domain \(x\gt-L\), let a one-dimensional flow have \(V_x=U_0(1+x/L)\) and \(\rho=\rho_0/(1+x/L)\), with \(U_0=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(L=5\ \mathrm m\) and \(\rho_0=1.2\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\). The mass flux \(\rho V_x=\rho_0U_0\) is constant, so \(\partial\rho/\partial t=0\) and \(\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf V)=0\): the field satisfies (4.16) and is steady. Yet \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=\mathrm dV_x/\mathrm dx=U_0/L=0.4\ \mathrm s^{-1}\) and, at \(x=3\) m, \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=V_x\,\mathrm d\rho/\mathrm dx=-0.300\ \mathrm{kg/(m^3\,s)}\), equal to \(-\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf V\) as (4.17) requires. The field satisfies the continuity equation; whether any real flow has it also depends on the momentum and energy balances, which this chapter does not use.

Incompressible, unsteady and with non-uniform density. Let \(\rho=\rho_f(x-Ut)\), where \(\rho_f\) is a smooth, nonconstant profile, and let \(\mathbf V=U\mathbf i\) with constant nonzero \(U\). Then \(\partial\rho/\partial t=-U\rho_f'\) and \(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla\rho=U\rho_f'\). Thus \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\), \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0\), and (4.16) holds exactly. Where the profile changes, the density varies in both space and time at a fixed point. For the smooth 850–998 \(\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) front and the difference steps used below, the computed residual prints as 0.00e+00. This finite-precision check complements the analytic cancellation; it does not prove that the numerical residual vanishes for every step or field. A sharp oil–water front still transports each particle without changing its density, but its pointwise density derivatives are undefined at the front. Integral mass conservation and the front condition in Exercise 5 replace the smooth pointwise calculation there.

Compressible, with small speeds. In the air tank of the lead the density is uniform in space and rises with time, so \(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla\rho=0\) and \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=\mathrm d\rho/\mathrm dt=4.18\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg/(m^3\,s)}\). Then (4.17) gives \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=-(1/\rho)\,\mathrm d\rho/\mathrm dt=-3.55\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm s^{-1}\) at the start, when \(\rho=\rho_1=1.176\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\). The flow in the tank is not incompressible in the sense of the definition, although the speed in the pipe is 2 m/s. The density of the gas in the tank rises by a factor \(\rho_2/\rho_1=2.96\) during the 552 s of the fill. The definition is exact: a flow has \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\) everywhere, or it does not. Whether the approximation \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0\) is acceptable is a separate judgement that depends on the quantity that the analysis needs. This chapter does not derive a speed criterion, such as a limit on the Mach number, for it.

The mass balance of the air tank used none of these simplifications. It used (4.9) with a fixed control volume and the density as a function of time alone, which is why it holds for the compressible gas and for the water alike.

The two cases test one idea at two levels. The water tank has a density constant in space and time and a moving free surface; the mass balance reduces to a balance of volume flow rates and gives \(9.12\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The air tank has a uniform but changing density and a fixed control volume; the balance gives the density rate in one line, and the fill time follows. The failure of the constant inlet speed shows that the mass balance is exact while the inflow it uses is data.

What the chapter establishes and what it does not

The results are exact within their hypotheses, which are of three kinds. The model assumptions are the continuum, conservation of mass for every system (\(\mathrm DM/\mathrm Dt=0\)), and, for the theorem, the continuity of \(\rho\eta\) and its first derivatives and the continuity of the velocity. The idealizations are uniform velocity over a port, rigid walls that no fluid crosses, a flat free surface that no water crosses, a tank of constant cross-section, a uniform density in the air tank and a constant temperature. The approximations are the constant density of water in the tank and the initial pressure \(1.013\times10^{5}\) Pa, taken as given, which differs from the standard 101,325 Pa by 0.02 %.

The results fail in identifiable places. Equation (4.6) is the special case \(\mathbf V_{cs}=\mathbf 0\) of (4.8), and using it for a control volume with a moving boundary loses the relative velocity (Exercise 3). The divergence theorem step fails when \(\rho\mathbf V\) is not continuously differentiable in the region (Exercise 2), and the smooth pointwise form (4.16) is undefined at a front across which \(\rho\) or \(\mathbf V\) jumps (Exercise 5). The condition \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0\) holds only for a flow with \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\) (Exercise 6). The fill time of the air tank (4.14) holds for a constant inflow and is a lower bound when the inflow falls. The mass balance does not by itself determine the inflow or the outflow, which are data; the momentum and energy balances, which this chapter does not use, are needed to relate them to the pressures.

The reader can now distinguish a system from a control volume; derive the fixed and moving forms of the transport theorem; apply the integral mass balance with a stated choice of control volume; derive the smooth continuity equation in two ways; and distinguish steady flow, incompressible flow, spatially uniform density, and density constant in both space and time. The water tank gives \(9.12\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s}\); the constant-inflow air-tank model gives 551.9 s. Each calculation includes the hypotheses needed to interpret its answer.

Exercise 1 (derive and compute: the Leibniz rule). A one-dimensional region is the interval \(\Omega(t)=[0,2t]\), with the field \(f(x,t)=xt\). The left end is fixed and the right end moves at \(w=2\) (units of length per unit time). (a) Evaluate \(I(t)=\int_0^{2t}f\,\mathrm dx\) and \(\mathrm dI/\mathrm dt\) directly. (b) Evaluate the right side of the one-dimensional form of (4.7), \(\int\partial f/\partial t\,\mathrm dx+\sum f\,(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf n)\), where the sum is over the two ends and \(\mathbf n\) is the outward normal of the interval. (c) What does the volume term alone give at \(t=1\)? (d) For a sphere of radius \(R(t)\) and \(f=1\), what do the two terms of (4.7) give?

Solution

(a) \(I(t)=t\int_0^{2t}x\,\mathrm dx=t\,(2t)^2/2=2t^3\), so \(\mathrm dI/\mathrm dt=6t^2\). (b) The volume term is \(\int_0^{2t}\partial(xt)/\partial t\,\mathrm dx=\int_0^{2t}x\,\mathrm dx=2t^2\). At the right end \(\mathbf n=+1\), \(w=2\) and \(f=2t\cdot t=2t^2\), which gives \(f\,w\,n=4t^2\). At the left end \(w=0\), and the term is zero. The sum is \(2t^2+4t^2=6t^2\), equal to the direct result. (c) At \(t=1\) the volume term alone is 2, while the derivative is 6: the omitted boundary term is two thirds of the answer. (d) For \(f=1\), \(\partial f/\partial t=0\), so the volume term is zero. The boundary term is \(\oint\mathbf w\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A=R'\cdot4\pi R^2\) with \(\mathbf w=R'\mathbf n\), and it equals \(\mathrm d(\tfrac43\pi R^3)/\mathrm dt\), as it must. With \(R=0.5\) m and \(R'=0.01\ \mathrm{m/s}\) the value is \(0.0314\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\).

Exercise 2 (check an assumption: a point source). Let \(\mathbf F=\mathbf x/r^3\), where \(\mathbf x\) is position measured from the origin and \(r=|\mathbf x|\). This geometric field is defined only for \(r\gt0\); it has units \(\mathrm{m^{-2}}\). The velocity of a physical source flow of volume flow rate \(Q\) is \(\mathbf V=(Q/4\pi)\mathbf F\). (a) Show that \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0\) for \(r\gt0\). (b) Compute its outward flux through a sphere of radius \(a\) centred at the origin. (c) Identify the failed hypothesis of (4.15). Then excise a ball of radius \(\varepsilon\lt a\) and account for both boundaries of the remaining shell. (d) For \(Q=0.01\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\), find the speed at \(r=0.5\) m.

Solution

(a) For \(r\gt0\), \(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf x r^{-3})=3r^{-3}-3r^{-5}\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x=0\). (b) On the sphere \(\mathbf n=\mathbf x/a\), so \(\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=1/a^2\). Multiplication by \(4\pi a^2\) gives flux \(4\pi\), independent of \(a\). The corresponding velocity flux is \(Q\).

(c) The field is undefined at the origin, so (4.15) cannot be applied to the full ball. On the shell \(\varepsilon\lt r\lt a\), however, it is smooth. The outward normal of the shell is radial outward at \(a\) and radial inward at \(\varepsilon\). The two fluxes are \(+4\pi\) and \(-4\pi\), whose sum is zero, equal to the volume integral of the divergence on the shell. For constant density, the inner boundary is a genuine inlet carrying mass rate \(\rho Q\); the outer boundary carries the same rate out. Thus (4.9) holds on the shell. If the origin is retained instead, mass injection must be added explicitly as a source term; it is not a port of the original outer boundary.

(d) \(V=Q/(4\pi r^2)=0.01/(4\pi\times0.25)=3.18\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The source strength supplies the units needed for a velocity.

Exercise 3 (compute: a control volume with a moving boundary). A syringe has a bore of diameter 30 mm and a nozzle of diameter 2 mm. The piston moves toward the nozzle at 5 mm/s and expels water; the density of the water is constant and the cylinder is full. (b) Repeat with the fluid velocity \(\mathbf V\) in place of \(\mathbf V-\mathbf V_{cs}\) at the piston face. (c) Which answer agrees with the volume swept out by the piston?

Solution

(a) The piston face of the control volume moves with \(\mathbf V_{cs}\) of speed \(V_p=5\ \mathrm{mm/s}\) toward the nozzle; the other faces are at rest. The water in contact with the piston moves with it, so \(\mathbf V=\mathbf V_{cs}\) at the piston face and \((\mathbf V-\mathbf V_{cs})\cdot\mathbf n=0\) there: the piston face contributes nothing to the surface integral. The mass in the control volume is \(\rho A\,x(t)\) plus a constant, where \(A=\pi(0.03)^2/4=7.069\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}\) and \(x\) is the distance from the piston to the nozzle section, with \(\mathrm dx/\mathrm dt=-V_p\). The same derivative follows from (4.7): the volume term is zero and the boundary term is \(\rho\,(\mathbf V_{cs}\cdot\mathbf n)A=-\rho V_pA\), because the outward normal at the piston face points away from the nozzle. The nozzle section has area \(A_n=\pi(0.002)^2/4=3.142\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}\) and contributes \(+\rho V_nA_n\). Then \(-\rho V_pA+\rho V_nA_n=0\), so \(V_n=V_pA/A_n=5\times10^{-3}\times225=1.125\ \mathrm{m/s}\). (b) With \(\mathbf V\) at the piston face the surface integral gets the extra term \(\rho\,(-V_p)A\): \(-\rho V_pA-\rho V_pA+\rho V_nA_n=0\), and \(V_n=2V_pA/A_n=2.25\ \mathrm{m/s}\). (c) The piston sweeps \(V_pA=3.534\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\), which a nozzle speed of 1.125 m/s carries away (\(1.125\times3.142\times10^{-6}\)). The answer of (b) would carry \(7.07\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\), twice the water the piston displaces, so it is wrong. The omission of \(\mathbf V_{cs}\) doubles the answer.

Exercise 4 (design and judge: filling a tank). A rigid tank of volume \(0.50\ \mathrm{m^3}\) holds air at \(1.0\times10^{5}\) Pa and 300 K. Solution

(a) Let the box have two parallel faces of area \(A\), at distances \(\delta\) on the two sides of the front. Translate the box with boundary velocity \(\mathbf V_{cs}=w\mathbf n\). Write its mass as \(M_\delta(t)\). Bounded density gives \(0\le M_\delta(t)\le2\rho_{\max}\delta A\), so \(M_\delta(t_1)-M_\delta(t_0)\to0\) on every fixed time interval \([t_0,t_1]\). This bound concerns the change of mass, not its time derivative.

Use the time-integrated mass balance associated with (4.9) from \(t_0\) to \(t_1\). The side faces contribute \(O(\delta)\), because their areas are proportional to \(\delta\) and the relative mass flux is bounded. As \(\delta\to0\), the other two face fluxes tend to their one-sided limits. Their outward normals are \(+\mathbf n\) on side 2 and \(-\mathbf n\) on side 1. Let \(P\) be their common limiting patch on the front, of area \(A\), translated with the front. Therefore

\[ \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\int_P\Bigl[\rho_2(\mathbf V_2\cdot\mathbf n-w)-\rho_1(\mathbf V_1\cdot\mathbf n-w)\Bigr]\,\mathrm dA\,\mathrm dt=0 . \]

The traces in the integrand may vary over the patch. The interval and patch are arbitrary. Their integrand is continuous in time and along the front, so it must vanish: a nonzero value would have one sign on a small patch and time interval, contradicting the zero integral. This gives \(\rho_1(\mathbf V_1\cdot\mathbf n-w)=\rho_2(\mathbf V_2\cdot\mathbf n-w)\) locally. For states uniform along the front, the inner integral is simply \(A\) times the bracket. A front carrying a separate surface mass would have its own storage term; the stated condition excludes that case.

(b) For a material oil–water front, \(\mathbf V_1\cdot\mathbf n=\mathbf V_2\cdot\mathbf n=w\). Both relative fluxes vanish, and the densities need not be equal. (c) The relative mass flux is \(1.2(300-100)=240\ \mathrm{kg/(m^2\,s)}\). Thus \(2.4(V_{2n}-100)=240\), where \(V_{2n}=\mathbf V_2\cdot\mathbf n\), and \(V_{2n}=200\ \mathrm{m/s}\). These data satisfy mass conservation; momentum and energy balances would be needed to decide whether such a physical front can occur.

Exercise 6 (counterexample: what does each condition imply?). For each field below find \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V\) and \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt\), check (4.16), and classify steadiness, incompressibility, spatial uniformity of density, and constancy of density in space and time. Take \(\rho_0\gt0\). (a) \(\rho=\rho_0\), \(\mathbf V=(ax,-ay,0)\), \(a=2\ \mathrm s^{-1}\). (b) \(\rho=\rho_0\mathrm e^{-bt}\), \(\mathbf V=\tfrac b3(x,y,z)\), \(b=0.3\ \mathrm s^{-1}\), \(\rho_0=1.2\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\). (c) \(\rho=\rho_0[1+0.1\sin(kx-\omega t)]\), \(\mathbf V=U\mathbf i\), \(U=\omega/k\), where \(k\ne0\) is a wavenumber in \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\) and \(\omega\ne0\) is an angular frequency in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

Solution

(a) \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=a-a=0\), \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\), and (4.16) is \(0+\rho_0\cdot0=0\). The flow is steady and incompressible; its density is uniform in space and constant in both space and time. (b) \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=3\cdot b/3=b=0.3\ \mathrm s^{-1}\). The density is uniform in space, so \(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla\rho=0\) and \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=\partial\rho/\partial t=-b\rho=-0.36\ \mathrm{kg/(m^3\,s)}\) at \(t=0\). Equation (4.16) gives \(-b\rho+\rho\,b=0\), so the field satisfies continuity. The flow is unsteady and not incompressible, even though the density is the same at every point at each time: spatial uniformity does not imply constancy in time. Unlike the filling air tank, this field has falling density and positive divergence: it describes expansion. (c) \(\partial\rho/\partial t=-0.1\rho_0\omega\cos(kx-\omega t)\), \(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla\rho=U\cdot0.1\rho_0k\cos(kx-\omega t)=0.1\rho_0\omega\cos(kx-\omega t)\), so \(\mathrm D\rho/\mathrm Dt=0\); \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0\); (4.16) is \(-0.1\rho_0\omega\cos+\,0.1\rho_0\omega\cos=0\). The flow is incompressible, unsteady and has a non-uniform density: a density wave carried by a uniform stream. Its density is neither spatially uniform nor constant in space and time.
