Flow in a pipe

Two open reservoirs have water surfaces at elevations 40 m and 5 m. A 200 m pipe of inside diameter 50 mm connects them through two elbows and two valves. The available 35 m of gravitational head drives about 4.58 L/s when the pipe has relative roughness 0.003. Raising 5.00 L/s through the same elevation difference instead requires about 5.35 kW of pump input at 70% efficiency. A calculation that omits friction cannot answer either question reliably. The engineering task is to connect pressure forces, wall stress, measured resistance and the energy budget, while checking whether each resistance law describes the flow that the calculation predicts.

Grounded in Lecture 22 (49:33), Lecture 23A (37:06) and Lecture 23B (25:24) of Prof. Sanjay Mittal's NPTEL Fluid Mechanics, IIT Kanpur. Lecture material: the Hagen–Poiseuille recap; entrance length and transition; roughness, dimensional pressure-loss formulation and the Moody diagram; turbulent correlations; fitting losses, elbows, gate and globe valves, contractions and expansions; and the two-reservoir calculation with its ideal comparison. Research extensions: Reynolds's disturbance-sensitive transition evidence; Nikuradse's measured rough-pipe resistance and smooth-pipe law; the reproduced Colebrook equation in Praks and Brkić's numerical-method paper; IAPWS water properties; and the finite low-Reynolds-number development length computed by Ray, Durst and Ray. Original teaching constructions: the wall-force and mechanical-power derivations, the explicit solver and error checks, the reverse-lift and diameter comparison, Figures 11.1–11.3 and six exercises. The source note at the end distinguishes several corrected board equations from the forms used here.

A model for delivery and lifting

The fluid is water at absolute temperature \(\Theta=300\ \mathrm K\), or 26.85 °C. Density \(\rho=996.556\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) comes from IAPWS R6-95(2018), Table 7, p. 15, at approximately 0.1 MPa. The dynamic viscosity, which relates viscous shear stress to a velocity gradient, is \(\mu=8.537\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\), rounded from the IAPWS 2008 viscosity formulation, equations (10)–(12), pp. 4–5, using the noncritical simplification on p. 8. The kinematic viscosity is \(\nu=\mu/\rho=8.567\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Uniform temperature and constant properties are modeling approximations throughout.

Let \(s\) measure distance along a straight pipe, \(D\) its inside diameter, \(R=D/2\) its radius, \(L\) its length, and \(A=\pi D^2/4\) its cross-sectional area. The volume flow rate \(Q\), in \(\mathrm{m^3/s}\), defines the bulk or cross-sectional mean velocity \(V_{\text{avg}}=Q/A\). In a turbulent flow this is also averaged over a time long compared with its fluctuations. Pressure \(p\) is in pascals; elevation \(z\) is measured vertically upward in metres. Gravitational acceleration has magnitude \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\).

The formal model consists of a flow rate, a mechanical-energy balance between named stations, and resistance relations for each component. A straight run contributes a distributed loss; a fitting contributes a localized loss. Conservation determines how the contributions combine. Exact laminar analysis or empirical turbulent correlations determine their magnitudes. This distinction survives every change of diameter, roughness or valve position.

The running case uses \(L=200\ \mathrm m\), \(D=0.050\ \mathrm m\), and roughness length \(\epsilon=0.000150\ \mathrm m\), hence \(\epsilon/D=0.003\). These are the reservoir example's geometry and roughness, with the water temperature specified here to close the calculation. The six fitting coefficients are 0.05, 5, 0.9, 0.9, 10 and 1; their meaning and limitations are developed below. The reverse-lift case is a constructed design extension with the same coefficients, rather than a claim about the behavior of these particular valves under reversed operation.

The exact laminar reference L22 2:35

A straight, smooth circular pipe has an exact reference solution. Assume an incompressible Newtonian continuum, constant viscosity, a stationary no-slip wall, steady laminar flow, no swirl, axisymmetry and full development. Full development means that the velocity profile is independent of \(s\); the pressure may still fall with \(s\). For a horizontal pipe define the signed pressure gradient \(K_p=\mathrm dp/\mathrm ds\), in \(\mathrm{Pa/m}\). The subscript distinguishes it from a fitting's loss coefficient introduced later.

Under these assumptions the axial momentum equation is \(K_p=(\mu/r)\,\mathrm d(r\,\mathrm dV_s/\mathrm dr)/\mathrm dr\), where \(r\) is radial distance and \(V_s\) is axial velocity. Its left side depends on \(s\) alone and its right side on \(r\) alone, so both are constant. Integration gives a quadratic term, a logarithm and a constant. Bounded velocity at \(r=0\) removes the logarithm; no slip at \(r=R\) fixes the constant. Thus

\[ V_s(r)=-\frac{K_p}{4\mu}(R^2-r^2),\quad V_{\text{avg}}=\frac1A\int_0^R V_s(r)2\pi r\,\mathrm dr =-\frac{K_pD^2}{32\mu},\quad V_{\max}=2V_{\text{avg}}. \tag{11.1} \]

The area integral weights outer rings by \(r\). This is why the mean is half the central maximum. Equation (11.1) is developed in detail in Chapter 7, “The Hagen–Poiseuille profile”; its role here is to supply a resistance law and a check on friction conventions. A falling pressure has \(K_p\lt0\), so it drives positive velocity. Reversing the pressure gradient reverses the velocity, an immediate sign test.

The diameter Reynolds number is \(\mathrm{Re}_D=\rho V_{\text{avg}}D/\mu=V_{\text{avg}}D/\nu\), dimensionless and based on bulk velocity and inside diameter. It compares inertial and viscous scales, but does not by itself certify a laminar state. If \(V_{\text{avg}}=0.10\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(D=1.0\ \mathrm{mm}\) and the stated water properties apply, then \(\mathrm{Re}_D=116.7\), and (11.1) gives a pressure drop of 2732 Pa over 1 m. At fixed flow rate, \(V_{\text{avg}}\propto D^{-2}\), so the laminar pressure drop varies as \(D^{-4}\). This is the engineering consequence of the exact solution; it holds only while its hypotheses remain appropriate.

Entrance length is part of the model L22 12:24

Fluid arriving from a reservoir can have an approximately uniform axial speed across most of the opening. No slip requires zero speed at the wall, so the initial profile cannot already be the parabola. Viscous momentum diffusion spreads the wall's influence inward, while continuity accelerates the core as slower fluid appears near the wall. A boundary layer here means the region adjacent to the wall in which velocity adjusts substantially from the incoming value. Full development is approached downstream; it is not established merely by entering a straight tube.

Define entrance length \(L_e\) as the distance required to satisfy a specified closeness criterion for a fully developed profile. A dimensional estimate balances diffusion across radius \(R\) in time \(t_d\sim R^2/\nu\) with travel \(L_e\sim V_{\text{avg}}t_d\). Consequently \(L_e/D\sim\mathrm{Re}_D/4\); the coefficient cannot be obtained from this scaling. The lecture gives the engineering estimates

\[ \frac{L_e}{D}\approx0.0575\mathrm{Re}_D\quad\text{(laminar)},\qquad \frac{L_e}{D}\approx4.4\mathrm{Re}_D^{0.167}\quad\text{(turbulent)}. \tag{11.2} \]

These are correlations, not consequences of the fully developed equations. Their inlet conditions, development criterion and calibration range are not specified in the recording. Use them as estimates with those limitations. At \(\mathrm{Re}_D=2300\), their arithmetic gives 132.25 and 16.03 diameters, respectively. The shorter turbulent estimate reflects stronger transverse momentum transport; it does not establish that turbulence actually exists at that Reynolds number. Applying both expressions is a conditional comparison of two assumed states.

The laminar estimate also fails as a universal low-Reynolds-number formula: it tends to zero as \(\mathrm{Re}_D\to0\), whereas axial viscous diffusion then matters. Ray, Durst and Ray's computations use a uniform inlet (p. 13) and a 99% centreline-velocity criterion (p. 16). Their no-slip pipe fit, equation (19), p. 22, has constants on p. 23:

\[ \frac{L_e}{D}=\left[0.6044^{1.5975} +(0.055935\mathrm{Re}_D)^{1.5975}\right]^{1/1.5975}. \tag{11.3} \]

Its zero-Reynolds-number limit is 0.6044 diameters, and its high-Reynolds-number laminar asymptote is close to (11.2). The reported maximum fit error is 2.47% against those computations, not an uncertainty bound for every real inlet. The analytical laminar branch can be computed beyond naturally occurring laminar operation; that is not evidence that a disturbed pipe will remain laminar there.

For the running case the eventual turbulent solution gives \(\mathrm{Re}_D\approx1.36\times10^5\). Equation (11.2) estimates \(L_e/D\approx31.7\), or about 1.6 m, compared with 200 m of pipe. This supports using a fully developed resistance over most of the long run. It does not remove local development after a valve or elbow. Short runs and closely spaced fittings need a combined developing-flow assessment; an entrance coefficient and a separate development model must not count the same dissipation twice.

Transition depends on disturbances L22 22:50

Laminar flow has organized motion without the irregular cross-stream transport characteristic of turbulence. A turbulent flow fluctuates in time and space even when its long-time mean is stationary. Reynolds's dye experiment distinguishes them visibly: a thin streak remains coherent in one state and spreads through the pipe in the other. A stationary mean turbulent profile must not be inserted into the laminar equation as though the fluctuations had vanished. Averaging the nonlinear momentum transport leaves additional correlations of fluctuating velocities.

Roughness, incoming disturbances and external vibration can change the transition observed in a given apparatus. Reynolds (1883), p. 943 (PDF p. 10), reports strong sensitivity of the critical velocity to disturbances before the water enters the tube. This is direct experimental evidence against a universal threshold. The lecture's unusually quiet-flow example at Reynolds number of order \(10^5\) is an illustration of that dependence; it is not adopted here as a design guarantee or independently verified measurement.

For routine classification the lectures use a laminar region roughly below 2000, a transitional interval roughly 2000–4000 and a turbulent region above 4000. These are operating conventions for choosing a correlation. A transition interval is not a license to interpolate between two unrelated formulas as though the interpolation were a physical law. Nikuradse's particular pipe experiments place initial transition around 2160–2500 (printed p. 11, PDF p. 13), which further identifies the number as an observation under particular conditions.

The distinction matters in the running case before any accurate friction calculation. Its velocities of order metres per second in a 50 mm tube put \(\mathrm{Re}_D\) around \(10^5\). The turbulent model is consistent with that operating assumption. A computed solution near 2300 would instead require a warning or additional evidence. More decimal places in the friction solve would not repair that missing physical information.

What a roughness length represents L22 28:48

An actual inner wall has peaks, valleys, spacing and shape. A dimensional roughness length \(\epsilon\), in metres, compresses this geometry into one parameter; relative roughness is \(\epsilon/D\). The lecture sketches an average surface irregularity. Engineering correlations more specifically use an equivalent sand roughness: a roughness length chosen to reproduce a reference sand-roughened surface's resistance. It need not equal an arithmetic surface-height average measured by a profilometer. Two surfaces with the same height statistic can have different drag because their spacing and shape differ.

Nikuradse's rough-pipe experiments provide a controlled reference, not a universal description of commercial pipes. On printed p. 10 (PDF p. 12) he identifies six roughness levels by average projection height \(k\) relative to pipe radius \(r_0\). In his notation \(k/r_0=2\epsilon/D\) if \(k\) is identified with the reference roughness height. That radius-to-diameter conversion must precede comparison with a Moody-type chart. His resistance factor \(\lambda\) is the Darcy factor used below.

The projected chart lists example roughness lengths in millimetres, including 0.0025 mm for glass/plastic/Perspex, 0.25 mm for coarse concrete and 3 mm for old sewers. These are the recording's indicative table entries, not certified values for a named installed pipe. A 0.15 mm roughness in a 50 mm pipe means \(0.15/50=0.003\); entering 0.15 as a dimensionless relative roughness would imply protrusions 7.5 mm high. Every roughness input therefore needs both its physical meaning and its unit.

The running case specifies \(\epsilon/D=0.003\). For the diameter comparison the physical roughness length is held fixed at 0.15 mm. Relative roughness then changes with diameter. Holding \(\epsilon/D\) fixed instead would compare different wall surfaces as the pipe size changes, a different design question.

Pressure drop from wall force and dimensional form L22 31:58

For a horizontal fully developed run define \(\Delta p=p_{\text{in}}-p_{\text{out}}\gt0\). Let \(\tau_w\gt0\) be the wall's resisting axial force per unit nominal wall area. Let \(\mathbf T\) denote total Cauchy stress. In a smooth circular laminar pipe the face-first stress convention gives \(T_{rs}=\mu\,\mathrm dV_s/\mathrm dr\) and \(\tau_w=-T_{rs}(R)\). The first index names the face normal, the second the traction direction, consistent with Chapter 2. The wall acts on the fluid upstream; the fluid acts on the wall downstream. For a rough pipe, \(\tau_w\) denotes the net axial wall resistance per nominal area, including pressure forces on roughness elements.

Take a cylindrical control volume of length \(L\). Its inlet and outlet momentum fluxes cancel because the profile and flow rate are identical at both ends. The downstream pressure force \(\Delta p A\) is balanced by upstream wall resistance \(\tau_w\pi DL\), as Figure 11.1 shows. For turbulent flow the cancellation is statistical, with matching mean momentum fluxes. Thus

\[ \Delta p\frac{\pi D^2}{4}=\tau_w\pi DL, \qquad \Delta p=\frac{4L}{D}\tau_w. \tag{11.4} \]

This is a force balance; it has not specified a turbulence law. For an inclined run replace \(\Delta p\) by the decrease of \(p+\rho gz\), assuming the same end profiles. Gravity can support the wall force even if pressure increases downstream. Confusing raw pressure drop with dissipative loss in a descending pipe would assign the gravitational work incorrectly.

The dynamic-pressure scale is \(\rho V_{\text{avg}}^2/2\). Define the skin-friction coefficient \(C_f\) and Darcy friction factor \(f_D\) by

\[ C_f=\frac{\tau_w}{\rho V_{\text{avg}}^2/2},\qquad f_D=\frac{\Delta p}{(L/D)\rho V_{\text{avg}}^2/2} =4C_f. \tag{11.5} \]

The factor four is geometric: the perimeter-to-area ratio is \(4/D\). Substituting (11.1) into (11.4) gives \(\tau_w=8\mu V_{\text{avg}}/D\); therefore \(C_f=16/\mathrm{Re}_D\) and \(f_D=64/\mathrm{Re}_D\). At \(\mathrm{Re}_D=1000\) these are 0.016 and 0.064. They are two normalizations of the same resistance, not competing predictions. Nikuradse's printed p. 12 (PDF p. 14) independently relates his \(\lambda\) to wall stress by \(\tau_w/\rho=\lambda V_{\text{avg}}^2/8\), confirming (11.5).

A dimensional formulation starts with seven variables \((\Delta p,V_{\text{avg}},D,\mu,\rho,\epsilon,L)\) and three independent base dimensions, mass, length and time. It permits four independent dimensionless groups, which may be chosen as \(\Delta p/(\rho V_{\text{avg}}^2/2)\), \(\mathrm{Re}_D\), \(\epsilon/D\) and \(L/D\). Dimensional analysis alone permits an arbitrary function of all three input groups. Statistical homogeneity of a fully developed uniform run adds the physical fact that equal lengths have equal mean pressure loss. Losses therefore add linearly with \(L\), giving

\[ \Delta p=f_D(\mathrm{Re}_D,\epsilon/D)\frac{L}{D} \frac{\rho V_{\text{avg}}^2}{2}. \tag{11.6} \]

The functional dependence is conditional on the modeled geometry and roughness description. Dimensional analysis does not determine the function, establish full development or prove that one roughness statistic describes every surface. The force balance explains the linear length factor; measurements or a constitutive solution must supply the remaining information.

Reading resistance data without confusing regimes L22 41:11

A Moody diagram plots Darcy factor against Reynolds number, usually on logarithmic axes, with a family of turbulent curves labeled by relative roughness. The laminar line is \(64/\mathrm{Re}_D\). Within a turbulent family, larger roughness generally increases resistance at fixed Reynolds number. A horizontal high-Reynolds-number part means the factor approaches a roughness-controlled limit. It does not mean that pressure drop stops increasing with speed: equation (11.6) still multiplies that factor by \(V_{\text{avg}}^2\).

Nikuradse distinguishes three roughness-influence ranges on printed pp. 10–11 (PDF pp. 12–13): a low-roughness-effect range, a range in which roughness changes the resistance, and a high-Reynolds-number range in which the factor depends on relative roughness but becomes independent of Reynolds number. His “transition range” for roughness influence is not identical to the initial laminar-to-turbulent transition. A turbulent flow can remain sensitive to viscosity while its friction factor is still descending.

For the initial turbulent branch of a smooth pipe, Nikuradse reports the Blasius fit \(f_D\approx0.316\mathrm{Re}_D^{-1/4}\) up to about \(10^5\) (printed pp. 10–11). In the lecture's slightly different rounded coefficient this is \(f_D\approx0.3164\mathrm{Re}_D^{-1/4}\), or \(C_f\approx0.0791\mathrm{Re}_D^{-1/4}\). The negative exponent is essential. At fixed diameter, it gives \(\Delta p\propto V_{\text{avg}}^{7/4}\); resistance still rises even though the factor decreases. At \(\mathrm{Re}_D=10^5\) the lecture-rounded Darcy value is 0.01779. Applying that smooth-wall value to a substantially rough pipe would omit a physical source of drag.

Figure 11.2 shows a calculated friction family, using the empirical model developed next. The curves are computed correlations, not digitized measurements or a reproduction of Moody's original chart. Keeping that distinction visible matters: a numerical curve can reproduce a chosen equation perfectly without verifying that the equation fits a particular aged, fouled or corroded pipe.

Head loss and pump power L23A 0:42

Head means mechanical energy per unit weight; it has units of length. At a station define mechanical head \(H=p/(\rho g)+z+\alpha V_{\text{avg}}^2/(2g)\), where \(\alpha=(A V_{\text{avg}}^3)^{-1}\int_A V_s^3\,\mathrm dA\) is the kinetic-energy correction for an axial profile. It equals 1 for a uniform profile and 2 for the laminar parabola. For a turbulent mean profile, using \(\alpha\approx1\) is an engineering approximation that also neglects fluctuation contributions to kinetic-energy flux. Equal fully developed end profiles cancel their kinetic terms whatever common correction is used.

For steady incompressible flow between two sections, with negligible heat-induced property change and no additional stored mechanical energy, the engineering mechanical balance is

\[ H_1+H_p=H_2+h_L,\qquad h_L=h_f+\sum_i h_{m,i},\qquad h_f=f_D\frac{L}{D}\frac{V_{\text{avg}}^2}{2g}. \tag{11.7} \]

Here \(H_p\) is head added to the fluid by a pump, \(h_L\ge0\) total head dissipated, \(h_f\) distributed pipe loss and \(h_{m,i}\) loss in fitting \(i\). These are mechanical-energy bookkeeping quantities. Total energy remains conserved: dissipation transfers mechanical energy into internal energy. Equation (11.7) is stated here as the engineering reduction of the first law, with the loss term representing the unresolved irreversible conversion; it does not assume inviscid flow.

For a horizontal uniform run, equal kinetic and elevation terms give \(h_f=\Delta p/(\rho g)\), which with (11.6) derives the last part of (11.7). The pressure work per second is \(\Delta p\,Q\): pressure force \(pA\) times mean throughflow speed \(Q/A\) supplies \(pQ\) at each end. Thus fluid power dissipated is \(\rho gQh_f\). More generally a pump adding head \(H_p\) delivers \(P_{\text{fluid}}=\rho gQH_p\). If efficiency \(\eta=P_{\text{fluid}}/P_{\text{in}}\) is specified, input power is

\[ P_{\text{in}}=\frac{\rho gQH_p}{\eta}. \tag{11.8} \]

At \(Q=0.005\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\), every extra metre of head requires 48.88 W of fluid power for this water, or 69.83 W of input at \(\eta=0.70\). This converts a hydraulic loss directly into a cost in power. It does not select a real pump, whose head–flow curve, efficiency map and suction conditions must also be checked.

A turbulent correlation and a controlled numerical solve L23A 3:48

The Colebrook model combines Reynolds-number and relative-roughness effects implicitly:

\[ \frac1{\sqrt{f_D}}=-2\log_{10}\left( \frac{\epsilon/D}{3.7}+\frac{2.51}{\mathrm{Re}_D\sqrt{f_D}}\right). \tag{11.9} \]

Praks and Brkić's numerical-method paper, p. 2, equation (1), reproduces precisely this diameter-based Darcy form and states \(4000\lt\mathrm{Re}\lt10^8\), \(0\lt\epsilon/D\lt0.05\). That paper is a primary source for its numerical-method investigation; its historical description of Colebrook and White is secondary. The original 1939 correlation paper has not been consulted here. Equation (11.9) is an empirical closure, not an exact solution of the turbulent velocity field. Setting \(\epsilon=0\) below uses its mathematical smooth-wall limit, beyond the strictly positive roughness interval quoted on that page.

The fully rough limit sets the Reynolds-number term to zero at fixed positive relative roughness. It gives \(f_{D,\infty}=[-2\log_{10}((\epsilon/D)/3.7)]^{-2}\). For \(\epsilon/D=0.003\), this is 0.0261649. At finite Reynolds number the second term increases the logarithm's argument and gives a larger factor. Being turbulent is therefore insufficient to justify the fully rough limit. A useful diagnostic is the ratio of the Reynolds term to the roughness term in (11.9); only when that ratio is small does the simplification have a numerical basis.

For solving (11.9), set \(x=1/\sqrt{f_D}\) and \(a=(\epsilon/D)/3.7\). Its residual is \(F(x)=x+2\log_{10}(a+2.51x/\mathrm{Re}_D)\). For positive \(x\),

\[ F'(x)=1+\frac{2}{\ln10}\frac{2.51/\mathrm{Re}_D}{a+2.51x/\mathrm{Re}_D}\gt0. \tag{11.10} \]

Monotonicity makes a sign-changing bracket contain exactly one root. The code checks \(F(1)\lt0\lt F(20)\), bisects this bracket, stops when its width is below \(10^{-11}\), and independently requires \(|F(x)|\lt2\times10^{-11}\). A maximum of 100 iterations prevents an endless loop. For the initial width 19, 41 bisection iterations reduce the width below \(10^{-11}\); extra precision controls arithmetic error in the model, not empirical uncertainty. Invalid regime inputs and a failed bracket raise errors.

The lecture also gives \(f_D\approx0.184\mathrm{Re}_D^{-0.2}\) for smooth pipes. At \(10^5\) this gives 0.0184, about 3.4% above the lecture-rounded Blasius value. The recording does not state its calibration range or scatter, so it is retained as a lecture approximation rather than used for the running rough-pipe case. A smooth-pipe fit is not a substitute for roughness information.

Localized losses, elbows and valves L23A 7:31

A bend changes the flow direction; a valve restricts or redirects a passage; a branch divides the flow. The resulting three-dimensional motion cannot generally be represented by the straight fully developed pipe profile. Define a fitting loss coefficient \(K_i\) by \(h_{m,i}=K_iV_i^2/(2g)\), where \(V_i\) is the explicitly specified bulk reference velocity. This \(K_i\) is dimensionless and is unrelated to the signed laminar pressure gradient \(K_p\). The customary name “minor loss” identifies a component type, not a bound on its magnitude.

The lectures approximate \(K_i\) as dependent only on geometry at sufficiently high Reynolds number. Real values also depend on Reynolds number, opening, manufacturing geometry and interactions with upstream components. Table 11.1 retains the lecture's indicative values. Its rows are useful for learning an energy budget; a design calculation needs data for the actual fitting and its operating state.

A gate valve inserts a plate or wedge into the flowing passage. Lowering it reduces the free area and creates a jet, separation and mixing downstream. Its coefficient can rise from 0.3 to 5 even though the pipe's nominal diameter is unchanged. A globe valve forces flow through an internal seat and a turning passage; its spherical-looking housing alone does not explain its larger resistance. The fully open example coefficient 10 already exceeds eleven 90° elbows of coefficient 0.9 each. Counting components without their coefficients would hide the dominant loss.

At a common speed the comparison between fittings and the straight pipe is \(\sum_iK_i\) against \(f_DL/D\). Equivalently a fitting has a comparison length \(L_{\text{eq}}=K_iD/f_D\). This length is conditional on the operating friction factor, so it is not a fixed material property. In the running case a fully open globe valve is equivalent to about 18.4 m of straight pipe when \(f_D\approx0.0271\). On a 2 m laboratory connection it can dominate the total resistance; on a 200 m run its fraction is smaller.

If diameters differ, the individual speeds differ by continuity, \(V_i=Q/A_i\). One may sum \(K_i\) directly only after converting them to one velocity reference. For example a coefficient referenced to a wide-section speed \(V_w\) becomes \(K_{\text{ref}}=K_w(V_w/V_{\text{ref}})^2\) when moved into a balance using \(V_{\text{ref}}\). Losing this square is a consequential error in a network with contractions.

Contraction, expansion and pressure recovery L23A 22:36

Let \(A_s\) be a smaller pipe area, \(A_b\) a larger area, and \(V_s=Q/A_s\), \(V_b=Q/A_b\) the corresponding bulk speeds. A sudden expansion sends a relatively fast jet into a larger passage. Flow separates at the step and mixes before becoming approximately uniform downstream. A sudden contraction can first narrow the jet more than the final pipe opening and then mix it back out. Rounded entry geometry reduces this contraction and separation. A protruding reentrant entrance instead produces a stronger separated jet.

Recirculation alone is not a thermodynamic explanation of energy loss: an ideal inviscid vortex can persist without dissipation. In the actual fitting, velocity gradients and turbulent transport move mechanical energy into small scales, where molecular viscosity converts it into internal energy. Real recirculation zones also exchange fluid with the throughflow. A drawn closed streamline should not be interpreted as proof that a named fluid particle remains trapped forever in a turbulent fitting.

For a short horizontal sudden expansion, assume uniform section velocities, negligible wall shear over the short mixing region, and pressure on the annular step approximately equal to upstream pressure \(p_s\). The downstream momentum balance gives \((p_s-p_b)A_b=\rho Q(V_b-V_s)\), or \(p_b-p_s=\rho V_b(V_s-V_b)\). Mechanical-energy balance then gives

\[ gh_m=\frac{p_s-p_b}{\rho}+\frac{V_s^2-V_b^2}{2} =\frac{(V_s-V_b)^2}{2},\qquad K_{\text{exp}}=\left(1-\frac{A_s}{A_b}\right)^2, \tag{11.11} \]

with \(K_{\text{exp}}\) referenced to the smaller-section speed \(V_s\). This derives the lecture's approximate expansion formula under a specific mixing model. It also explains how pressure can recover while mechanical head falls: deceleration raises static pressure, but only part of the lost kinetic energy is recovered. For \(A_s/A_b=0.2\), \(K_{\text{exp}}=0.64\). If \(V_s=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), the head loss is 0.1305 m while the pressure rises by \(0.64\rho\), about 638 Pa.

As \(A_s/A_b\to1\), the model loss tends to zero because the step disappears. As \(A_s/A_b\to0\), \(K_{\text{exp}}\to1\): the pipe's kinetic head is dissipated in a large receiving reservoir. Merely rounding the end of a discharge into a large tank does not recover that jet energy. A gradual diffuser is a different geometry and can recover more pressure if it avoids excessive separation. At the other end, rounding a reservoir entrance can lower its coefficient from the lecture's sharp-entry estimate 0.42 to about 0.05. The numerical difference is empirical, while the distinction between pressure recovery and head loss follows from the balance.

The two-reservoir calculation L23B 0:13

Apply (11.7) between the two free surfaces of the running case. Each surface is exposed to the same atmospheric pressure. Each reservoir is much wider than the pipe, so surface velocities are negligible. The instantaneous water-level change is slow enough for a quasi-steady approximation: a sequence of steady calculations at changing levels. There is no pump. Pressure and kinetic terms cancel or vanish, leaving \(h_L=z_1-z_2=35\ \mathrm m\). The six losses are a well-rounded entrance, a half-open gate valve, two 90° elbows, a fully open globe valve and the exit into the lower reservoir.

All six coefficients use the same 50 mm pipe speed, so their sum is \(\sum K_i=0.05+5+0.9+0.9+10+1=17.85\). The flow is the positive solution of

\[ 35=\left[17.85+f_D\!\left(\frac{V_{\text{avg}}D}{\nu},0.003\right) \frac{200}{0.05}\right]\frac{V_{\text{avg}}^2}{2g}. \tag{11.12} \]

There are two dependencies to close: speed determines Reynolds number; Reynolds number and roughness determine friction factor. The lecture reads \(f_D\approx0.027\) from the chart, obtaining \(V_{\text{avg}}=2.3359\ \mathrm{m/s}\), then \(Q=4.5866\ \mathrm{L/s}\). That is an adequate graphical estimate. With the explicitly stated 300 K properties, its Reynolds number is about \(1.36\times10^5\); the recording's approximately \(1.2\times10^5\) corresponds to a different or rounded viscosity assumption. The state must accompany the number.

Solving (11.12) together with (11.9) gives \(V_{\text{avg}}=2.330632\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(Q=4.576185\ \mathrm{L/s}\), \(\mathrm{Re}_D=136032\) and \(f_D=0.0271428\). Distributed loss is 30.0582 m and fitting loss is 4.9418 m. Their sum is 35 m. The precision displays the computational closure, not an engineering uncertainty of one part in a million.

The fully rough limit 0.0261649 is about 3.6% below this finite-Reynolds-number factor. In the Colebrook logarithm the Reynolds term is about 13.8% of the roughness term, so discarding it is an approximation with a measurable effect, even though the flow is turbulent. The fixed chart value 0.027 happens to give speed within about 0.23% of the iterative model. Agreement here does not establish Reynolds-number independence.

The long pipe contributes about 85.9% of the available head. The fitting total is smaller but still consequential: removing it would change the computed flow. The globe valve alone contributes about 2.77 m at the solved speed. Conversely the rounded entrance contributes only about 0.014 m. This ranking identifies where a resistance reduction is likely to matter before any detailed optimization.

What the ideal comparison actually assumes L23B 20:04

It is tempting to set \(h_L=0\) between the two free surfaces and solve for a higher speed. With equal atmospheric pressures and negligible surface velocities, however, that equation would assert \(40=5\). It has no steady solution. The dissipative receiving reservoir is part of the original physical system; deleting its loss while keeping both resting free surfaces changes the problem inconsistently.

The lecture obtains a useful ideal comparison by choosing the downstream station just at the pipe discharge, before the jet mixes in the lower reservoir. Assume steady, inviscid, incompressible flow along a streamline, no shaft work and negligible upstream surface speed. Bernoulli's relation then applies along that streamline. Approximate discharge pressure by the receiving reservoir's local hydrostatic pressure. If the outlet is depth \(h_b\) below its surface, then \(p_b=p_{\text{atm}}+\rho gh_b\) and \(z_b=5-h_b\), so \(p_b/(\rho g)+z_b=p_{\text{atm}}/(\rho g)+5\). The depth cancels.

The resulting ideal outlet speed is \(V_b=\sqrt{2g(40-5)}=26.205\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The corresponding 50 mm jet flow is about 51.45 L/s, roughly 11.24 times the resistive prediction. This is an ideal jet-energy scale under the stated outlet-pressure approximation. It is not a lossless reservoir-to-reservoir steady flow, and it should not be interpreted as a measured upper-capacity guarantee for the actual valves, pressure field or pipe.

Diameter and pump input for the reverse lift

Now require \(Q=5.00\ \mathrm{L/s}\) from the lower elevation to the upper one. Assume the same modeled 200 m length, 0.15 mm equivalent roughness, total fitting coefficient 17.85 and water state. Between the large free surfaces the pump must supply both 35 m of elevation and the positive resistance loss: \(H_p=35+h_L\). Reversing the sign of friction because the physical pipe points uphill would violate the dissipative energy balance.

For each candidate diameter, continuity fixes speed before the friction solve: \(V_{\text{avg}}=4Q/(\pi D^2)\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_D=4Q/(\pi\nu D)\). Substitute into (11.7) to obtain

\[ h_f=\frac{8f_DLQ^2}{g\pi^2D^5},\qquad \sum_i h_{m,i}=\frac{8(\sum_i K_i)Q^2}{g\pi^2D^4}. \tag{11.13} \]

The different diameter powers explain why replacing a long narrow pipe can outperform changing a small entrance coefficient. They do not say that turbulent friction scales exactly as \(D^{-5}\), because \(f_D\) changes through both Reynolds number and relative roughness. The approximation holds the fitting coefficients fixed across the comparison; actual fittings must be selected and checked for each pipe size.

Executable resistance and energy check

The following standard-library Python program implements (11.9), (11.12) and the diameter comparison in SI units. Its fixed inputs are the stated water properties, geometry, roughness, fitting sum, head difference and assumed efficiency. It reads no files, performs no network access and prints only the results and check status. The turbulent function accepts \(4000\lt\mathrm{Re}_D\lt10^8\), \(0\le\epsilon/D\lt0.05\); zero roughness is explicitly the model limit described above. A nonpositive flow or geometry, negative loss input, invalid regime or missing sign-changing bracket raises an exception.

The inner bisection solves friction in \(x\); the outer bisection solves positive speed in the gravity case. For the outer solve, the lower speed corresponds to Reynolds number 4001 and the upper speed is 100 m/s. Both endpoints fall in the accepted turbulent domain for this geometry. The residual is loss minus the available 35 m; its endpoint signs are checked.

To establish uniqueness, keep diameter, water properties and relative roughness fixed. Then \(\mathrm{Re}_D\) is proportional to \(V_{\text{avg}}\), and \(a=(\epsilon/D)/3.7\) is constant. Define the positive dimensionless quantity \(\beta=[2/\ln 10](2.51/\mathrm{Re}_D)/(a+2.51x/\mathrm{Re}_D)\). Implicit differentiation of \(x+2\log_{10}(a+2.51x/\mathrm{Re}_D)=0\) gives

\[ (1+\beta)\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\mathrm{Re}_D} =\frac{\beta x}{\mathrm{Re}_D},\qquad \frac{\mathrm d\ln x}{\mathrm d\ln\mathrm{Re}_D} =\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\lt1. \]

The distributed loss is proportional to \(f_DV_{\text{avg}}^2=V_{\text{avg}}^2/x^2\). Its logarithmic derivative with respect to speed is therefore \(2-2\beta/(1+\beta)=2/(1+\beta)\gt0\). The fitting loss, proportional to \(V_{\text{avg}}^2\) with fixed nonnegative coefficients, also increases for the running case. Their sum is continuous and strictly increasing within the selected model domain. The sign-changing bracket therefore contains exactly one model operating point.

import math

RHO, MU, G = 996.556, 8.537e-4, 9.81
NU = MU / RHO


def bisect(fun, lo, hi, tol=1e-11):
    """Increasing continuous residual; sign-changing bracket required."""
    if not fun(lo) < 0 < fun(hi):
        raise ValueError("root not bracketed")
    for _ in range(100):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if fun(mid) > 0:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo = mid
        if hi - lo < tol:
            return (lo + hi) / 2
    raise RuntimeError("bisection did not converge")


def darcy(re, rough):
    """Colebrook model; rough is epsilon/D, not epsilon in metres."""
    if not (4000 < re < 1e8 and 0 <= rough < 0.05):
        raise ValueError("outside turbulent model domain")

    def residual(x):
        return x + 2 * math.log10(rough / 3.7 + 2.51 * x / re)
    x = bisect(residual, 1.0, 20.0)
    assert abs(residual(x)) < 2e-11
    return 1 / x**2


def losses(speed, diameter, length, epsilon, ksum):
    if min(speed, diameter, length) <= 0 or min(epsilon, ksum) < 0:
        raise ValueError("positive geometry/flow and nonnegative losses")
    re = speed * diameter / NU
    factor = darcy(re, epsilon / diameter)
    pipe = factor * length / diameter * speed**2 / (2 * G)
    fitting = ksum * speed**2 / (2 * G)
    return re, factor, pipe, fitting


D, L, EPS, KS = 0.05, 200.0, 0.00015, 17.85


def deficit(v):
    return sum(losses(v, D, L, EPS, KS)[2:]) - 35


speed = bisect(deficit, 4001 * NU / D, 100.0)
re, factor, pipe, fitting = losses(speed, D, L, EPS, KS)
flow = math.pi * D**2 / 4 * speed
print(f"gravity: V={speed:.6f} m/s Q={1000*flow:.6f} L/s")
print(f"Re={re:.1f} f_D={factor:.7f} C_f={factor/4:.7f}")
print(f"heads: pipe={pipe:.6f} m fittings={fitting:.6f} m")
print(f"energy residual={abs(deficit(speed)):.2e} m")
v_chart = math.sqrt(2 * G * 35 / (KS + 0.027 * L / D))
f_inf = (-2 * math.log10((EPS / D) / 3.7))**-2
print(f"chart V={v_chart:.6f} m/s rough-limit f_D={f_inf:.7f}")
for d in (0.04, 0.05, 0.06):
    v = 0.005 / (math.pi * d**2 / 4)
    r, f, hp, hm = losses(v, d, L, EPS, KS)
    head = 35 + hp + hm
    power = RHO * G * 0.005 * head / 0.70
    print(f"lift: D={1000*d:.0f} mm Re={r:.0f} f_D={f:.6f} "
          f"H_p={head:.3f} m P_in={power/1000:.3f} kW")
print(f"entrance at Re2300: laminar={0.0575*2300:.3f} D "
      f"turbulent-form={4.4*2300**0.167:.3f} D")
bad = 0
for args in ((2300, 0.003), (1e5, -0.003), (1e5, 0.06)):
    try:
        darcy(*args)
    except ValueError:
        bad += 1
try:
    bisect(lambda x: x*x + 1, 1, 2)
except ValueError:
    bad += 1
assert bad == 4
tau = factor * RHO * speed**2 / 8
dp = RHO * G * pipe
area = math.pi * D**2 / 4
assert abs(dp * area - tau * math.pi * D * L) < 1e-7

The energy residual checks numerical closure of the coupled correlation and balance. The force check compares \(\Delta p A\) with \(\tau_w\pi DL\) using the pipe-loss head. The program deliberately evaluates the wrong wall-force sign and confirms a large nonzero imbalance, rather than assuming that two agreeing formulas would detect it. It also exercises four failures: a transitional Reynolds number, negative relative roughness, roughness outside the accepted interval and a residual with no root in its bracket. These are observed tests of arithmetic and the input contract. They do not validate the resistance correlation or the fitting coefficients against an installed system.

What the calculation captures and misses

The model combines conservation with empirical closure. The pressure-to-wall-force relation follows from momentum balance with matching end profiles; the power relation follows from pressure work and the definition of head. The laminar law follows from the Newtonian, no-slip, fully developed solution. The turbulent friction and fitting coefficients are empirical inputs with narrower claims. A tiny equation residual establishes only that these chosen pieces fit together numerically.

Several physical checks remain outside this one-dimensional model. Pressure must stay above the vapor-pressure margin required to avoid cavitation; total head alone does not establish suction safety. Water hammer during valve operation requires an unsteady compressible model. Strong heating changes viscosity; flexible or vibrating walls alter the geometry. Non-Newtonian fluids require another constitutive relation. Corrosion, deposits and biological growth can change both effective diameter and roughness, so an initial material label is not a perpetual resistance measurement.

Near transition, in short developing tubes, at interacting fittings and in strongly nonuniform passages, a single fully developed friction factor and independent constant loss coefficients may not suffice. A branched network adds continuity at junctions and an energy balance around each connection; it is not represented by summing coefficients along an arbitrarily chosen path. The two-reservoir example has one series flow rate, which is why its scalar residual closes the problem.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive and diagnose a convention). A horizontal fully developed tube has \(D=0.020\ \mathrm m\), \(L=10\ \mathrm m\), bulk speed 1 m/s and density \(1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\). Its measured pressure drop is 7500 Pa. Find wall resistance, \(C_f\), Darcy factor and dissipated fluid power. A calculation uses \(C_f\) in the Darcy head-loss formula. Quantify its error.

Solution

The pressure force is balanced by wall resistance, so \(\tau_w=\Delta pD/(4L)=7500(0.02)/(40)=3.75\ \mathrm{Pa}\). Dynamic pressure is 500 Pa; hence \(C_f=3.75/500=0.0075\) and \(f_D=4C_f=0.030\). The flow rate is \(Q=\pi(0.02)^2/4=3.1416\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}\), so \(P=\Delta pQ=2.356\ \mathrm W\). Substituting \(C_f\) for \(f_D\) predicts 1875 Pa and 0.589 W, one quarter of the measured resistance and power. The wall stress must oppose flow on the fluid control volume; using it downstream would make pressure and wall forces reinforce instead of balance.

Exercise 2 (check an assumption). Water with the chapter's properties flows at \(\mathrm{Re}_D=1000\) through a 1 mm tube only 20 mm long. Estimate entrance length with (11.2). Can a fully developed pressure gradient be applied over the entire tube? What happens to that estimate at Reynolds number tending to zero?

Solution

Equation (11.2) gives \(L_e/D=57.5\), or \(L_e=57.5\ \mathrm{mm}\), almost three times the actual length. The inlet occupies the entire tube under this criterion. Bulk velocity is \(\mathrm{Re}_D\nu/D=0.8567\ \mathrm{m/s}\); low Reynolds number does not remove the inlet region. Applying the fully developed laminar gradient over 20 mm neglects a change of profile and the associated momentum and energy corrections, so it cannot be asserted as the total pressure loss. The linear estimate tends to zero as Reynolds number tends to zero, while the computational no-slip fit (11.3) tends to 0.6044 diameters. Axial diffusion invalidates the simple transverse-diffusion travel argument in that limit.

Exercise 3 (counterexample and energy). A horizontal sudden expansion has \(A_s/A_b=0.2\), upstream speed 2 m/s and the chapter's water density. A student calls any pressure increase a negative loss. Compute pressure change and head loss under the assumptions of (11.11), and explain the error.

Solution

Continuity gives \(V_b=0.4\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Momentum gives \(p_b-p_s=\rho(0.4)(2-0.4)=637.8\ \mathrm{Pa}\), a positive static-pressure recovery. The lost kinetic energy per unit mass is \((2^2-0.4^2)/2=1.92\ \mathrm{J/kg}\). Only \((p_b-p_s)/\rho=0.64\ \mathrm{J/kg}\) becomes pressure energy. The remaining 1.28 J/kg is dissipated, giving \(h_m=1.28/9.81=0.13048\ \mathrm m\), also \(0.64(2^2)/(2g)\). The student's criterion omits kinetic energy. Head, rather than static pressure alone, measures the mechanical-energy loss in this horizontal example.

Exercise 4 (compute and bound a design approximation). For the gravity running case compare the iterative Darcy factor with its fully rough limit. Does \(\mathrm{Re}_D\gt10^5\) by itself justify deleting the Reynolds term? Explain how the error affects the inferred gravity flow rate.

Solution

The iterative values are \(f_D=0.0271428\), \(\mathrm{Re}_D=136032\). The rough limit is \([-2\log_{10}(0.003/3.7)]^{-2}=0.0261649\), about 3.60% lower. With \(x=1/\sqrt{f_D}\approx6.07\), the Reynolds term is \(2.51x/\mathrm{Re}_D\approx1.12\times10^{-4}\), while the roughness term is \(0.003/3.7\approx8.11\times10^{-4}\); their ratio is about 0.138. This ratio, rather than the name “turbulent,” determines the size of the omitted term. Using the lower limiting factor underestimates resistance and overestimates the gravity-driven speed. The fitting term moderates the effect because it stays in the denominator of the speed estimate. The numerical residual of the full equation cannot bound the physical correlation error.

Exercise 5 (design judgment). At 5 L/s in the 50 mm reverse-lift case, replace the fully open globe valve of coefficient 10 with a fully open gate valve of coefficient 0.3. Keep all other inputs and pump efficiency fixed. Estimate power saved and compare it with increasing the pipe to 60 mm.

Solution

The speed remains \(V_{\text{avg}}=4(0.005)/(\pi0.05^2)=2.5465\ \mathrm{m/s}\), because flow rate and diameter are fixed. The coefficient decreases by 9.7, so head decreases by \(9.7V_{\text{avg}}^2/(2g)=3.206\ \mathrm m\). The fluid power saving is \(\rho gQ(3.206)=156.7\ \mathrm W\), and input saving at 70% efficiency is about 223.9 W. Increasing diameter from 50 to 60 mm saves \(5.354-3.610=1.744\ \mathrm{kW}\) under Table 11.2's fixed-coefficient comparison, substantially more at this long-run operating point. This is a hydraulic comparison only; the two valves may have different control duties, and replacing one is not justified by the coefficient alone.

Exercise 6 (numerical method and model boundary). Explain why bisection of (11.9) in \(x=1/\sqrt{f_D}\) has at most one root. What do the bracket and residual checks establish? Would a converged value at \(\mathrm{Re}_D=2300\) establish a valid flow prediction? Explain the inconsistency in setting all losses to zero between the two resting reservoir surfaces.

Solution

For positive \(x\) and nonnegative roughness, the logarithm's argument is positive. Equation (11.10) gives \(F'(x)\gt0\), so the residual is strictly increasing and has at most one zero. Opposite endpoint signs establish existence by continuity; halving the interval gives a bound on numerical root error, while the final residual tests substitution into the equation actually coded. Neither check tests empirical validity. The implemented function rejects Reynolds number 2300 because it lies outside its selected turbulent domain; forcing convergence there would solve an extrapolated equation. Finally, resting atmospheric free surfaces have mechanical heads differing by 35 m. With no pump and no losses the steady balance would require their equality. An ideal 26.2 m/s jet comparison moves the downstream station to the un-mixed discharge, where kinetic head remains, and therefore describes a different boundary condition.
