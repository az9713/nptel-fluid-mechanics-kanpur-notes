Inviscid flow: velocity potential and the potential-flow equations

A two-dimensional flow enters the top of a rectangular region 2 m long and 1 m high at 0.50 m/s, turns toward the right, and leaves through its right edge. The lower and left edges are impermeable walls. Can one scalar function produce a velocity field that conserves mass, has no local rotation, and respects both walls? The field constructed here carries 1.00 m²/s per unit depth and predicts a pressure decrease of 375 Pa between two specified points for a model density of 1000 kg/m³. A second test is more demanding: fluid circulates around an excluded circular core. Its vorticity is zero throughout the fluid, yet no globally single-valued velocity potential exists. Both tests are necessary to understand what the potential-flow equations actually assert.

Grounded in Lecture 26 and Lecture 27 of NPTEL Fluid Mechanics, Prof. Sanjay Mittal, IIT Kanpur, using the course recording labels. Lecture material: the inviscid Euler equations; local rotation and examples with circular and straight streamlines; circulation, Stokes' theorem, and Kelvin's circulation theorem; path independence and velocity potential; the two-dimensional stream function; Laplace equations, superposition, solid-surface and far-field conditions; orthogonal streamlines and equipotentials; and pressure recovery from Euler's equations. Research extensions: the role of independent boundary circulations in ideal flow with holes, as formulated by Iftimie, Lopes Filho, Nussenzveig Lopes, and Sueur (2013 preprint). Original teaching constructions: the rectangular turning flow, the annular counterexample, explicit hypotheses and proofs, the boundary compatibility and uniqueness arguments, the local complex-variable convention, computed figures, executable checks, and six exercises. The constructions test the equations; they are not measured flows.

A fluid domain, a velocity field, and boundary data

The formal model consists of a fluid region \(D\), a velocity field \(\mathbf V(\mathbf x,t)\), a pressure field \(p(\mathbf x,t)\), and boundary data. Here \(\mathbf x=(x,y,z)\) is position in metres, \(t\) is time in seconds, \(\mathbf V=(V_x,V_y,V_z)\) has units m/s, and \(p\) has units Pa. The unit vectors along the Cartesian axes are \(\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\). Density \(\rho\), in kg/m³, is constant in space and time. The field is differentiable wherever an equation is applied. A singular point, at which a field or its derivatives fail to exist, is excluded from the regular fluid domain.

The running velocity field is steady and planar: \(V_z=0\), and \(V_x,V_y\) do not depend on \(z\). The coordinates \(x,y\) lie in a horizontal plane; gravity is balanced by the separate hydrostatic pressure variation in \(z\). The planar region is \(0\lt x\lt L\), \(0\lt y\lt H\), with \(L=2\ \mathrm m\) and \(H=1\ \mathrm m\). The boundaries at \(x=0\) and \(y=0\) are fixed impermeable walls. The top is an open inflow and the right edge is an open outflow, not two more walls. All flow rates in this planar model are per unit depth in \(z\), hence in m²/s.

A strain rate \(a=0.50\ \mathrm s^{-1}\) sets the candidate field

\[ \mathbf V=(ax,-ay,0). \tag{13.1} \]

It stretches fluid in \(x\) and compresses it in \(y\). At the top \(V_y=-aH=-0.50\ \mathrm{m/s}\); at the right \(V_x=aL=1.00\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Those two speeds act across edges of different lengths. Equal speeds are unnecessary for equal flow rates. The density 1000 kg/m³ used for pressure arithmetic is a chosen model parameter, not a water-property measurement. Each added assumption below will be tested against this same field.

What removing viscosity does to the equations L26 1:17

An inviscid model neglects viscous stress, so the total Cauchy stress is \(\mathbf T=-p\mathbf I\), with \(\mathbf I\) the identity tensor. The stress convention is the face-first convention of Chapter 2. In a Newtonian viscous model, dynamic viscosity \(\mu\), in Pa s, multiplies the rate of deformation. Setting that contribution to zero changes a constitutive assumption about force. It does not prescribe the velocity gradients.

For constant density, conservation of mass and Newton's second law give the incompressible Euler equations:

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf V=0,\qquad \frac{\partial\mathbf V}{\partial t}+(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V =-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\mathbf f. \tag{13.2} \]

The vector \(\mathbf f\) is body force per unit mass, in m/s². The gradient \(\nabla p\) points toward increasing pressure; its negative produces the pressure acceleration. The divergence \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf V\) is the sum of the three same-coordinate velocity derivatives. Its vanishing expresses zero local volume expansion. The convective term \((\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V\) is the acceleration caused by motion through a spatially varying field.

The Euler momentum equation contains first spatial derivatives of velocity. The constant-viscosity Navier–Stokes equation contains the additional second-derivative term \((\mu/\rho)\nabla^2\mathbf V\). Removing it lowers the differential order but leaves the product of velocity and its derivative. Euler's equations are still nonlinear. Adding two velocity solutions need not give another solution with the sum of their pressures.

In a real fluid, an inviscid description can approximate a region in which viscous forces are small relative to inertia. That approximation need not hold near a solid wall. For characteristic speed \(U\), length \(\ell\), and nonzero \(\mu\), the ratio of inertial to viscous force scales is the Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho U\ell/\mu\). Large \(\mathrm{Re}\) based on an outer length does not make gradients across a thin wall layer small. The inviscid model concerns a chosen region and force balance; it does not turn a real material's viscosity off everywhere.

Local rotation is different from streamline curvature L26 6:08

The vorticity is \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf V\), in s⁻¹. A flow is irrotational in \(D\) if \(\boldsymbol\omega=\mathbf0\) at every point of \(D\). This is a kinematic condition. It can be checked without knowing \(\mu\) or \(p\). For clarity, the velocity gradient has component-row, differentiation-column entries \(G_{ij}=\partial V_i/\partial x_j\). Its symmetric and skew-symmetric parts are \(\mathbf E=(\mathbf G+\mathbf G^{\mathsf T})/2\) and \(\mathbf W=(\mathbf G-\mathbf G^{\mathsf T})/2\), respectively. Then \(\mathbf G=\mathbf E+\mathbf W\). The symmetric part describes deformation. The skew part satisfies \(\mathbf W\mathbf b=(\boldsymbol\omega/2)\times\mathbf b\) for every vector \(\mathbf b\); direct multiplication verifies the identity. The local rotation rate is therefore \(\boldsymbol\Omega=\boldsymbol\omega/2\), not \(\boldsymbol\omega\).

In planar flow the only vorticity component is

\[ \omega_z=\frac{\partial V_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial V_x}{\partial y}, \qquad \Omega_z=\frac12\omega_z. \tag{13.3} \]

One way to interpret the factor one-half is to follow two initially perpendicular material lines. A line along \(x\) turns counterclockwise at rate \(\partial V_y/\partial x\); a line along \(y\) turns counterclockwise at rate \(-\partial V_x/\partial y\). Their mean is (13.3). The lines can also change their angle with each other, through \(\mathbf E\). Zero local rotation does not mean that every marked material line keeps its original direction.

Three examples separate streamline shape from local rotation. A streamline at a fixed time is a curve tangent to the velocity wherever the velocity is nonzero. In polar coordinates \(r,\theta\), with \(\theta\) increasing counterclockwise, take a purely tangential field \(V_r=0\), \(V_\theta=F(r)\). Its streamlines are circles. The vorticity follows by applying circulation to a small annular sector, or by the polar curl formula:

\[ \omega_z=\frac1r\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dr}(rF). \quad \begin{cases} F=\Omega_0 r &: \omega_z=2\Omega_0,\\ F=c/r &: \omega_z=0\quad(r\gt0). \end{cases} \tag{13.4} \]

Here \(\Omega_0\) is a constant angular speed in s⁻¹, and \(c\) is a constant in m²/s. The first field is rigid rotation, often called a forced vortex; the second is the free-vortex velocity field. They have identical circular streamline geometry and different local rotation. For \(c=1\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), speeds at radii 1 and 2 m are 1 and 0.5 m/s, yet the vorticity is zero at both radii. The origin is not part of this field's regular domain. The name free vortex must not conceal that exclusion.

Straight streamlines do not imply irrotationality either. Between plates at \(y=\pm h\), with upper-plate speed \(U\), the steady Couette field is \(V_x=U(1+y/h)/2\), \(V_y=0\). It has \(\omega_z=-U/(2h)\) and \(\Omega_z=-U/(4h)\): clockwise rotation even though every streamline is straight. With \(U=1\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(h=0.50\ \mathrm m\), these are −1 and −0.50 s⁻¹. The half-gap symbol \(h\) translates the board's \(b\), following Chapter 7.

The distinction also separates viscosity from vorticity. A spatially uniform velocity has zero vorticity even in a fluid with nonzero viscosity. Conversely, rigid rotation is a steady Euler solution with a suitable radial pressure gradient and nonzero vorticity. Neither “viscous” nor “inviscid” decides irrotationality. The running field (13.1) has \(\mathbf G=\operatorname{diag}(a,-a,0)\), so \(\mathbf W=0\) and \(\mathbf E\ne0\). It deforms fluid without local spin.

Circulation and the surface required by Stokes' theorem L26 31:09

For an oriented closed curve \(C\), define circulation by

\[ \Gamma_C=\oint_C\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf s. \tag{13.5} \]

The line element \(\mathrm d\mathbf s\) points along the traversal. Circulation has units m²/s. For planar curves the positive orientation is counterclockwise as seen from \(+z\). Reversing the traversal reverses \(\Gamma_C\). Circulation is an integral of tangential velocity; it is not angular momentum and does not contain density or a moment arm.

If \(C\) bounds an oriented surface \(S\) on which the velocity is continuously differentiable, Stokes' theorem gives

\[ \Gamma_C=\iint_S\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA. \tag{13.6} \]

Here \(\mathbf n\) is the unit normal consistent with the traversal by the right-hand rule, and \(\mathrm dA\) is area. The entire surface must lie in a region where the velocity and its derivatives are defined. For a planar counterclockwise curve with a regular interior, (13.6) is \(\Gamma_C=\iint_S\omega_z\,\mathrm dA\). A region with positive and negative vorticity can have zero total circulation. Checking one large loop cannot establish irrotationality. Checking all sufficiently small loops around every point can: dividing their circulation by their area and shrinking them recovers the local vorticity component.

For rigid rotation, a counterclockwise circle of radius \(r\) gives \(\Gamma_C=2\pi r(\Omega_0r)=2\Omega_0\pi r^2\), matching vorticity times disk area. For the free-vortex field, the same line integral is \(\Gamma_C=2\pi c\), independent of radius. It is nonzero even though \(\omega_z=0\) at every fluid point. There is no contradiction: a disk spanning that circle crosses the excluded origin. On an annulus, the boundary of an annular surface includes both circles with opposite orientations; their circulations cancel, exactly as (13.6) requires.

Thus an irrotational field has zero circulation around every loop that bounds an admissible surface within the fluid, but may have nonzero circulation around a hole. In ideal-flow analysis this is additional physical information. Iftimie and coauthors (2013 preprint), pp. 3–4 and 8, distinguish vorticity from the circulations around boundary components and use both to reconstruct velocity. Their formulation concerns domains with obstacles; it makes explicit the information that a local curl test misses.

Why initially irrotational fluid can stay irrotational L26 38:40

Kelvin's circulation theorem concerns a material loop \(C(t)\): every point of the loop moves with its fluid particle. It does not concern a fixed circle through which particles pass. Assume a smooth inviscid flow, constant density, and a conservative body force \(\mathbf f=-\nabla\Phi_b\), where \(\Phi_b\), in m²/s², is a single-valued body-force potential. Gravity has \(\Phi_b=gz\), with \(g\) the positive gravitational acceleration and \(z\) elevation. Require that the loop and its evolution stay in the regular fluid.

Parameterize the loop by a periodic material label \(\lambda\), and write its position as \(\mathbf X(\lambda,t)\). Since \(\partial_t\mathbf X=\mathbf V(\mathbf X,t)\), differentiating its circulation yields two terms:

\[ \frac{\mathrm d\Gamma_{C(t)}}{\mathrm dt} =\oint_{C(t)}\frac{\mathrm D\mathbf V}{\mathrm Dt}\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf s +\oint_{C(t)}\mathrm d\!\left(\frac{|\mathbf V|^2}{2}\right). \tag{13.7} \]

The material derivative is \(\mathrm D/\mathrm Dt=\partial_t+\mathbf V\cdot\nabla\). The second term comes from the moving line element: \(\mathbf V\cdot\partial_\lambda\mathbf V\,\mathrm d\lambda=\partial_\lambda(|\mathbf V|^2/2)\,\mathrm d\lambda\). It vanishes around a closed loop. Euler's equation makes the first term \(-\oint\mathrm d(p/\rho+\Phi_b)=0\). Hence \(\mathrm d\Gamma_{C(t)}/\mathrm dt=0\). Pressure can redistribute momentum while contributing no net circulation under these hypotheses.

If the initial vorticity is zero, every sufficiently small material loop initially has zero circulation. A smooth invertible particle motion carries those loops to small loops at later times. Their circulation remains zero, so the local vorticity remains zero. Smoothness and the ability to follow the loop are essential. This reasoning does not assert that a viscous wall layer, a shock, or an arbitrary singular weak solution preserves the same conditions. It also does not force an initially nonzero circulation around a hole to vanish: that circulation is preserved.

A direct check uses the running field. Its particle paths obey \(x(t)=x_0e^{at}\), \(y(t)=y_0e^{-at}\), so areas are preserved and a material rectangle changes aspect ratio. The velocity integral along each of its horizontal edges is the difference of \(ax^2/2\) at its endpoints; along each vertical edge it is the difference of \(-ay^2/2\). The four differences cancel at every time. This is a circulation check on a deforming loop, not an inference from its fixed shape.

For variable density, the pressure step requires more than inviscidity. A sufficient generalization is a barotropic relation \(p=p(\rho)\), so \(\mathrm dp/\rho\) has a scalar primitive. Without that condition the pressure contribution need not be an exact differential. The theorem proved here uses constant density throughout. The classical circulation conservation discussed by Iftimie and coauthors, p. 3, is explicitly distinguished there from their treatment of less regular solutions.

Constructing a velocity potential, locally and globally L26 43:42

A velocity potential is a scalar \(\phi\), in m²/s, such that \(\mathbf V=\nabla\phi\). The sign convention is positive gradient. The representation is kinematic and can exist even when viscosity is nonzero. In this chapter, potential flow means the inviscid irrotational model, with incompressibility imposed separately. Assume first that every closed velocity integral in a connected domain \(D\) vanishes. Choose a reference point \(\mathbf x_*\), and define

\[ \phi(\mathbf x,t)=\phi_*(t)+\int_{\mathbf x_*}^{\mathbf x}\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf s. \tag{13.8} \]

The integral is evaluated at fixed \(t\). Two paths from the reference point to \(\mathbf x\) form a closed loop when one is reversed. Their integrals differ by that loop's circulation, which is zero by hypothesis. Thus (13.8) is path independent. Adding a short segment \(\delta\mathbf x\) at the endpoint changes \(\phi\) by \(\mathbf V\cdot\delta\mathbf x\) to first order. It follows that \(\mathrm d\phi=\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf x\), and therefore

\[ V_x=\phi_x,\quad V_y=\phi_y,\quad V_z=\phi_z, \qquad \mathbf V=\nabla\phi. \tag{13.9} \]

A subscript on a scalar here denotes partial differentiation. Conversely, if a single-valued differentiable \(\phi\) exists, the gradient theorem makes every closed velocity integral zero. If \(\phi\) has continuous second derivatives, equality of mixed derivatives also gives \(\nabla\times\nabla\phi=0\). A potential therefore implies local irrotationality and global zero circulation, but those two conclusions contain different amounts of information.

For a continuously differentiable curl-free field, a potential exists in each sufficiently small ball inside \(D\). The ball has no hole obstructing the spanning surfaces. A connected domain is simply connected if every closed curve can be continuously contracted to a point without leaving it. On such a domain a smooth curl-free field has a globally single-valued potential. This sufficient topology condition is not necessary for a particular field: a uniform velocity has a single-valued potential even if the domain has holes. The exact field-specific condition is zero circulation around every closed loop.

The running region is simply connected. Integrating \(V_x=ax\) gives \(\phi=ax^2/2+F(y,t)\); requiring \(\phi_y=-ay\) gives \(F=-ay^2/2+f_0(t)\). Thus

\[ \phi=\frac a2(x^2-y^2)+f_0(t). \tag{13.10} \]

The arbitrary function \(f_0\) is a gauge freedom: a spatially uniform shift changes no velocity. From \((0,0)\) to \((2,1)\), (13.8) gives \(a(4-1)/2=0.75\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Integrating first horizontally and then vertically gives \(1.00-0.25=0.75\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\); reversing the order gives the same result. The check uses the field components directly.

Now exclude the disk \(r\le R_1\) and take an annulus \(R_1\lt r\lt R_2\). For \(V_\theta=c/r\), the gradient in polar coordinates requires \(\partial_r\phi=0\), \(r^{-1}\partial_\theta\phi=c/r\). Hence locally \(\phi=c\theta+f_0(t)\). After one positive circuit, its value changes by \(2\pi c=\Gamma\). It cannot be a single-valued scalar on the whole annulus when \(c\ne0\). A radial cut permits a continuous branch of \(\theta\), but its two sides differ by \(\Gamma\). That cut records bookkeeping; it is not an impermeable wall. The velocity remains smooth across it.

Figure 13.1 makes the obstruction visible. Both paths connect the same endpoints, but reversing one to close the loop encloses the hole. A local potential can be assigned on a cut region without equating the two path integrals on the original annulus.

A two-dimensional stream function and the flux it measures L26 50:21

A stream function describes a different property of the field. In a planar incompressible flow define \(\psi(x,y,t)\), also in m²/s, by

\[ \mathrm d\psi=-V_y\,\mathrm dx+V_x\,\mathrm dy, \qquad V_x=\psi_y,\quad V_y=-\psi_x. \tag{13.11} \]

The minus sign is part of the convention. It makes continuity an identity: \(\partial_xV_x+\partial_yV_y=\psi_{yx}-\psi_{xy}=0\). Existence works in the reverse direction too. The compatibility condition for integrating \(-V_y\,\mathrm dx+V_x\,\mathrm dy\) is \(-\partial_yV_y=\partial_xV_x\), exactly planar continuity. A continuously differentiable divergence-free planar field therefore has a local stream function and a global one on a simply connected domain. Neither inviscidity nor irrotationality is needed for this construction.

For a curve directed from point \(A\) to point \(B\), choose its right-hand unit normal in the plane. Its normal line element is \(\mathbf n_R\,\mathrm ds=(\mathrm dy,-\mathrm dx)\). The signed volume flux through the curve per unit depth is

\[ q_{A\to B}=\int_A^B\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf n_R\,\mathrm ds =\int_A^B(V_x\,\mathrm dy-V_y\,\mathrm dx) =\psi(B)-\psi(A). \tag{13.12} \]

Thus differences of stream-function values have a quantitative meaning, including a sign. Along a streamline \((\mathrm dx,\mathrm dy)\) is parallel to \((V_x,V_y)\), so \(\mathrm d\psi=0\). Away from zero velocity, connected regular level curves of \(\psi\) are streamlines. In unsteady flow this statement applies at a fixed instant; such curves need not be particle paths.

For (13.1), \(\psi=axy\), up to a spatially uniform additive function. Its positive level curves are \(xy=\text{constant}\). At the right edge, directed upward so that its right normal points outward, \(\psi(L,H)-\psi(L,0)=aLH=1.00\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Direct integration gives \(\int_0^H aL\,\mathrm dy=1.00\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). At the top, outward flux is \(\int_0^L(-aH)\,\mathrm dx=-1.00\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Their sum is zero, while each wall carries zero normal flux pointwise.

The polar form follows from the same definition. Substituting \(\mathrm dx=\cos\theta\,\mathrm dr-r\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\), \(\mathrm dy=\sin\theta\,\mathrm dr+r\cos\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\), and the rotated velocity components into (13.11) gives \(\mathrm d\psi=-V_\theta\,\mathrm dr+rV_r\,\mathrm d\theta\). Thus \(V_r=\psi_\theta/r\) and \(V_\theta=-\psi_r\). These formulas concern planar polar coordinates, not an axisymmetric three-dimensional stream function.

A global stream function has its own topology condition: the flux integral in (13.12) must vanish around every closed curve. For the annular tangential field, \(\psi=-c\ln(r/R_1)\) is single valued, although \(\phi\) is not. By contrast, the planar radial field \(V_r=q_s/(2\pi r)\), \(V_\theta=0\), has flux \(q_s\) outward across every circle. Here \(q_s\), in m²/s, is positive source strength per unit depth; negative \(q_s\) is a sink. Integrating (13.11) requires \(\psi=q_s\theta/(2\pi)\) locally, so \(\psi\) is multivalued if \(q_s\ne0\). The origin is again excluded. This is a test of global existence, not an assumption that fluid is created at an ordinary fluid point.

In general three-dimensional flow one scalar \(\psi\) with (13.11) is insufficient. A divergence-free field can locally be represented by a vector potential \(\mathbf A\) with \(\mathbf V=\nabla\times\mathbf A\), which has additional gauge freedom. Special symmetric three-dimensional flows may admit their own scalar stream functions with different formulas. Neither statement extends (13.11) unchanged to arbitrary three-dimensional motion.

Continuity and irrotationality give two Laplace equations L27 2:45

Using a potential automatically enforces irrotationality. Substitution into incompressible continuity supplies the remaining kinematic equation:

\[ 0=\nabla\cdot\mathbf V=\nabla\cdot\nabla\phi =\nabla^2\phi. \tag{13.13} \]

The operator \(\nabla^2\), the Laplacian, is \(\partial_{xx}+\partial_{yy}+\partial_{zz}\) in Cartesian coordinates. A twice differentiable scalar satisfying this zero-Laplacian equation is called harmonic. In two dimensions the \(z\) term is absent. Incompressibility is necessary for this deduction: potential representation alone does not make \(\phi\) harmonic. With variable density the mass equation is instead \(\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla\phi)=0\).

Using a planar stream function automatically enforces continuity. Vorticity supplies its equation:

\[ \omega_z=\partial_x(-\psi_x)-\partial_y(\psi_y) =-\nabla^2\psi. \qquad \omega_z=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \nabla^2\psi=0. \tag{13.14} \]

The two routes exchange the roles of the constraints. For rotational incompressible flow, (13.14) is a Poisson equation \(\nabla^2\psi=-\omega_z\), not a Laplace equation. In the Couette example, \(\psi=Uy/2+Uy^2/(4h)\) gives \(\nabla^2\psi=U/(2h)\), recovering \(\omega_z=-U/(2h)\). A stream function can exist while failing to be harmonic.

The running potentials pass both tests: \(\phi_{xx}=a\), \(\phi_{yy}=-a\), and \(\psi_{xx}=\psi_{yy}=0\). These cancellations hold everywhere in the rectangle. The annular field also has harmonic local potentials; the word “local” does not disappear when Laplace's equation is written. Solving a differential equation cannot by itself remove the circulation around an excluded core.

What superposition preserves L27 13:31

Laplace's equation is linear. If harmonic potentials \(\phi_1,\phi_2\) are defined on the same regular domain and \(\alpha,\beta\) are spatially constant dimensionless numbers, then

\[ \nabla^2(\alpha\phi_1+\beta\phi_2) =\alpha\nabla^2\phi_1+\beta\nabla^2\phi_2=0. \tag{13.15} \]

The corresponding velocity is \(\alpha\mathbf V_1+\beta\mathbf V_2\). A constituent singularity must remain outside the common regular domain. The equation alone does not guarantee that a chosen combination satisfies a particular boundary condition.

For example, \(\phi_1=a(x^2-y^2)/2\) and \(\phi_2=U_0x\), with constant speed \(U_0\), are both harmonic. Their sum gives \(\mathbf V=(ax+U_0,-ay)\). At \(y=0\), the normal velocity is still zero, but at the fixed left wall \(x=0\), it is \(-U_0\) with outward normal \(-\mathbf i\). Taking \(U_0=0.20\ \mathrm{m/s}\) produces 0.20 m²/s of inward wall flux through its 1 m length. The field is divergence free and curl free but fails the original wall condition. Harmonicity is only one part of a boundary-value problem.

If both constituent fields satisfy a homogeneous condition \(\partial_n\phi=0\) on the same wall, their combination also does. If they instead share identical nonzero boundary data, an arbitrary sum rescales those data; an affine combination with \(\alpha+\beta=1\) preserves them. Far-field velocities combine too. These facts follow from applying the same linear boundary operator to (13.15), rather than assuming that every sum solves every geometry.

Pressure is a separate issue. The kinetic energy per unit mass of the sum is \(|\mathbf V_1+\mathbf V_2|^2/2=|\mathbf V_1|^2/2+|\mathbf V_2|^2/2+\mathbf V_1\cdot\mathbf V_2\). The cross term generally survives. Once pressure is recovered from momentum balance, adding constituent pressure fields misses that term. The linearity belongs to the kinematic potential equation with compatible boundary data.

Impermeable walls, open boundaries, and uniqueness L27 17:15

Let \(\mathbf n\) be the outward unit normal of the fluid, and \(\mathbf V_s\) the solid-surface velocity. The impermeability condition is \((\mathbf V-\mathbf V_s)\cdot\mathbf n=0\): fluid cannot cross the moving solid. In potential form it becomes

\[ \partial_n\phi\equiv\nabla\phi\cdot\mathbf n=\mathbf V_s\cdot\mathbf n. \quad\text{For a fixed wall:}\quad \partial_n\phi=0. \tag{13.16} \]

This condition fixes the normal derivative, not the value of \(\phi\); it is called a Neumann condition. Setting \(\phi\) constant on a wall would instead constrain its tangential derivative, hence tangential velocity. The two conditions answer different physical questions. No slip would additionally set tangential fluid velocity equal to the wall's. A general Euler or potential-flow solution cannot also enforce arbitrary no-slip data. It is permitted to slip along an impermeable wall.

For a fixed planar wall, parameterize its curve by arclength \(s\) with unit tangent \(\mathbf t=(\mathrm dx/\mathrm ds,\mathrm dy/\mathrm ds)\), oriented so that the fluid's outward normal is \(\mathbf n=(t_y,-t_x)\). Equation (13.11) gives \(\mathrm d\psi/\mathrm ds=-V_yt_x+V_xt_y=\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf n\). Thus impermeability makes \(\psi\) constant on each connected wall component. The constants on different disconnected components need not be equal; their differences can encode flux between them. For a moving wall the right-hand side need not vanish in the laboratory frame.

In the running rectangle, \(x=0\) and \(y=0\) both have \(\psi=0\) and \(\partial_n\phi=0\). On the bottom \(\mathbf V=(ax,0)\); it slips at up to 1 m/s. On the left \(\mathbf V=(0,-ay)\); it slips at up to 0.50 m/s. Requiring no slip there would reject this otherwise valid inviscid solution. A viscous realization needs a treatment of the wall region before its stress or dissipation can be predicted.

The open edges supply the through-flow. In Neumann form the complete boundary data are zero on both walls, \(\partial_n\phi=-aH\) on the top, and \(\partial_n\phi=aL\) on the right. Integrating Laplace's equation over the rectangle and using the divergence theorem gives the compatibility condition

\[ \oint_{\partial D}\partial_n\phi\,\mathrm ds=0. \tag{13.17} \]

The top contributes \(-aHL\) and the right \(+aLH\). Imposing inflow at the top while closing the right edge would violate (13.17); no incompressible potential can satisfy those data. The condition generalizes to a closed three-dimensional boundary with area element \(\mathrm dA\). It expresses mass conservation, not a numerical solver preference.

Uniqueness clarifies why a bounded tank cannot support an arbitrary single-valued potential flow. Suppose \(\phi_1,\phi_2\) solve Laplace's equation in a bounded connected region with the same Neumann data and enough boundary regularity for integration by parts. Their difference \(\chi=\phi_1-\phi_2\) is harmonic with zero normal derivative. Then

\[ \int_D|\nabla\chi|^2\,\mathrm dA =\oint_{\partial D}\chi\partial_n\chi\,\mathrm ds -\int_D\chi\nabla^2\chi\,\mathrm dA=0. \tag{13.18} \]

The integrand is nonnegative, so \(\nabla\chi=0\); connectedness makes \(\chi\) spatially constant. This proves uniqueness up to a gauge for single-valued potentials. In particular a bounded region with an entirely fixed impermeable boundary has only zero velocity within that class. The annular circulating solution does not contradict the proof, because it has no single-valued \(\phi\) to substitute into (13.18). Circulations around the holes distinguish additional curl-free fields. The bounded-domain discussion of Iftimie and coauthors, p. 32, retains these circulation data.

For an exterior-flow problem the distant boundary is replaced by a far-field condition. A prescribed uniform velocity \(U_\infty\mathbf i\) requires \(\nabla\phi\to(U_\infty,0)\) as distance from the body tends to infinity in the planar problem. The corresponding uniform potentials are \(\phi=U_\infty x+\text{constant}\), \(\psi=U_\infty y+\text{constant}\). They grow with position; one should not impose \(\phi\to0\) at infinity for a nonzero uniform stream. A disturbance potential may obey a decay condition, but its definition must first subtract the uniform contribution.

Orthogonal level curves and a consistent complex convention L27 24:41

An equipotential is a level curve of \(\phi\). At a regular point its tangent is perpendicular to \(\nabla\phi=\mathbf V\). A streamline has tangent parallel to \(\mathbf V\), so the curves meet at right angles wherever \(\mathbf V\ne0\). This conclusion does not require slopes to be finite. In components, (13.9) and (13.11) give

\[ \nabla\phi=(V_x,V_y),\qquad \nabla\psi=(-V_y,V_x),\qquad \nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi=0. \tag{13.19} \]

When both component ratios are defined, the equipotential slope is \(-V_x/V_y\), and the streamline slope is \(V_y/V_x\); their product is −1. At a stagnation point, where velocity is zero, both gradients vanish and there need not be a unique level-curve tangent. Orthogonality is a statement about regular level curves. It must not be imposed on the zero-velocity corner of the running case as if its tangent were unique.

For \(\phi=a(x^2-y^2)/2\) and \(\psi=axy\), equipotentials satisfy \(x^2-y^2=\text{constant}\), while streamlines satisfy \(xy=\text{constant}\). At \((1,0.50)\ \mathrm m\) the velocity is \((0.50,-0.25)\ \mathrm{m/s}\); the streamline slope is −0.50 and the equipotential slope is 2. Their product is −1. Figure 13.2 computes both families from these equations rather than drawing guessed orthogonal curves.

There is a useful optional notation for planar flow. Define a dimensionful complex coordinate \(\zeta=x+iy\), with \(i^2=-1\), and the complex potential \(W(\zeta)=\phi+i\psi\). The component definitions give \(\phi_x=\psi_y\) and \(\phi_y=-\psi_x\), the Cauchy–Riemann relations. For continuously differentiable fields these make \(W\) locally complex differentiable. Differentiating along \(x\) fixes the convention:

\[ \frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm d\zeta}=\phi_x+i\psi_x=V_x-iV_y. \tag{13.20} \]

The running case has \(W=a\zeta^2/2\), and its derivative \(a\zeta=ax+iay\) equals \(V_x-iV_y\), confirming the sign. This is a compact representation of the definitions, not an additional physical law. A global single-valued \(W\) requires both scalar functions to be single valued on the domain; the annular vortex only has local branches. Using \(z\) for the complex coordinate would collide with physical elevation, so \(\zeta\) is used here.

Recovering pressure and the extent of Bernoulli's constant L27 29:51

Laplace's equation contains no pressure. Pressure is recovered from Euler momentum balance after velocity is found. Assume constant density, inviscidity, smooth fields, and the conservative force \(-\nabla\Phi_b\). The component identity

\[ (\mathbf V\cdot\nabla)\mathbf V =\nabla\!\left(\frac{|\mathbf V|^2}{2}\right)-\mathbf V\times\boldsymbol\omega \tag{13.21} \]

follows by expanding the derivatives. In two dimensions its \(x\) component is \(V_x\partial_xV_x+V_y\partial_yV_x\): the \(V_y\partial_xV_y\) in the energy gradient cancels the corresponding part of \(V_y\omega_z\). This cancellation is the hinge of the irrotational simplification. When \(\boldsymbol\omega=0\), the convective acceleration is a gradient.

On a region with a smooth single-valued velocity potential, substitute \(\partial_t\mathbf V=\nabla\partial_t\phi\) into Euler's equation. It becomes

\[ \nabla\!\left(\partial_t\phi+\frac{|\mathbf V|^2}{2} +\frac p\rho+\Phi_b\right)=0. \quad \partial_t\phi+\frac{|\mathbf V|^2}{2}+\frac p\rho+\Phi_b=B(t). \tag{13.22} \]

Here \(B(t)\), in m²/s², is spatially constant on each connected region. Zero spatial gradient does not require it to be constant in time. Replacing \(\phi\) by \(\phi+f_0(t)\) changes \(B\) to \(B+f_0'(t)\), so the recovered pressure is unchanged. For any chosen gauge of \(\phi\), a single specified pressure value at each instant determines \(B(t)\). Local branches can also be used, provided their time-dependent branch differences and the physical single-valued pressure are treated consistently.

The steady statement is stronger than a claim about a global velocity potential. Set \(\partial_t\mathbf V=0\) directly in Euler's equation, and define the globally physical scalar \(B_s=|\mathbf V|^2/2+p/\rho+\Phi_b\). Equation (13.21) gives

\[ \nabla B_s=\mathbf V\times\boldsymbol\omega. \quad \boldsymbol\omega=0\ \Longrightarrow\ B_s=\text{constant on connected }D. \tag{13.23} \]

This deduction never uses a globally single-valued \(\phi\). In a connected steady irrotational annulus, \(B_s\) is constant throughout even though \(\phi=c\theta\) is multivalued. If the steady flow is rotational, dotting (13.23) with \(\mathbf V\) gives \(\mathbf V\cdot\nabla B_s=0\). Then Bernoulli's quantity is constant along each streamline, but may differ between streamlines. Disconnected fluid regions can have different constants in either case.

For the horizontal running flow, choose \(p_A=100{,}000\ \mathrm{Pa}\) at \(A=(1,1)\ \mathrm m\). At \(B=(2,1)\ \mathrm m\), the two speed squares are \(a^2(1^2+1^2)=0.50\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\) and \(a^2(2^2+1^2)=1.25\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). At equal elevation, (13.23) gives \(p_B-p_A=-\rho(1.25-0.50)/2=-375\ \mathrm{Pa}\). Thus \(p_B=99{,}625\ \mathrm{Pa}\). These points lie on different streamlines because \(\psi_A=0.50\) and \(\psi_B=1.00\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). The comparison is licensed by irrotationality, not merely by steady flow.

The full pressure field at any elevation is \(p=\rho[B_s-a^2(x^2+y^2)/2-gz]\). Its horizontal gradient is \((-\rho a^2x,-\rho a^2y)\). The convective acceleration computed directly from (13.1) is \((a^2x,a^2y,0)\), and the vertical pressure gradient is \(-\rho g\), so all three Euler components hold. This supplies an independent momentum check beyond continuity and curl. For the annulus with \(c=1\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), pressure rises by \(\rho c^2(1/R_1^2-1/R_2^2)/2=375\ \mathrm{Pa}\) from \(R_1=1\) to \(R_2=2\ \mathrm m\), at equal elevation and \(\rho=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\). Its radial derivative \(\rho c^2/r^3\) supplies the inward centripetal acceleration \(-c^2/r^3\). The multivalued potential causes no multivalued pressure.

Executable checks of local equations, flux, and a hole

The program implements the rectangular and annular fields already defined. All inputs are fixed SI values in the listing; it reads no files and writes only standard output. It requires only Python's standard library. Central differences check divergence and curl independently of analytic derivative formulas. Edge quadrature checks the rectangular inflow and outflow. The annular samples and their finite-difference stencils stay strictly between radii 1 and 2 m; the origin is excluded explicitly by the field evaluator. A midpoint line integral checks circulation around a circle, which a local curl test cannot determine.

The assertions use absolute tolerances in the stated units: 10⁻⁸ s⁻¹ for local derivative residuals, 10⁻¹² m/s for wall normal velocity, and 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s for rectangular flux balance and circular circulation. A zero or negative differentiation step, a stencil leaving the regular annulus, or a point at its excluded core raises ValueError. The tested parameters are modest; the listing is a numerical diagnostic, not an input-general flow solver. The printed derivative bounds report the enforced tolerances rather than insignificant last-digit roundoff.

import math

A, L, H = 0.5, 2.0, 1.0  # s^-1, m, m
C = 1.0  # free-vortex coefficient, m^2/s


def rectangle(x, y):
    return A * x, -A * y


def vortex(x, y):
    r2 = x * x + y * y
    if not 1.0 < r2 < 4.0:
        raise ValueError("point outside regular annulus")
    return -C * y / r2, C * x / r2


def derivatives(field, x, y, step=1e-4):
    if not math.isfinite(step) or step <= 0:
        raise ValueError("step must be finite and positive")
    xp, xm = field(x + step, y), field(x - step, y)
    yp, ym = field(x, y + step), field(x, y - step)
    divergence = (xp[0] - xm[0] + yp[1] - ym[1]) / (2 * step)
    curl = (xp[1] - xm[1] - yp[0] + ym[0]) / (2 * step)
    return divergence, curl


def local_check(field, points):
    residuals = [derivatives(field, x, y) for x, y in points]
    div_max = max(abs(row[0]) for row in residuals)
    curl_max = max(abs(row[1]) for row in residuals)
    assert div_max < 1e-8, "mass balance failed"
    assert curl_max < 1e-8, "irrotationality failed"
    return div_max, curl_max


def wall_check(field):
    residual = max(
        max(abs(field(0, j / 100)[0]) for j in range(101)),
        max(abs(field(L * j / 100, 0)[1]) for j in range(101)),
    )
    assert residual < 1e-12, "fixed-wall condition failed"
    return residual


def midpoint(function, lower, upper, count=4000):
    width = (upper - lower) / count
    return width * math.fsum(
        function(lower + (j + 0.5) * width) for j in range(count)
    )


points = [(0.2 + 0.2 * i, 0.1 + 0.1 * j)
          for i in range(8) for j in range(8)]
local_check(rectangle, points)
wall_check(rectangle)
outflow = midpoint(lambda y: rectangle(L, y)[0], 0, H)
top_flux = midpoint(lambda x: rectangle(x, H)[1], 0, L)
assert abs(outflow + top_flux) < 1e-10
print("rectangle: div and curl < 1e-8 s^-1; wall < 1e-12 m/s")
print(f"outflow={outflow:.6f}; top outward flux={top_flux:.6f} m^2/s")

annular_points = [(r * math.cos(t), r * math.sin(t))
                  for r in (1.2, 1.5, 1.8)
                  for t in (0.2, 0.7, 1.4, 2.3, 3.8, 5.1)]
local_check(vortex, annular_points)
radius = 1.5


def tangent_integrand(theta):
    x, y = radius * math.cos(theta), radius * math.sin(theta)
    vx, vy = vortex(x, y)
    return vx * (-y) + vy * x


circulation = midpoint(tangent_integrand, 0, 2 * math.pi)
assert abs(circulation - 2 * math.pi * C) < 1e-10
print("annulus: div and curl < 1e-8 s^-1; stencils stay inside")
print(f"counterclockwise circulation={circulation:.10f} m^2/s")

rho = 1000.0
speed_a2 = sum(v * v for v in rectangle(1, 1))
speed_b2 = sum(v * v for v in rectangle(2, 1))
pressure_difference = -rho * (speed_b2 - speed_a2) / 2
assert abs(pressure_difference + 375) < 1e-12
print(f"p_B-p_A={pressure_difference:.1f} Pa")

try:
    local_check(lambda x, y: (A * x, A * y), points)
except AssertionError as error:
    print("sign mutation caught:", error)
else:
    raise AssertionError("sign mutation was not detected")


def shifted(x, y):
    return A * x + 0.2, -A * y


local_check(shifted, points)
try:
    wall_check(shifted)
except AssertionError as error:
    print("uniform addition caught:", error)
else:
    raise AssertionError("wall mutation was not detected")

for function in (lambda: vortex(0, 0),
                 lambda: derivatives(rectangle, 1, 0.5, 0),
                 lambda: derivatives(vortex, 1.00001, 0)):
    try:
        function()
    except ValueError:
        pass
    else:
        raise AssertionError("invalid input was not rejected")
print("invalid core, zero step, and crossing stencil: rejected")

The first block verifies local kinematics and the boundary data. The nonzero circular integral establishes the global obstruction despite the small local curl residual. The sign-error test replaces Vy=-a*y with Vy=+a*y; its nonzero divergence fails the mass-balance assertion. The wall-error test adds a uniform horizontal velocity, which preserves divergence and curl but fails the fixed left-wall condition. These failures are executed in the listing. Finite samples check the implementation and catch these errors; the analytic derivations establish the continuum statements.

What a potential-flow solution establishes

The rectangular construction is an exact smooth solution of incompressible Euler flow with its stated open-edge data, wall impermeability, and recovered pressure. It carries 1.00 m²/s per unit depth. Its potential and stream function each satisfy a Laplace equation, and their regular level curves are orthogonal. The annular construction establishes a different fact: local incompressibility and irrotationality, even with impermeable boundaries, do not determine the circulation around a hole. That circulation can obstruct a global potential while leaving a global steady Bernoulli scalar perfectly well defined.

The model supplies neither viscous wall stress nor a wall-layer velocity profile. It does not predict dissipation, separation, or whether a given real inflow will remain close to the assumed steady planar field. Laplace's equation is a kinematic reduction under incompressibility and irrotationality, not a replacement for all force and initial data. Inviscid rotational flows require more information. Compressible potential flows need a density relation and a different mass equation. Singular ideal fields are valid only on their declared regular domains.

A useful order of checks follows from these limits: identify the regular domain and holes; specify initial circulation information; test divergence and curl; apply the actual normal boundary conditions; establish local or global potential representation; then recover pressure under its momentum hypotheses. A formula can pass one check and fail the next. The uniform addition in the code is a concrete example.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive a local rotation test). Consider the steady planar linear field \(V_x=\alpha x+\beta y\), \(V_y=\gamma x+\delta y\), where the four coefficients have units s⁻¹. Find the necessary and sufficient coefficient conditions for incompressibility and irrotationality. When both hold, construct \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) and verify their Laplacians. Does inviscidity follow from either condition?

Solution

The divergence is \(\alpha+\delta\), so incompressibility requires \(\delta=-\alpha\). The vorticity is \(\gamma-\beta\), so irrotationality requires \(\gamma=\beta\). These are independent conditions. Under both, integration gives \(\phi=\alpha(x^2-y^2)/2+\beta xy\). Its gradient is \((\alpha x+\beta y,\beta x-\alpha y)\), as required. Integrating \(\psi_y=\alpha x+\beta y\) gives \(\psi=\alpha xy+\beta y^2/2+F(x)\); \(-\psi_x=\beta x-\alpha y\) requires \(F=-\beta x^2/2+\text{constant}\). Hence \(\psi=\alpha xy+\beta(y^2-x^2)/2\). The two same-coordinate second derivatives cancel for each function. Both scalars have units m²/s. Inviscid behavior does not follow: the conditions constrain velocity, whereas inviscidity concerns viscous stress in the force model. In fact a nonzero symmetric velocity gradient gives viscous deformation stress for a Newtonian fluid with \(\mu\ne0\).

Exercise 2 (counterexample with a quantified obstruction). In the annulus \(1\lt r\lt2\ \mathrm m\), prescribe \(V_\theta=1/r\) in SI units and \(V_r=0\). Compute the velocity integrals from \((1.5,0)\) to \((-1.5,0)\) along the upper semicircle traversed counterclockwise and along the lower semicircle traversed clockwise. Explain the role of Stokes' theorem, find a stream function, and decide whether a global steady Bernoulli constant is permitted.

Solution

Along a circle, \(\mathbf V\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf s=(1/r)r\,\mathrm d\theta=\mathrm d\theta\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). The upper path increases \(\theta\) by \(\pi\), giving \(+\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). The lower clockwise path decreases \(\theta\) by \(\pi\), giving \(-\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Their difference is \(2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), so a single-valued potential difference cannot represent both. Vorticity vanishes in the annulus, but the closed contour surrounds the excluded disk and has no spanning disk in the fluid. Stokes' theorem on an annular surface includes its inner boundary and is satisfied. The function \(\psi=-\ln(r/R_1)\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), with \(R_1=1\ \mathrm m\), is single valued and gives the prescribed field through the polar versions of (13.11). Under steady constant-density Euler flow with conservative body force, (13.23) gives one Bernoulli constant on this connected annulus. Global single-valuedness of \(\phi\) is unnecessary for that conclusion.

Exercise 3 (diagnose an incompatible boundary design). A designer closes the right edge of the running rectangle while keeping the top inflow at 0.50 m/s. All walls are fixed and impermeable. Can a steady incompressible potential exist? If the right edge instead stays open but is shortened to 0.50 m while the top inflow is unchanged, what mean outward speed must it carry? Explain what this calculation can and cannot determine about its profile.

Solution

The imposed top inflow is \(0.50\ \mathrm{m/s}\times2\ \mathrm m=1.00\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). With every other edge closed, total outward flux is −1.00 m²/s, violating (13.17). No steady incompressible field, potential or otherwise, can satisfy those data in a fixed region. Unsteady incompressible flow also cannot accumulate volume in the same completely fixed region with that flux imbalance. A free surface, a moving boundary, or compressibility would change the stated problem.

For a 0.50 m opening, conservation requires mean outward speed \(1.00/0.50=2.00\ \mathrm{m/s}\). This is an integral constraint. It does not specify the pointwise velocity distribution, which depends on the opening geometry and other boundary data; the original linear field no longer solves the altered wall problem. Treating the computed mean as the complete new potential-flow solution would omit that boundary-value calculation.

Exercise 4 (numerical regime comparison). The listing uses a midpoint line integral around a circle. Replace the tangential field by rigid rotation \(V_x=-\Omega_0y\), \(V_y=\Omega_0x\), with \(\Omega_0=1\ \mathrm s^{-1}\), and compare circulation at radii 1.2 and 1.8 m with the annular free-vortex values. Explain which ratio distinguishes the fields even though their streamlines have the same shape. Would zero circulation around one large curve exclude all rotational flow?

Solution

At each sampled angle the line-integral density for rigid rotation is \(\Omega_0r^2\), so the midpoint sum gives \(2\pi\Omega_0r^2\): 9.047787 and 20.357520 m²/s at 1.2 and 1.8 m. Their ratio is \((1.8/1.2)^2=2.25\). For \(c=1\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), the free-vortex value is \(2\pi=6.283185\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) at both radii, a ratio of 1. The distinct radius dependence is circulation's measurement of vorticity enclosed by admissible loops: rigid rotation adds uniform vorticity \(2\Omega_0\) as disk area grows, while the annular field has none between the loops. Equal streamline shapes do not fix those integrals.

A zero integral around one curve is insufficient. For example a smooth planar shear \(V_x=y^2\) with its coefficient understood as \(1\ \mathrm{m^{-1}s^{-1}}\), \(V_y=0\), has \(\omega_z=-2y\) in corresponding units. On a rectangle symmetric about \(y=0\), positive and negative vorticity cancel in the area integral, although almost every point is rotational. Local loops, or the pointwise curl, reveal it.

Exercise 5 (pressure recovery and a gauge). Replace \(a\) in the running field by \(a(t)=a_0+bt\), where \(a_0=0.50\ \mathrm s^{-1}\) and \(b=0.10\ \mathrm s^{-2}\). Find an unsteady potential and the pressure difference \(p_B-p_A\) at \(t=0\) between \(A=(1,1)\ \mathrm m\) and \(B=(2,1)\ \mathrm m\), at equal elevation and \(\rho=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\). What would the steady formula miss? What changes if \(10t^2\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) is added to the potential?

Solution

The potential is \(\phi=a(t)(x^2-y^2)/2+f_0(t)\), and its Laplacian remains zero at each instant. At \(t=0\), the kinetic contribution to \((p_B-p_A)/\rho\) is −0.375 m²/s², as in the steady case. The potential time-derivative difference is \(b[(4-1)-(1-1)]/2=0.15\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). Equation (13.22) therefore gives \(p_B-p_A=-1000(0.375+0.15)=-525\ \mathrm{Pa}\). The steady formula misses 150 Pa of pressure difference needed for the local acceleration \(\partial_t\mathbf V=(bx,-by,0)\).

Write the added term as \(kt^2\), with \(k=10\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\). Its time derivative is \(2kt\), in m²/s². It changes no velocity and adds the same derivative to \(\partial_t\phi\) everywhere. The function \(B(t)\) shifts by \(2kt\), so neither pressure nor its difference changes. Holding \(B(t)\) fixed while changing the gauge would introduce an artificial uniform pressure change.

Exercise 6 (judging superposition and regularity). Consider adding to the running potential the radial potential \(\phi_s=q_s\ln(r/\ell_0)/(2\pi)\), with \(q_s=0.20\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), reference length \(\ell_0=1\ \mathrm m\), and \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). Is the sum harmonic at the origin, in the open rectangle, and across a small circle enclosing the origin? Does it preserve the two wall conditions away from the corner? Explain how an integral mass balance detects information a pointwise Laplacian misses.

Solution

For \(r\gt0\), \(\partial_r\phi_s=q_s/(2\pi r)\) and the planar radial Laplacian \(r^{-1}\partial_r(r\partial_r\phi_s)\) is zero. The sum is harmonic in the open rectangle because that rectangle excludes the origin. It is not a regular harmonic field at the origin: the logarithm and its gradient are singular. A full disk enclosing the origin contains that singularity, so one cannot apply the regular divergence theorem over it while declaring the divergence zero everywhere.

On \(y=0,x\gt0\), the radial addition has zero \(y\) velocity; on \(x=0,y\gt0\), it has zero \(x\) velocity. It therefore preserves impermeability away from the singular corner. It changes the open-edge fluxes, however, and injects \(q_s/4=0.050\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) into this first-quadrant region through a quarter-circle surrounding the excluded corner. To see the normalization, outward flux through a full circle is \(\int_0^{2\pi}[q_s/(2\pi r)]r\,\mathrm d\theta=q_s\). A quarter-circle has one quarter of that value. In the domain with a small quarter-disk removed, the normal on that inner arc points toward the origin, so its outward flux from the fluid domain is \(-q_s/4\), balancing the extra outward flux on the distant open edges. The addition is not a solution of the original complete boundary data. It introduces a singular inflow at the corner even though each regular interior point still has zero divergence and both wall conditions hold away from the corner.
